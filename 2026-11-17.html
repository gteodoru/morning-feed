Open source on the defensive: Amodei's warning, Gurley's charge, and Hugging Face's answer

X — @vikktorrrre · Hugging Face · Nov 16

A clip of Dario Amodei warning that open-weight models could reach "a very dangerous place" set off the week's biggest argument — and the numbers say open models are winning anyway.

A clip of Dario Amodei, posted by @vikktorrrre and reposted by Yann LeCun, set it off. Amodei's argument, in plain terms: a model behind an API comes with three controls you lose once the weights are public — you can watch how it is used, cut off a user, and change its behaviour after release. His challenge to open-source advocates: say what replaces those controls. The poster's own gloss — that Amodei "clearly doesn't believe intelligence should be in the hand of the masses" and that Sam Altman agreed — is the poster's claim, not sourced.

The counter-charge came fast. Bill Gurley quote-posted the clip: "The only risk to their strategy is open models. So beg government to stop them. High IRR activity." Gurley had introduced "handoverism" earlier the same day — the idea that humanity will hand control of the world to AI — so his case against Anthropic now has two parts: the policy motive and the worldview. SemiAnalysis, meanwhile, argued Nvidia's turmoil makes open source "look a lot weaker" — "there's more than one and a half gigawatts of chips in China that are not supposed to be there according to US law" — and that this "bodes really well for Anthropic's IPO next week." Nat Lambert answered the financing question: if not Nvidia, then "Meta / Msft / Amazon / Google / govt will have to step up, as they should." Ethan Mollick added a cold fact: "we have not seen a Fable-class open model more than 8 months after Fable/Mythos was first made public" — Alibaba's Qwen4 Max sits at 43 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, 3 points behind Kimi K3.1 Pro at 46.

The open-source side's answer arrived the same afternoon, in numbers rather than rhetoric. Hugging Face moved its Inference Providers onto OpenRouter's router: any chat model on the Hub that some provider serves can now be called with a Hugging Face token, through OpenAI- or Anthropic-style APIs, with OpenRouter picking the provider per request on live price, speed and reliability. Open-weight models went from 26.8% of OpenRouter tokens in October 2025 to 71.7% at the end of September 2026, peaking at 77.5% in July; weekly open-weight volume grew 60x against ~10x for closed models, at about a quarter of the price ($0.28/$1.62 per million input/output tokens vs $1.27/$6.36). The honest caveat is in Hugging Face's own post: the best open model, Kimi K3.1 Pro, scores 46 against 65 for Claude Fable 5.2 — at about a seventh of the cost per task ($3.68 vs $26.54). One breaking change: for language models you can no longer bring your own provider API key; everything is billed through Hugging Face, with no markup.

OpenAI's embedded assessments: a useful pilot, not yet employee-like access

X — @gdb · @NeelNanda5 · Nov 16

OpenAI is letting outside researchers inside the company to test alignment. Safety researchers say the access falls short of what was promised.

OpenAI, amplified by Greg Brockman, said it is "working with Apollo Research, METR, Transluce, and Goodfire on embedded assessments: giving outside experts deep access to our internal models and systems to investigate alignment and test our safeguards." The concept is the bank-examiner model: instead of testing a finished model from outside, outside researchers sit inside the company, see training runs, internal tools and incidents, and talk to staff — to catch problems a company might not volunteer, or might not even know it has.

The safety researchers who would actually do this work say the pilot is good and the name is wrong. Neel Nanda: "It's great that OpenAI is doing these assessments but they fall short of Sam's commitment to employee-like access" — no Slack, only OpenAI-selected staff for interviews, formal staff conversations must be logged, and METR and Apollo have so far only agreed access to training-run information, not internal incidents. Peter Wildeford: "OpenAI controls what evaluators see, who they talk to, and what they say, and can ignore it all." Nathan Calvin: judged as a pilot towards embedded evaluations, as Apollo describes it, the announcement is good; described as "embedded assessments" themselves, "they are woefully inadequate." His test case: would these assessors have caught an incident like the Artifactory outage tied to an "unauthorized exploit gym message board"? "Based on what I have seen here, I think the answer is no."

The takeaway for anyone building an agent platform: everyone in this thread agrees the pilot is good, and the fight is over the name — independent oversight, or access controlled by the company being overseen. Calvin's incident test is a good question to ask of any lab's version, including your own internal evals: would your reviewers have caught the incident you didn't know you had?

Washington closes the cloud loophole on China's AI chips

WSJ — Amira McKee · Bloomberg · Nov 16

The Commerce Department's new rule stops Chinese firms renting advanced US chips from overseas data centers — the first policy answer to the Nvidia scandal.

The Commerce Department on Monday issued an interim final rule, "Addressing Remote Access to Advanced Semiconductors," closing the route Chinese companies have used most since direct exports were banned: renting computing power from data centers in other countries. Overseas data centers that supply more than 12 million in total processing performance — roughly the maximum output of about 750 Nvidia H100 or H200 chips — to companies headquartered in China or other restricted destinations will need US export licenses within 90 days to keep serving them. Exporters will also need licenses to ship advanced AI chips to Singapore, Thailand, Malaysia, Indonesia and Brazil, named as presenting a higher-than-normal risk of diversion. The rule formally rescinds the Biden-era AI Diffusion Rule of January 2025, which was never enforced.

The rule is the Trump administration's first policy response to the scandal that began with former Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang's fall. Huang resigned November 12 after admitting an affair with Alice Huang, a former executive at Singapore-based cloud company Megaspeed — which rents computing power at its data centers and has come under scrutiny for possible illegal diversion of chips to China. The Journal reports Alice Huang's husband built a data-center empire backed by the Chinese state. Commerce said the rule was written at the direction of the Treasury Department and the National Security Council, and announced it as President Trump flew to Asia to meet Xi Jinping in Shenzhen on Thursday.

The market reaction was immediate: shares of data-center and chip supply-chain companies in Southeast Asia tumbled Tuesday. Analysts call it "export controls 2.0" — Chinese AI developers can no longer bypass physical hardware embargoes by renting advanced US compute, and the regulatory burden shifts from hardware manufacturers to cloud providers, who now face massive "Know Your Customer's Workload" requirements.

The political fallout runs in every direction. Trump said Huang "learned a big lesson" and that Nvidia should consider reinstating him; he also contradicted his own intelligence officials on whether Alice Huang is a spy. Nvidia's board is not considering reinstating Huang, the Journal reports, despite an open letter signed by Uber co-founder Travis Kalanick, Shopify CEO Tobias Lutke, Epic Games CEO Tim Sweeney and investor Gavin Baker — and despite Kalanick crowdsourcing ways to "persuade" the board. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, a key Huang ally, is now a target of finger-pointing over efforts to stall export restrictions. And Sam Altman, asked at Semafor's event whether Huang should return, demurred — "I don't think I know enough to answer that question" — compared it to his own 2023 ouster ("heroes have flaws") and said of the industry: "the revenues and demand are growing so strongly that I think the industry will be fine."

The deeper context is Huang's own long-standing position. In a May 2023 interview with Bloomberg's Ian King, he spent eight minutes on opening up the Chinese market: "We care about national security, obviously we care about all those things, but the economy is a very important pillar of national security. To be deprived of one-third of the technology industry's market has got to be catastrophic." Three things worth your time today, plus a grant deadline to put on the calendar: how to sandbox a coding agent so it can't leak your keys, a new workspace for thinking with AI, and the arithmetic bug that bites exactly at convergence.

External sandboxes for coding agents

Prime Radiant — Simon Willison · Nov 16

Your coding agent runs on your Mac, but every command it executes goes to a Linux sandbox that sees only what you allow — and never holds your API keys.

Coding agents run shell commands on your laptop, and their built-in permission prompts either nag you constantly or get switched off. Simon Willison, writing for Prime Radiant, describes the pattern that fixes it: the agent process stays on your Mac, but the commands it executes are sent over a localhost HTTP/WebSocket protocol to a Linux sandbox. The sandbox sees only the folders you mount, read-only or read-write. No network by default — add --proxy for an allow-list of hosts. And the credential piece, the one Willison's readers say they'd turn on first: the proxy injects API keys into outgoing requests for specific hosts, so the secret never exists inside the sandbox or the agent's context. A prompt-injected agent has nothing to leak.

Three interchangeable backends share the same protocol: external-sandbox-v86 (Node, Alpine Linux in an x86 emulator compiled to WebAssembly, port 8686, via npx); external-sandbox-container (Python, Apple Containers, port 8687, via uvx, Apple silicon); and external-sandbox-quicksand (Python, a full QEMU VM via Microsoft's Quicksand, port 8688). Try it with `container system start` then `uvx external-sandbox-container --preserve-paths --mount-rw "project=$PWD"`. A web UI gives you a browser terminal, the proxy rules, and a log of every network request.

Know the limits before you adopt it: Claude Code sends only its Bash tool to the sandbox, so its native Read/Edit still touch the host; Codex sends process and filesystem operations including apply_patch; Pi sends bash/read/write/edit/grep/find/ls. All three integrations are labelled experimental and unaudited. And sandboxing isn't verification — track state from git, not from the agent. For your role, the takeaway is the credential-proxy pattern: it is the piece to adopt first, whether in the Riot agent platform or the SaaS triage agent, because it removes the whole class of "agent leaked the keys" incidents by construction.

SolveIt goes public: a shared workspace where you keep your understanding

When KL gradients go silently wrong — exactly near convergence

arXiv — Brandon Amos (Reflection AI) · Nov 16

Rounding and cancellation erase the tiny differences the gradient depends on — right in the regime of distillation near convergence and RL with a KL penalty.

Barron et al. (2026) showed that softmax and cross-entropy gradients can silently go wrong. Brandon Amos of Reflection AI now shows that KL divergence (forward and reverse), generalized Jensen-Shannon divergence, Monte Carlo KL estimators and entropy have the same problem when two distributions are nearly equal: rounding and cancellation erase the tiny differences the gradient depends on. That is exactly the regime of knowledge distillation near convergence, RL with a KL penalty to a reference policy, and proximal updates.

The fix: rebuild the log-ratio from logit differences, and use a stable series for e^x−1−x. Amos gives eight reformulations in JAX, validated by property-based tests, plus a "closeness guard." He is honest about the limits: the examples "isolate the arithmetic rather than measure large-scale training outcomes." If your distillation or RLHF runs behave oddly near convergence, check the arithmetic before blaming the data — the bug bites hardest precisely when the model is almost right.

OpenAI opens free ChatGPT Plus to up to 200,000 researchers

OpenAI · Nov 16

50,000 new ChatGPT Plus grants for researchers open Monday, November 23 — first come, first served. Each covers the recipient and two collaborators.

OpenAI is expanding its ChatGPT for Academic Researchers program to offer free access to up to 200,000 researchers worldwide — double the goal announced when the pilot launched in July. Applications for the next round open Monday, November 23. OpenAI will award 50,000 new grants, first come, first served, dropping the lottery used in the summer pilot. Each grant gives the recipient and up to two collaborators free ChatGPT Plus access, plus training on using it in research. People already on the waitlist who qualify will be emailed before applications open.

Two things to note. First, the new grants are ChatGPT Plus, not the Pro tier the summer pilot provided — most pilot users stayed within Plus limits, which is why OpenAI moved the program down a tier. Second, the evidence for the program's value is self-reported: in OpenAI's study of more than 1,800 pilot researchers, surveyed participants estimated the same work would have taken nearly twice as long without AI. The pilot enrolled 20,000 researchers in 33 countries since July. If anyone on your teams qualifies, November 23 is the date — first come, first served means the early applicants get the grants.
