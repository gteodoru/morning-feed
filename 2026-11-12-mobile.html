Google joins the decision-model race with Gemini Decision 1

X — @OfficialLoganK · Nov 11

A model that doesn't generate text — it answers up to 1,000 questions about one input in a single pass, in 150–250 ms, for $0.10 per million tokens.

Google has entered the category we covered on Nov 8 — the "big model + cheap decider" pattern — with a new endpoint, the Decisions API, and a new model, gemini-decision-1. It doesn't generate text. You send one shared input (up to 262,144 tokens — text, images, audio, video or PDF, mixed in one request) and up to 1,000 independent questions, and the model scores every candidate answer in a single forward pass and returns probabilities.

There are three question types: "noul" (yes/no, a calibrated probability from 0.01 to 0.99 plus a confidence score), "choice" (2–255 options; returns the winner and a probability for each) and "score" (an ordered rubric of 2–255 levels; returns a probability-weighted expected score). Price: $0.10 per million input tokens, $0 for output — the shared input is billed once no matter how many questions you ask — with a free tier in AI Studio. Latency is 150–250 ms end to end, per Google. Not supported: batch, caching, tools, thinking, structured outputs or text generation.

The comparison matters more than the launch. Simon Willison says the API is "almost identical" to TypeSafe's Jev — same three question types, and it even reuses Jev's word "noul." It costs the same as OpenAI's GPT-6 Luna decision model ($0.10/M). Jev is still cheapest at $0.042/M but handles text and JSON only; OpenAI's handles text and images; Gemini's is the first fully multimodal one. VentureBeat adds Amazon's Strands Decider, which you can run on your own infrastructure.

Willison's test is the telling bit: he asked four yes/no questions about his stomping-robot video (dragon? robot? France? USA?). Jev and OpenAI returned hard 1.0/0.0 answers. Gemini returned 0.05 / 0.92 / 0.08 / 0.92 — "a little less confident," which is what calibrated probabilities should look like. On the lists, the dominant camp called it a routing and guardrail primitive: at $0.10 in and $0 out, "it turns agent routing and guardrails from a generative bottleneck into a deterministic primitive" (@Pipflare), fast enough to gate a user-visible action "and have the user not notice" (@smwca).

Why it matters for you: in an agent pipeline, many steps aren't really generation. They're judgments — which tool, which model, does this pass the policy, is this answer good enough. A generative model does those slowly, in free text you then have to parse, and it isn't trained to know how sure it is. A decision model makes each of those steps a typed, sub-second, almost-free call with a number you can threshold — and the confidence score lets your code send ambiguous cases to a fallback model or a human. That's also why Google can price it like an embedding model. If you're designing the Riot agent platform, this is the layer that sits between the big model and the action.

Continual-ARC: your agent's notes aren't learning

X — @DanielLenton1 · Nov 11

A new benchmark finds today's agent harnesses take notes but don't actually learn from experience — while training a 9B model's weights does.

Do agent harnesses actually learn from experience? Unify's new benchmark, Continual-ARC, says mostly no — and the result should change how you design an agent that is supposed to get better at your team's work over time.

The benchmark is a long stream of ARC puzzles, which need no domain knowledge, only basic priors like objects and counting. The same puzzles come back after gaps with fresh inputs, and new puzzles keep arriving. On every instance the learner has a budget of 16: it can pay 1 to see a worked example, or submit an answer, and a wrong answer costs 1 too. The learner may keep anything between instances — notes, a program library, or updated weights — so memory-based and weight-based learners compete on the same scale. That's the main design point: most continual-learning benchmarks only test one kind of learner.

The findings, on Qwen3.5-9B with GLM-5.3 Flash as a stronger teacher: harness persistence generally didn't improve memorization unless an expert told it what to store. Most harness methods learned less than a dumb floor that just caches each task's demonstrations. No harness-based method gained on unseen puzzles. Weight training (Qwen trained to imitate GLM's moves, the DAgger method) saved more than half the budget on seen puzzles and more than a quarter on the 100 new ones. A concrete run: Prime Agent "as shipped" used 82.8% of its budget on average, solved 22% of 200 instances, and spent $79.42 over 16,368 model calls in 79.5 hours. A program-library method shows the split clearly: on seen puzzles it saved 65% of budget (solve rate 43% → 78%), but on held-out puzzles only 3% (28.5% → 32.6%). Notes and libraries help memorization, not skill.

Daniel Lenton's analogy in the replies: harness notes are "like Leonard in Memento." Good for basic knowledge, but "note-taking doesn't cut it" when the model needs an intuitive feel for a new domain. The reply camps agreed on the upside — "model specialization is unreasonably effective" (@dzhulgakov) — and flagged the open question: which forgetting is safe? (@NashBriggsy: a specialist dropping internet trivia is fine; "the tricky part is knowing which forgetting was the useful kind before some edge case bites you.")

The takeaway for your platform: today's memory files are a cache, not learning. If you're betting on agents that improve at your team's work, the measured gains are in weight updates — fine-tunes on your own trajectories. That's exactly what Monte, the YC post-training platform in the venture section, is pitching.

Build distribution, not more features

This Week in Startups · Nov 11

From TWiST's VC roundtable: anyone can vibe-code a Slack clone in a weekend — the moat is the users.

From TWiST's VC roundtable, four tactical lessons for founders — each of which reads as a strategy note for a 12-person SaaS, too.

Distribution is your moat. Ben Lerer (Lerer Hippeau): anyone can vibe code a version of something like Slack over a few nights and weekends. What people underestimate is the importance of distribution, and the massive data advantage a user base creates.

Charge for the work, not the time. Lerer Hippeau backed Blacklatter, an AI-first law firm charging for its output as opposed to a "billable hours" strategy. If you can guarantee a quality product at a fixed price, consumers prefer it.

Know whether your round fits the fund's math. A small check at a high valuation means that a fund like LAUNCH would need an Uber-sized exit to see a real return. Before you pitch, make sure the ownership on offer would make a real difference to that fund.

Take tough questions head-on. Some firms, including Ben's, score founders on how they handle being seriously challenged during a meeting. VCs want founders who address challenges directly rather than changing the subject.

Guests: Ben Lerer, Jenny Fielding (Everywhere Ventures), D.A. Wallach (Time BioVentures). The through-line for your SaaS: the AI transition is a distribution problem as much as a capability problem — the users and their data are the moat, not the next feature.

AlphaGenome 2 reads DNA cell type by cell type

DeepMind · Nov 11

DeepMind's new model predicts what a DNA variant does in a specific cell — and finds nearly twice as many causal variants as version 1.

About 98% of the genome is non-coding, and most disease-linked variants sit in regulatory stretches that decide when and where a gene switches on. AlphaGenome 1 predicted those effects but didn't say in which cell type — one researcher told Nature that was "a bit of a problem, which is why it hasn't been hugely useful for us."

Version 2 is conditional: it takes the million-letter DNA sequence plus one target condition (a tissue, cell type or state) and feeds that condition into every stage of the model, so it learns how the same DNA is read differently in different cells. It was trained on more than 100 million single-cell profiles across 68 tissues, covering 637 human and 311 mouse cell types. And you can condition it on up to 256 of your own single-cell RNA-seq cells instead of a reference cell type — predictions for a new disease state or a vaccination response without fine-tuning.

The results DeepMind and Peter Donnelly report: causal variants on TenK10K (28 immune cell types) found 30% vs 16%, and 43% vs 23% for the direction of the effect, versus AlphaGenome 1 fine-tuned on the same single-cell data. The share of within-tissue expression differences it explains rises from about 23% to 34%. Linking enhancers to genes more than 100 kb away improves from AUPRC 0.48 to 0.62 on CRISPR-validated pairs. Two worked examples: it reproduced a known IBD finding — a distant regulatory variant that over-activates the ETS2 gene only in gut macrophages — and on unseen COVID-vaccine single-cell data, it showed a severe-COVID variant disrupts gene activity more strongly in activated T cells than resting ones, an effect you'd miss in resting cells.

The caveat, from Donnelly: it is selective. It makes confident calls for only about 2% of the ~6.8 million variants scored, though about 15x more often among variants that fine-mapping marks as likely causal. Where it does call, you get the three things a drug team needs: a gene, a direction and a cell type. Access is a free API for non-commercial research, private preview for commercial use. Research only, not for clinical use.

The decision-model pattern, compared hands-on

Simon Willison · Nov 11
