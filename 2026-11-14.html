Thinking Machines trained a 594B model in 4-bit — end to end, in 68 hours

Thinking Machines · Nov 13

Native NVFP4 from the first pretraining step to the last RL rollout, at a ~2% compute-efficiency cost.

Thinking Machines' Jack Cook and team explain how they trained Inkling 2 Small with a native 4-bit forward pass all the way through — no calibration step, no separate 4-bit checkpoint to ship. The ingredients: NVFP4 block scaling (blocks of 16 values sharing one 8-bit scale), re-quantization with stochastic rounding so the backward pass stays unbiased in expectation, and Hadamard-rotated 8-bit communications between GPUs.

Why it matters: on a GB300, NVFP4 runs at 15 PFLOPS versus 5 for MXFP8 and 2.5 for BF16, and values take 4.5 bits instead of 16. The team quantized only the MoE expert layers in the forward pass — the most compute-heavy and most quantization-tolerant part — and computed gradients in MXFP8, reasoning that backward passes average over many tokens so random error washes out while bias compounds. Once the math got ~3x faster, the bottleneck moved to moving tokens between GPUs, so those go in NVFP4 too.

The result: about a 2% compute-efficiency cost in pretraining, an 8% wall-clock speedup in pretraining and 20% in RL, 'with no discernible difference in reward.' The open question, raised in the replies, is whether end-to-end 4-bit stability holds at larger scales. If it does, a full pretraining run becomes a weekend experiment — and the bottleneck moves from compute to having good hunches worth testing.

Agents get wallets and front doors: ChatGPT Wallet meets Sierra's Poppy

OpenAI / Sierra · Nov 13

Payments (Wallet) plus permissions (Poppy) plus shopping (UCP) — the agent-commerce stack is taking shape on different rails.

OpenAI's ChatGPT Wallet lets an agent pay without ever seeing your card number: you add a card once, review items and total before each purchase, approve with Face ID or a fingerprint, and the card network issues a one-time payment token — a substitute card number, CVV and expiry. Rewards and dispute protections stay. It's live for dots and Pro subscribers in the US, 18+, with pre-approved spending limits by category or time window promised later. Amex, Mastercard and Visa are on board, and fraud vendors and payment processors are working on recognizing legitimate agent purchases.

Sierra's Poppy 1.0 is the other half: a protocol for a personal agent to discover what a company offers and what it's allowed to do. A company publishes /.well-known/poppy.json listing its channels, capabilities and sign-in options; a signed Session header identifies the agent and a pseudonymous user, with per-company user IDs so companies can't cross-match people. Company content must be treated as data, not instructions — a prompt-injection rule written into the spec — and tokens must stay out of model context. Every Shopify store now auto-publishes a Poppy file; Mercury, Synchrony and Turkish Airlines are live.

Read together: Wallet is how an agent pays, Poppy is what it's allowed to do, and Shopify's Universal Commerce Protocol is how it shops. The WSJ reports the other side of the same coin — banks racing to keep up — in AI Venture.

Google's Antigravity teamwork: multi-agent research that checks its own work

X — @ymatias · Nov 13

Parallel exploration plus dedicated falsifiers, and context partitioned so critics review from clean slates.

Google Research's Antigravity /teamwork is a design answer to two known multi-agent weaknesses: unreliable single attempts, and context decay on multi-day tasks. The fixes are parallel exploration with dedicated 'falsifiers' (Lean 4 proofs, unmodified test suites, simulators, hardware counters) and context partitioning — each agent gets only the files and tools for its step, and critics review from clean contexts without the producer's reasoning.

The receipts are striking: a full proof of the 2013 Courtade–Kumar conjecture, formalized in 50M lines of Lean 4; ~1.8M lines of Lean formalizing the Weil conjectures across 28,800 subagent calls; 30 agents plus a spectral simulator reaching 95% top-1 on structure-from-NMR, nearly double a single agent; and ~800,000 lines of safe Rust completing the Zircon kernel port, passing all kernel unit tests and booting on arm64 — scheduled module-by-module only after dependencies compile and test against the original, unmodified suite, so agents can't cheat by stubbing. TPU kernels got a 4.09x geomean speedup via 23-second 'yardstick' proxies checked against full runs.

The pattern worth stealing for your own platform: verification is a first-class agent role, not an afterthought, and the critic must not inherit the producer's reasoning. Google is giving 10,000 academic researchers six months free.

The CPU strikes back: Apple's former chip architect bets AI data centers still need a better brain

Bloomberg — Power On · Nov 12

Gerard Williams III says AI cut early CPU research from 7–12 months to 2–3 months. His new startup, Nuvacore, is reportedly raising at a $2.5B valuation.

Mark Gurman's Power On sits down with Gerard Williams III, the former Apple chief CPU architect behind the A7 and M1, who founded Nuvia (acquired by Qualcomm for $1.4B in 2021) and now runs Nuvacore, building high-performance processors for AI data centers. His argument: even as Nvidia's GPUs dominate the conversation, CPUs remain essential — and AI itself is accelerating chip development, cutting early CPU research and performance modeling at Nuvacore from 7–12 months to 2–3 months. A CPU designed from scratch still typically takes two and a half to three years to reach a finished product.

Nuvacore's design bet is 'Core First': the core is developed without initially tying itself to a specific instruction set — x86, Arm or RISC-V — and only later adapted to the target ecosystem. Reuters reports the six-month-old startup, backed by Sequoia, is raising hundreds of millions at roughly a $2.5 billion valuation, with no product, customers or tape-out date disclosed. The caveats are substantial, but the direction is worth watching: as data centers move from about one CPU per 30 GPUs to a much higher ratio, the CPU becomes a constraint again — and the team that built Apple Silicon is betting it can own it.

The best learning question of the week is also the biggest: is AI research about to automate itself? Two long, serious pieces disagree in the most useful way — one forecasts the regime, the other measures that it hasn't started yet.

Is AI research about to automate itself? Dwarkesh vs. Imas & Garicano

Dwarkesh Patel · Nov 13

Dwarkesh expects AI as good as the best AI researchers by ~2028. Two economists measure the R&D production function — and find ideas getting harder to find.

The two long pieces of the week reach opposite-sounding conclusions and are, as Imas himself says, not in disagreement. Dwarkesh Patel's essay argues AI will match the best human AI researchers around 2028 (median; 2027 plausible, 25% chance after 2032) and beat top experts in every field soon after, by 2030. His evidence: METR, after three months inside Anthropic, found progress on well-scoped tasks is 2x a year ago, mostly from AI-assisted R&D; OpenAI's heaviest researchers went from ~$1,000/day of coding agents to over $7,000/day by end of August; Claude 'leads' 26% of Anthropic's R&D work, up from under 1% in February. His core claim: AI research looks like the math AI is already crushing — mostly combining known ideas through persistence and many experiments, with verifiable proxies. The footnote that went viral: 'SlothAI,' an OpenAI whose researchers work one hour a week — compute isn't the only bottleneck, and next to automated researchers, today's OpenAI is SlothAI.

Alex Imas and Luis Garicano do the accounting. From Anthropic's S-1: R&D ran at ~$3.55B a year (Jan–Sep 2025) to ~$22.0B (Jan–Sep 2026), a 6.19x jump, while technical staff went from ~630 to ~1,960. Capability improved 1.91x faster — so capability per dollar fell ~69%, and per technical employee-year ~38%. Their model (Jones 1995) puts φ, the 'ideas getting harder to find' parameter, at ≈0.84: below 1 means diminishing returns, 1 means self-sustaining growth. An intelligence explosion needs AI to supply its own research input at a rate they estimate at ψ ≈ 0.01–0.04, against a required ~0.34–0.46. Keeping today's pace means roughly 4x more spending per year.

The distinction to carry into your platform planning: scaling laws are exponential capability bought with exponential inputs; an intelligence explosion is increasing returns, where each advance makes the next cheaper. Dwarkesh is forecasting the regime once AI does the research; Imas and Garicano measure that it hasn't started yet. Both are worth the full read — Dwarkesh's appendix and footnote 5 especially.

JEPA world models: 600 experiments say planning is the bottleneck, not prediction

X — @badlogicgames · Nov 13

Swap the learned predictor for a perfect physics simulator and nothing changes. The planner is where the work is.

Haddy Alchaer's write-up of ~600 experiments with LeCun's JEPA world models (LeWM) on the Push-T robot task is the most careful empirical post of the week. Finding one: swapping LeWM's learned predictor for the simulator's exact physics changed nothing (78% vs 77% with expert subgoals) — prediction is already good enough. Planning the full 75 steps at once gets 22%; chaining 25-step subgoals gets 80%.

Finding two: a memory graph of ~460,000 demonstration frames, searched for subgoals, works — but free jumps between demonstrations produced impossible paths, and adding a cost per jump raised success from 62% to 85%, matching expert subgoals. Finding three: the standard test is easy, because all demos end in the same spot and 84% of test goals had near-duplicates in memory; on 250 fresh episodes, a reported 68% became 44%. Finding four, the most general: goal images also fix where the pusher should end up — scoring only the block took the exact-physics planner from 9/50 to 39/50. 'A goal should be a set of end states, not a copy of an image.'

For anyone building agents that plan: the lesson is to invest in the planner and the goal representation, not in a marginally better world model — and to distrust benchmarks where the test goals leak from the training demos.

Benchmarks disagree: the same six models rank in opposite orders on ParseBench and OmniParseBench, and on ParseBench 73% of chart rules check values the chart doesn't even print.

Metrics that break: edit distance calls a fully correct HTML table 0.77 different from the same table in markdown, while a table with 1,410 misread as 1,710 scores 0.016. LLM judges drift — in Datalab's eval, gpt-4.1 graded 'NOT FOUND' correct 16–19 times per run. What works: unit tests (olmOCR-bench style yes/no checks), with care — about 3–4% of those tests are wrong or too opinionated. Four checks for any metric: swap the format (the score shouldn't move), corrupt one digit (the score must drop), read the failures every strong system shares (the test is usually wrong), and look for bias toward one vendor.

The DIY recipe worth stealing: segment documents and score per segment. The PDF text layer gets 63/63 on born-digital pages and 0/18 on scans. Ask every question twice — once with only the answer page (tests reading) and once through RAG (tests retrieval). On clean filings all three OCR parsers read 81/81 and the differences came from chunking; on 1903–1983 Canadian annual-report scans, reading finally separated them: Datalab 94, Mistral OCR 87, Reducto 74 of 95.
