Thorsten Ball's 15 rules for working with agents: prompt the goal, demand proof, review only what can hurt you

X — @thorstenball · Oct 25

The Amp builder's field guide — 3.7K likes, 8,059 bookmarks — says the job has moved from babysitting models to tooling, evidence, and evals.

Use the latest model, Ball argues — a model wrong 10% of the time in unknown places costs more in retries than it saves — and prompt the what, not the how: 'Make the bytecode compiler and VM AS FAST AS YOU CAN. Do not stop until it's 2x faster.' The agent should see everything you see: production logs, deploys, feature flags, bug reports, customer email, because 'if the agent can't see something, it makes it up.'

Two ideas are immediately stealable. First, treat AGENTS.md like code that can regress: spawn five blind agents, control vs. treatment, and measure whether your instructions actually help. Second, end every task with 'irrefutable proof' — end-to-end walkthroughs, demo videos, before/after numbers — and write 'this is not evidence' into your AGENTS.md for anything weaker. Review only the fewer than five central files plus database migrations; for the rest, check that it works, that you understand it at the arrows-in-a-diagram level, and that you could change it in a month.

The pushback is worth hearing. Mario Zechner, who called it 'recommended reading,' rejects the claim that slop is solved: 'the models are definitely not there yet.' Others warn against copying 'latest model' into production, where a new flagship can quietly break passing prompts — pin the model for shipped work and let the newest earn its place in a side-by-side eval — and say compaction still quietly eats prompt instructions. For Gabriel's platform work, the AGENTS.md experiment and the proof requirement are the two cheapest, highest-leverage habits to adopt this week.

StableReinforce, decoded: a closed lab's secret RL loss turns out to be REINFORCE plus Score Centering

X — @mrtnm · Oct 25

Martin Marek recovered Thinking Machines' undisclosed loss gradients exactly — and found his own paper inside.

The problem is real for anyone running RL: sampling runs asynchronously and in lower precision than training, so sampled tokens come from slightly different weights than the learner's, and that drift compounds step after step. The usual importance-sampling fix collapses when the probability ratio is e^-20 — 'not a correction. It's a zero.'

Marek's keyhole: train a rank-1 LoRA on the unembedding matrix so the gradient reveals the logits gradient, then run Adam with epsilon set to 10,000 so the update exposes the raw gradient. StableReinforce put a gradient on only 8 logits — the giveaway. Score Centering subtracts the expected score under the sampler, estimated here from 8 tokens drawn from the sampler; his reimplementation matched 'within numeric precision across all layers.' Nathan Lambert's read: 'RL details used by closed labs slowly leaking out.' The method itself is a reusable trick for auditing any black-box training API, and the fix — additive, tuning-free, composable with importance sampling — matters to anyone doing post-training on a budget.

Subliminal learning crosses models: a teacher's hidden preference transfers even without a shared base

X — @Vishesh1301 · Oct 25

Distilling from a model you don't fully trust? Its traits may ride along in the output distribution — and filtering trait words out doesn't fully stop it.

The classic demo: a teacher fine-tuned to love owls generates plain number sequences, and a student trained on those numbers starts preferring owls. Earlier work suggested this needed a shared base model. The new paper (EleutherAI's SOAR program) finds transfer survives different initializations, data orders, corpora, sizes, and even architectures — Pythia to OLMo, Qwen2.5 to Qwen3 — and soft-label, on-policy distillation transferred in 26 of 30 cross-model pairs, sometimes more than the student's own base.

The practical sting: on-policy distillation is spreading because it's cheaper than RL, and it transferred preferences 'more consistently, with less training' than hard-label fine-tuning. Filtering animal names from text or loss cut transfer sharply but not completely. Chris Potts pushes back that cross-model effects look 'weak at best' in some figures and may work by different mechanisms than same-model transfer. Either way, the takeaway for a teacher-to-small-model pipeline like Nanbeige's is concrete: test distilled students for inherited traits, don't assume filtering cleans the data.
