When the defender's AI says no: OpenCode's 48-hour siege

X — @AndrewCurran_ · Oct 18

Attackers hammered OpenCode non-stop for 48 hours — and mid-incident, the frontier models defending it started refusing to help.

After the Console provider-key breach, OpenCode's Dax Raad says attackers kept trying to break in for 48 hours straight; the team's response was to harden everything and run defense agents around the clock — because that's what the attackers have. Mid-investigation, the frontier models they were using "did quite a bit and then started refusing": analyzing the attack traffic tripped the models' own cyber guardrails. Andrew Curran framed it as the same wall Hugging Face hit.

The fix was OpenAI's Daybreak Blue — reduced-refusal access for vetted defenders, which OpenCode had applied for a few days earlier and was granted on Oct 17. The argument now: Dean Ball calls it "a fairly modest hindrance that was quickly resolved," fine against the cost of enabling autonomous attacks; Stella Biderman, sarcastically, notes "OpenAI has graciously decided OpenCode may defend itself" — access to defense depends on a lab's approval, and a small, unknown company may not get it. Paul Graham's summary: "This is how things will be for the foreseeable future."

The practical takeaway, from the replies: defenders need a trusted fast lane set up before the incident, not an application filed during it — and a way to inspect a blocked request and escalate it without starting over, with an audit trail.

The 10-point lead that stopped mattering

The Information — Eric Bellomo · Oct 18

A 10-point intelligence lead now buys ~20% more developer traffic — down from ~69% in late 2025. Developers choose 'good enough' and cheap.

The Information, building on OpenRouter usage data, finds that developers are no longer chasing the smartest model for routine work — a 10-point intelligence advantage now earns just 20% more developer traffic, down from nearly 69% in late 2025. The cheap, fast, 'good enough' models are winning the day-to-day.

Ethan Mollick's reframe is the one to sit with: developers "are going to be a small part of total token usage over time, as the internal stats on usage at OpenAI and Anthropic both show," and smarter models pay off most on long-running, high-value tasks where verification is hardest — which, he notes, is precisely where coding, the domain where cheap models are easiest to check, is the worst place to measure the value of intelligence. The replies push back on the obvious catch: if the highest-value tasks are also the hardest to verify, buyers can't tell quality apart and default to the cheaper model anyway.

For a platform builder: price intelligence where errors are expensive, not where demos are easy. The emerging rule in the replies — "cheap tasks, cheap models" — is a model-routing policy, not just an observation.

Where's the intelligence explosion?

Ramez Naam — Noahpinion · Sep 27

The clearest skeptical model of recursive self-improvement, with actual numbers — and the essay the best minds on the lists kept citing.

Naam's essay is the number the swarm debate needed. Step by step: five types of self-improvement — AI boosting human researchers and strong AI improving weaker AI are here; the runaway loop needs accelerating returns nobody has shown. OpenAI's own research-task data puts its models' 80%-success horizon at ~15 minutes of human work, against ~4 hours implied by benchmark extrapolation. Inside OpenAI, researchers used 124x more tokens per person and shipped ~7x more lines of code — but experiments per researcher rose only 1.6x. Anthropic's Mythos Preview estimates staff productivity uplift at ~4x, and says doubling overall progress would need uplift "roughly an order of magnitude larger."

The loop math: per Toby Ord, speedup scales roughly with the square root of agent count — 100 agents buy ~10x speed at ~10x the cost, and agents think alike (after the first ten AI responses, about two extra AI responses add as much creative variety as one extra human). Tom Cunningham's self-sustaining threshold is ~15% more research productivity per ECI point; Naam estimates today's loop at 2–3% per point — a 5–10x gap. Narrow superintelligence in verifiable domains (formal math, chess, parts of coding and cyber) is real; a fast takeoff isn't supported by current data.

Takeaway for your role: calibrate agent ambitions to verifiable domains, and budget verification as the scarce resource. When Levie's swarms meet Naam's math, the question isn't 'how many agents' — it's 'what can be checked automatically.'

Verbalizable Representations Form a Global Workspace in Language Models

Anthropic — arXiv 2607.15495 · Oct 2026

The J-space paper: a small set of 'verbalizable' directions that behave like a global workspace — and reveal hidden deliberation in safety audits.

The Jacobian lens asks, for each vocabulary word and each layer, which direction in the activations raises the chance the model will say that word now or later, averaged over a large corpus — a refinement of the logit lens that corrects for representations changing across layers. The set of those directions is the J-space, and the paper argues it works like a global workspace: its contents can be reported, deliberately summoned and held, used for silent reasoning steps, and passed into other computations. It only carries coherent content in a middle band of layers and holds on the order of tens of concepts at once.

Why safety people care: in alignment audits, the J-space revealed "strategic deliberation, evaluation awareness, and trained-in misaligned dispositions that never appear in the model's outputs." Post-training installs the Assistant's point of view there. The paper also introduces "counterfactual reflection training," which improves behavior by training only what the model would say if interrupted and asked to reflect.

Takeaway: if you're deploying agents at Riot scale, interpretability is moving from research curiosity to audit surface. You don't need to run the Jacobian lens yourself — but knowing that hidden deliberation is now legible changes how you think about agent oversight, red-teaming, and what 'reviewed' means.
