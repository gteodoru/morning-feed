Gemini 4 Carbon: Google is back at the frontier — and it brought a swarm

Google / X — @sundarpichai · Nov 10

Google's strongest model yet tops CursorBench and posts a 94.2% ARC-AGI-2 score at $0.38 a task — but the price doubles on November 30.

Carbon is Google's top model, built on Gemini 4 Argon, and it went from a defenders-only preview to general availability on Tuesday. Google's claims: #1 on CursorBench 4.0 at 60.4%, top of Perplexity's WANDR and Zapier's AutomationBench, and 'more than 2X lower cost per task compared to the next best external model.' The independent numbers back up the efficiency story more than the raw scores: ARC Prize verified ARC-AGI-2 at 94.2% for $0.38 a task, and ARC-AGI-3 at 65.1% on the standard harness — versus 99.1% on Google's own provider-adapter harness, a reminder that on interactive benchmarks the harness is worth 30 points and a score without its harness doesn't mean much.

The pricing is the part to calendar: $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output until November 30, then $4/$20 — exactly Opus 5.5's price, as one reply noted, so 'November is an eval window, not a discount.' SemiAnalysis ran the numbers on materials-science tasks and found Fable 5.2 costs 7.5x more than Carbon for about the same score. And access is gated: paid API and AI Studio, plus Google AI Ultra for consumers — Pro subscribers spent the launch morning complaining they couldn't use it.

The more durable story may be the /swarm design doc Google shipped alongside it in Antigravity. A coordinator agent spins up specialized workers, each in its own git worktree so edits never collide; dedicated 'adversarial verifiers' run tests and challenge diffs; all agents talk on a shared, append-only message board; idle agents 'park' at zero token cost and wake when needed. Google's recommended split: Carbon for coordinator, architecture, security audit and adversarial-verification roles, lighter models for parallel file edits. Compare with the 1,000-agent Fermat run from the day before, which used a shared Postgres database as the coordination layer — both designs replace a single coordinator's context with a shared log. Google's own warning: swarms 'consume quota much faster than a standard chat.'

DeepMind formally verified Signal's actual machine code — in four weeks

DeepMind / X — @pushmeet · Nov 10

About 6.5 million lines of machine-checked Lean proof cover the compiled ARM64 Signal library — the best learning story of the day.

The target is libsignal-protocol v0.105.4, the official Rust library behind Signal, wrapped in a small command-line harness — and the theorems are about whole runs of the compiled AArch64 binary, not the Rust source. That matters because real bugs often live between the design and the code that actually runs. What was proved, for one-to-one sessions: the post-quantum PQXDH handshake and the Triple Ratchet have forward secrecy, authentication, post-compromise security and handshake secrecy, covering init, send and receive. Key generation is not verified; the parties' keys are assumed.

The scale is what makes this a story rather than a demo: about 6.5 million lines of machine-checked Lean proof, resting on about 26,000 lines of trusted specs and ARM64 semantics, with two independent proofs — one in the symbolic (Dolev–Yao) model, one in the computational (game-based) model. It took four weeks; DeepMind's Kohli says similar efforts 'could take teams decades of combined effort.' And the sanity test is the kind of thing to steal: they put two real, already-fixed bugs back into an isolated copy, and the system produced machine-checked proofs that those versions were insecure.

The workflow is the lesson for anyone building with agents. Humans plus a mix of models translate the security goals into formal Lean specifications, and human experts review every spec. Gemini Carbon agents write the proofs, accepted only if Lean's kernel checks them against the approved spec. Agents then audit the proof and flag about 1% of it for human review. One reply picked out the detail that matters most for agent-written tests generally: the comparator checks each statement and every definition it depends on against a copy no agent can write to, because 'a goal can be weakened by editing a definition.' Lock the fixtures and helpers too, not just the asserts. Limits, honestly stated: this is libsignal, not the Signal apps — group messaging, multi-device and key generation are out of scope, and the symbolic result assumes perfect cryptography. You can check the proofs yourself: the repo builds and kernel-checks everything with Lean 4 v4.34.0 and pinned Rust 1.98.1, in several hours and about 15–34 GiB of peak RAM.

Hackers are stealing millions in AI computing power

WSJ — Robert McMillan · Nov 11

A mid-October breach at Railway exposed up to 100,000 accounts and ran up $750,000 in AI charges — and the stolen keys feed a black market of 700+ resale sites.

AI computing power has become valuable enough to steal at industrial scale. In mid-October, attackers broke out of a software container at cloud company Railway, accessed as many as 100,000 customer accounts and ran up at least $750,000 in AI charges across Amazon Bedrock, OpenAI, Anthropic and Google Gemini — plus $371,000 in stolen cryptocurrency — in a practice known as 'LLM jacking' that can cost a single organization $1 million.

The stolen credentials feed illicit 'transfer station' websites — more than 700 counted by Chainalysis, with the top five making nearly $6 million in cryptocurrency — that sell cheap access to American AI models in restricted countries including China, Russia and Iran, sometimes to clone US models through distillation. The wave began in early October and has also hit Zeabur (which shut down permanently), Warp and OpenCode — and it appears to be carried out by AI-powered 'agent swarms' that never sleep. The practical takeaway for anyone running agents against paid APIs: treat API keys like production database credentials — scoped, rotated, spend-capped and monitored — because the other side is now automating the theft with the same tools you use to build.

Don't let Claude Code design your LLM judges

Decoding AI Magazine — Louis-François Bouchard · Nov 10

28,200 LLM judgments on a writing benchmark taught one hard lesson: agents build what you decide — they can't decide what a score should mean.

Louis-François Bouchard spent five months vibe-coding ToneBench, a benchmark that asks whether a model can write a YouTube script in his voice — 141 model configurations, 7,050 drafts, 28,200 judgments from four LLM judges plus code checks. Everything about the scores came down to a handful of decisions Claude Code never suggested, and the results would have been 'TERRIBLE and untrustworthy' if he had let the agent make them too.

First: read every prompt your agent writes for a judge, and write the rubric yourself. On the early-October baseline, 119 of 141 models scored above Bouchard's own scripts on anti-slop, because the rubric punished habits he actually has — like reading an exact figure out loud and repeating it for emphasis. 'Your best outputs should sit at the top of your scale. If they don't, read the rubric again.' Second: measure the noise before you rank anything. Grok 4.7 ranks 5th on its best drafts, 29th on average and 67th on its worst; Opus 5.5 on xhigh moves about ±0.9 points and always lands first. And judges are noisy too: a Gemini judge tested against his human calibration set rated models almost randomly compared with the other four judges, so it was rejected — without the calibration set he would never have known.

Third, the judges have preferences of their own: GPT-6.1 Sol gives OpenAI's drafts about 4 points more than the other judges predict, Opus 5.5 gives Anthropic's about 2 — no judge ever sees a model name, so it reacts to writing style. The operating rule that falls out of all of it, and the one that generalizes far beyond writing benchmarks: let agents write the code, make every measurement and design decision yourself, and never let the AI grade its own work without a human calibration set to check it against.

How a small team ships with agents: push to main, release in 15 minutes

X — @mitchellh · Nov 10

Mitchell Hashimoto's three rules at Superlogical: everyone pushes to main, anyone can cut a release, and teams sync by fixing fast instead of planning.

Mitchell Hashimoto's account of how his startup Superlogical ships is the most concrete 'agents in production' post of the day, and it inverts the usual process. Three rules: (1) Everyone pushes to main. Machine-aided review happens before the push, human-plus-machine review after it; PRs are only for work outside your lane. (2) Anyone can cut a release. Starting one triggers Slack and a 15-minute countdown; anyone can pause it, nobody can skip it. (3) Teams stay in sync by fixing fast instead of planning. Platforms ship features independently, and post-hoc human and AI review flags mismatches — 'Windows has X, Mac doesn't' — with parity following within 24–48 hours.

All of it rests on mutual trust: 'if we ever hire someone who we DON'T trust to push directly to main, then they probably aren't the right fit.' The replies asked the obvious scaling questions — how do two people touching the same code sequence pushes, who reviews changes to the pre-push checks, how does decision context spread to people and their agents without sync meetings — and the 15-minute kill switch got the most praise. Worth reading alongside: an Anthropic engineer in the same window arguing 'we need fewer one shot demos and more tools that show how you can collaborate with models.'

CoreWeave's workaround to capital constraints — for now

WSJ — Rolfe Winkler & Robbie Whelan · Nov 10

Q3 revenue $3.7B beat expectations and the backlog hit $133B — but with unsecured debt yielding ~15%, the buildout runs on $9B of customer prepayments.

CoreWeave reported third-quarter revenue of $3.7 billion, slightly above the $3.6 billion analysts expected, with its backlog rising to $133 billion — and its shares rose about 2% in after-hours trading after closing Tuesday at $48.73, easing fears at least temporarily after the stock more than halved since August on rising borrowing costs. CEO Michael Intrator pointed to contract-backed financing, operating cash flow and about $9 billion in customer prepayments and deposits expected in the next three months.

The workaround is the story. With unsecured debt yielding around 15% and Intrator saying the company will not tap that market at current yields, CoreWeave is funding its buildout through customer prepayments, equipment financing from suppliers and the high-margin revenue from older, paid-off chips — while planning up to $39 billion of capital spending this year and targeting 8 gigawatts of installed computing power by 2030, up from 1.7 gigawatts today. It is fragile: data-center landlords are demanding investment-grade backstops, the equipment-financing model could leave CoreWeave on the hook if deals collapse, and political pressure is rising after the midterms. Analyst Gil Luria's acid note: CoreWeave earns only about 3% returns on its data centers versus a far higher cost of debt, unlike hyperscalers — and Nvidia, a 12.8% shareholder and key financial backer, helps finance its chip purchases, feeding skeptics' concerns about circular financing. Gavin Baker's broader observation from the same day's lists: GPU clusters are moving from offtake agreements to customer prepayments, which makes operating cash flow to EBITDA the key metric and moves the diligence burden from private-credit funds to the actual GPU customer.

Bloomberg · Nov 10

5,182 confirmed cases this year, eight deaths, and kindergarten MMR coverage below herd immunity — with the U.S. elimination status under review this week.

The U.S. has confirmed 5,182 measles cases this year as of November 5 — more than double the 2,289 recorded in all of 2025 and the highest annual total since 1991, when 9,643 were reported. The CDC counts four measles-associated deaths; state-level data puts the toll at eight, including two infants. Pennsylvania has been hit particularly hard, with more than 1,500 infections and six deaths, and has deployed hundreds of health workers and pop-up clinics. 96% of cases involved people who were unvaccinated or whose vaccination status was unknown.

The /swarm design doc: how Google runs multi-agent coding

Google Antigravity · Nov 10

A coordinator spins up specialized workers in separate git worktrees, adversarial verifiers challenge every diff, and idle agents park at zero token cost.

If yesterday's edition was the theory of multi-agent coding, today brings the design doc. In Antigravity's /swarm (preview), a coordinator agent spins up specialized workers, each in its own git worktree so edits never collide; dedicated 'adversarial verifiers' run tests and challenge diffs; all agents talk on a shared, append-only message board; idle agents 'park' at zero token cost and wake when someone needs them. Each agent can be pinned to a different model, and Google's recommended split is Carbon for coordinator, architecture, security audit and adversarial-verification roles, with lighter models for parallel file edits.

Compare it with the 1,000-agent Fermat run from the day before, which used a shared Postgres database as the coordination layer: both designs replace a single coordinator's context with a shared log. The takeaway for your platform work: the coordination layer — worktrees plus an append-only board — is the product, not the agents. And Google's own warning, worth pricing into any plan: swarms 'consume quota much faster than a standard chat.'

Audit your own work with AI — before someone else does

Michael Frank · Nov 10

A Stanford researcher had Claude audit all 79 papers he co-authored in five years. Over half had at least one error — and one spurious result is headed for correction or retraction.

Stanford's Michael Frank had Claude (Opus 5.5) audit all 79 papers he co-authored over five years — 59 of them empirical — and published the results. Openness: 58 of 59 posted code and data, but only 31 of 58 (53%) could be fully rerun. Reproducibility: 40 of 58 had at least one number not reproducible within 10% — about 2% of numbers, but papers carry a median of 155 numbers each, over 10,000 in total; of 216 deviations, 60 were typos, and ten crossed a significance boundary. Errors: at least one error in over half the papers, including a reversed boys/girls coefficient, missing SES coded as high-SES, and one upstream bug in a model-training pipeline that 'manufactured a spurious result' — a correction or retraction is under discussion for that one. Privacy: participant identifiers and Prolific IDs had been pushed to repositories.

His lesson is the one to carry into your own teams: science needs software-style reliability engineering — routine pre-submission AI audits and repo bots that catch leaked IDs the way bots catch leaked secrets. The meta-point is just as useful: the audit itself is a template. Pick a body of work your team has shipped, point a strong model at it with a clear rubric, and read what comes back before your users or your auditors do.

Hybrid attention is now the default in open models

X — @rasbt · Nov 10

Sebastian Raschka's survey: by late 2026 no open model in his set uses full attention throughout — and the KV cache, not compute, is what breaks first for agents.

Sebastian Raschka, previewing a bigger open-LLM roundup, sorts open models into three groups — full attention throughout, some full attention mixed with sliding-window or recurrent layers, and no full attention at all. The trend he reports: in 2025 there were still many full-attention models; by the second half of 2026 there are none in his set, and models split about 50/50 between 'some full attention' and 'no full attention.'

Why it matters for agents: long context gets cheaper, which helps agents that make many tool calls — and there are two different savings. Less compute, because sparse attention attends only to selected tokens or blocks and can still reach distant positions; and less memory, because recurrent layers like Gated DeltaNet keep a fixed-size state. That compression hurts exact retrieval, which is why models still mix in some attention layers. The replies added the operational point: memory bandwidth on the KV cache is usually what breaks first — agent traffic runs about 7x the tokens of a chat turn, so the KV cache 'eats the GPU before any reasoning happens.' The open question the thread leaves hanging, and the one to test in your own evals: 'Can these track a corrected tool result fifty calls later?'
