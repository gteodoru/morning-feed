It's Time to Stop Renting Your AI

This Week in Startups · Oct 14

Reflection AI's CEO makes the enterprise case for owning your own models.

Jason sat down 1-on-1 with Misha Laskin, CEO and co-founder of Reflection AI, for a 60-minute interview (E2350). Laskin's core argument is a real-estate analogy: when you're starting out, you rent — but once you're spending hundreds of millions of dollars on AI models and tooling, owning the stack starts to make more sense. The company controls its data and its costs, and stops being beholden to any single intelligence provider.

The striking line: Laskin says "six months ago, no one was really talking about open source, and now it's the opposite." Reflection trained what it presents as a powerful US open-source competitor with a relatively small team in about a year — evidence that the open-weight frontier is closing in on the closed labs.

The takeaway for your role: as you build Riot's AI foundation platform, this is the build-vs-buy framing you should internalize. Owning the stack becomes the rational default at scale — and for the 12-person SaaS, open-weight models you can self-host are increasingly a serious alternative to per-token rentals. Keep an eye on Reflection's releases as options for the support-triage capstone.

The engineer accused of bugging OpenAI's top-secret conference room

WSJ — Maxwell Zeff, Abeer Ayyoub · Oct 15

OpenAI fired Abdulrahman Alfozan the day of his arrest, days before a scheduled flight to Saudi Arabia.

The FBI charged former OpenAI engineer Abdulrahman Alfozan with violating a federal recording statute for allegedly planting an encrypted recording device — wrapped in toilet paper — to capture audio in an executive conference room used for OpenAI's most sensitive research and business discussions. OpenAI fired him the day of his arrest, days before a scheduled flight to Saudi Arabia.

Background per WSJ: a decade in Silicon Valley — Facebook systems engineer from 2017, open-source contributor, Nash, and a venture partner at Raed Ventures since early 2025; he joined OpenAI in July 2025 as an engineering lead on the product team while keeping close Saudi ties (a 2023 anecdote places him in a limousine with Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman during a meeting with OpenAI CEO Sam Altman). A camera reportedly caught him peering into the room. The thumb-sized device was plugged into a TV's USB port on September 28 and discovered September 30; its stored data was encrypted and inaccessible. OpenAI says it used its own internal AI models to identify him and trace the device's origins — and has evidence he intended to plant more devices in sensitive areas. "At this time, we have no indications of this individual inappropriately accessing or exfiltrating model weights, source code, or customer data," the company said.

Takeaway: lab-grade AI security is now counter-intelligence-grade. If you build on frontier labs' models, the vendor's security posture is a supply-chain question — and for Riot's platform, insider-threat thinking needs to cover physical devices, not just data access.

AI companies play cat-and-mouse with secret-stealing spies

Stripped logos, honeypot briefings, tiered model access — the labs' counter-intelligence playbook is the story behind the arrest above.

Even before the Alfozan arrest, the labs were on espionage alert. OpenAI and Anthropic restrict public LinkedIn bios, strip office logos, and tell employees not to wear company gear in public. OpenAI runs internal briefings warning about "honeypots" (romantic traps) and foreign-intelligence recruitment via conferences and recruiters; rapid hiring created insider-threat openings. Bloomberg doubles down on conversational AI

WSJ — Steven Rosenbush · Oct 14

ASKB now reaches every Terminal user — the enterprise playbook: proprietary structured data, citations, and injected user context.

Bloomberg has made its conversational AI interface ASKB available to ALL Terminal users — and now lets users feed it their own context: watchlists and ~40 million daily Instant Bloomberg chats, alongside Bloomberg's proprietary market data. A limited rollout began earlier this year; more than a third of Terminal subscribers already use it.

The build: since 2025, ~9,000 engineers and ~500 AI specialists; model-agnostic (proprietary + open-weight LLMs) with a "harness" directing the models and dozens of agents. Three trust pillars: content (proprietary data, certified sell-side research), accuracy (structured financial data instead of the open web), transparency (direct citations). Example workflows: analyze watchlist companies for CPI sensitivity, summarize themes/risks/catalysts in IB chats, map bullish/bearish sentiment. Bloomberg is also bringing a generative AI assistant into its BQuant data-science platform for Python investment models and dashboards. CEO Vlad Kliatchko calls it "premature to focus on the ROI" — invest aggressively now; "in the age of AI, news is one of our most valuable assets."

Takeaway: this is the enterprise AI playbook that matters — a conversational layer on top of proprietary structured data with citations, user context injected, repeatable workflows. For your company: don't build a chatbot on raw docs; build an agent harness over trusted internal data.

David's Bridal uses AI to power new strategy

WSJ — Belle Lin · Oct 14

A 76-year-old retailer rebuilt itself around agentic AI with a sub-10-person team and flat budgets — the small-org playbook.

The 76-year-old retailer — bankrupt in April 2023, acquired by CION Investment Corp. — is pivoting "aisle to algorithm." Its Pearl Planner (AI wedding-planning tool for brides) and Pearl Connect (AI platform for vendors) are powered by an in-house agentic AI orchestration system with a knowledge graph at its core — combining first-party data, proprietary research, and real anecdotes from in-store stylists. Pearl can automatically adjust planning timelines when a wedding date changes and book vendors and appointments.

The engineering team is fewer than ten full-time developers plus outsourced help, using agentic coding tools: "What would have taken one hundred-plus people before, we were able to do with just tens." Results: a threefold increase in customer engagement, 26x user growth, four years of work done in 18 months — with investment kept flat year-over-year (reallocated). Plans to integrate with Meta's Muse agent.

Takeaway: the best small-org AI adoption case study this week — agentic orchestration over first-party knowledge, a tiny team amplified by coding agents, flat budget. Directly applicable to your 12-person SaaS.

Anthropic: small models could soon self-replicate through IoT devices

TWiST (citing Ars Technica) · Oct 14

Anthropic's Frontier Red Team warns a new malware class — AI worms that carry model weights as payload — may be months away.

Anthropic's Frontier Red Team is warning of a new malware class it calls "small weight-based replicators" — AI worms that carry a model's weights as their payload and run inference directly on compromised hardware. The research asks: could small models create self-replicating infections across IoT devices the way Mirai worms install DDoS code? Unlike Mirai, these AI worms could tailor exploits and payloads dynamically, depending on the target — and each replicator that fails can leave prompts, code and notes for the next one, learning on the job.

The demonstrated flow: find targets, gain code execution, establish inference, propagate to new hosts. Anthropic ran small open-weight models on real or simulated low-cost hardware — a 9B-parameter model established inference on eight of nine hardware types, and Google's Gemma4-26B-A4B distributed across 15 cheap Android TV boxes with 4GB RAM each (slow, but running). The company says the capability may be only months away, and that such a replicator "would be challenging to shut down, since there's no centralized point to shut off — though it may be slow (with a doubling time of days)."

Takeaway: this is the shadow side of the "own your models" story above. Small models running everywhere means small models attacking everywhere. For the AI foundation platform, agent safety and device security need safety-by-design thinking for any IoT-adjacent deployment — network segmentation, AI access controls, and treating edge inference as an attack surface.

CoreWeave is the canary: the AI buildout's private-credit question

Investopedia · Oct 14

CoreWeave's 2032 bonds hit a 15% yield and the stock fell ~15% after a Palihapitiya chart — crystallizing the question of whether the AI buildout is a private-credit bubble.

The chain of events started with a chart. Chamath Palihapitiya posted on X that the yield on $1.25 billion of CoreWeave's 2032 bonds had touched 15% and closed at a record 14.5% — and asked, "Is the AI buildout creating a private credit bubble?" Elon Musk replied with a single word: "Yikes." Shares of the AI cloud provider then fell about 15% to $65.78, the worst performer in the Nasdaq 100, with contagion spreading to Nebius (−11%), Oracle (−9%) and Dell (−11%). Bloomberg's Markets Daily called it an AI rout driving investors toward bonds, with CoreWeave "at the center of the storm"; Korean stocks tumbled and bond yields rose globally.

The numbers behind the chart: CoreWeave carries roughly $52 billion in debt and leases; its Q2 interest bill was $640 million — about a quarter of revenue; its unsecured 2032 bonds traded below 80 cents on the dollar, the threshold for distressed debt, with CDS pricing implying a default probability above 50% within five years. S&P rates the company B+, in junk territory. CEO Mike Intrator told staff the market is acting "irrationally" and that the $104 billion+ backlog more than covers all debt obligations, blaming weak and delayed IPOs and debt financings at rivals for bleeding into CoreWeave's narrative.

Chamath's deeper question — the subject of his new Social Capital deep dive — is not whether AI is a bubble but whether private credit, which is funding a growing share of the buildout, is one. In Q2 2026, Apollo and Blackstone led a $35 billion vehicle to buy chips and lease them to Anthropic; UBS called it the largest private credit deal on record — one AI financing larger than all traditional PE-backed direct lending that quarter ($23B). S&P Global forecasts Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Microsoft, Oracle and SpaceX (xAI) will spend about $870 billion this year and more than $1.3 trillion in 2027, with debt, leases and new shares filling the gap. Yet in January the BIS found private loans to AI companies carried just a 6.2-point spread over base rates versus 6.1 for other private credit — lenders barely price AI risk. And in early 2026, major private credit funds paid out more in withdrawals than they raised from new investors, for the first time.

The risk cuts both ways: if AI spreads quickly it could undermine the software companies private credit already lends to; if the buildout takes longer to pay off, AI borrowers face revenue stress. Takeaway: your org sits on the buying side of this, but the lesson is structural — the buildout's financing is fragile at the margin, and heavily levered counterparties (neoclouds, infrastructure vendors) carry risk that could show up in their pricing, their survival, or your next contract's cost. Watch credit-market signals as a leading indicator of the buildout's health.

For your platform strategy, the open-source ecosystem's consolidation under a hyperscaler is worth watching: it could accelerate open-weight quality (good for self-hosting ambitions) while concentrating influence over the commons.
