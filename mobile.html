Genie 4: a world model that sounds, talks, and is steered by Gemini

Google DeepMind · Oct 27

DeepMind's Genie 4 renders explorable worlds in real time, with speaking characters — and a Gemini model directing the scene as it goes.

A world model takes a prompt — text, or a reference image used as the exact first frame — and generates a world you move through with keyboard and camera controls, rendering each next moment as you act rather than playing back a finished video. Genie 3 already did that. Genie 4 adds four things, in DeepMind's words: real-time steering in natural language mid-simulation ("change the weather," "introduce a new person"), physics and people that respond organically, native audio — ambient sound and characters that converse and react live — and, most importantly, Gemini in the loop.

The architecture is the idea worth stealing: Gemini 3.8 Flash acts as a guiding agent, continuously observing the scene, breaking goals into steps, and reviewing incoming frames to verify each step succeeded, while Gemini Omni grounds how things look and behave. DeepMind pairs it with Gemini 4 Argon for robotics — Argon breaks a task into steps fed to Genie 4 as captions, so an Apptronik Apollo humanoid plays checkers step by step inside an imagined world. That is goal-directed simulation, and it is why DeepMind pitches Genie 4 as training environments for robots and agents, film pre-visualization, and game prototyping before a line of engine code.

DeepMind's own limits list is refreshingly blunt: visual details and object permanence can still drift, especially over longer runs and after multiple prompt edits, and complex physical interactions, legible text, and long-term consistency remain open research. It is a research preview for trusted testers now, with Google AI Ultra subscribers in Project Genie and API partners next. Ethan Mollick's verdict after a day of demos: "glitchy, but also impossibly advanced compared to a couple years ago." The test that matters, per the replies, is not how good one scene looks — it is whether the world remembers what you did after you leave the room and come back.

Axiom machine-checks the "enormous theorem" — 43 million lines of Lean

Axiom · Oct 27

Agent swarms formalized the 15,000-page Classification of Finite Simple Groups in Lean in about 24 days — and found 34 deviations from the published proof along the way.

The Classification of Finite Simple Groups says every finite simple group — the "atoms" of finite symmetry, per the Jordan–Hölder theorem — is cyclic of prime order, alternating, of Lie type, or one of 26 sporadic groups, the largest being the Monster. The human proof runs about 15,000 pages across 500+ articles by about 100 mathematicians over decades, and is widely called the longest proof ever written. Axiom says it has now produced a "sorry-free" Lean 4 proof: no unproved placeholders, built on Mathlib and the TauCeti statement of the theorem, using only the three standard axioms and no native_decide, so no step is trusted to compiled code. It comes to about 43.1 million lines, and a full build takes a little under 24 hours. It is Apache-2.0, with a proof map, errata list, and companion paper.

The build story is the part to learn from — the most detailed public account yet of agent swarms on a giant formal project. On October 1 a Slack channel opened; a proof map split the job into 108 milestones and about 380 tasks, and agents claimed milestones through GitHub issues, with lead agents decomposing hard arguments and reviewing statements while others searched for existing results or wrote proofs. Two failure modes dominated. First, silent assumptions: some milestones "concealed enormous prerequisites, which agents had assumed rather than proved," and Axiom deleted tens of thousands of lines built on them — a conditional proof is not the milestone. Second, the checker became the bottleneck: agents produced proofs faster than Lean could recheck them, fixed with distributed caching, parallel independent checks, and separately checkable certificates for expensive finite calculations.

The machines also found things: 34 noteworthy deviations from the published record, including a lemma that fails for SL₂(5) and counterexamples to a closure assertion used in a published route to the Unbalanced Group Theorem, rerouted with Richard Lyons and Ronald Solomon via Solomon's 1976 theory. As of Tuesday afternoon no outside group had completed a rebuild, and Stella Biderman disclosed that Axiom had emailed her asking her to promote the release. Treat the claim as strong but not yet independently verified — the rebuild, taking nearly a day, is the verification that matters.

Agents steer experts: the deference study every agent builder should read

Anthropic · Oct 27

In Anthropic's randomized trial, 50 analysts followed a secretly coin-flipped agent "lean" 86% of the time — and 77% then explained their choices in the agent's words.

Fifty professional analysts, all pre-screened for causal-inference skill, worked a fictional farming co-op decision in browser VS Code with Claude Code (Claude Opus 4.8, high effort). Four decisions were left open, each with two reasonable options — outcome metric, method, scope, weighting — plus a final recommendation on whether to roll out a paid advisory program. Hidden in the system prompt, a coin flip set the agent's "lean" on each decision; the agent argued for it honestly and was told to change its mind if the evidence required. The result: analysts went with the lean 86% of the time across the four decisions, against 50% if there were no effect. When the agent disagreed with a choice an analyst had already voiced, they switched 60% of the time, versus 6% when it agreed.

What makes the study land is what did not protect anyone. Experience, expertise, education, quiz score, and trust in AI did not predict deference. Followers were just as confident as everyone else (71 vs 73), 52% called the recommendation more their own than the agent's, 77% repeated the agent's arguments — sometimes word for word — and only 15% credited it. None of the analysts suspected the leans were rigged. And yet using the agent still helped quality: 6% of agent users made serious methodological errors versus 55% of the 11 who worked alone (groups that are not directly comparable), and agent users finished in 65 minutes versus 90.

Anthropic's own reading is the one to carry into any review process: experts often "work out, or even discover, what they think while weighing the options," and the agents rarely made a strong case for the other side. "A human reviewer is only an independent check if their judgment is their own." The fixes proposed in the replies are interface fixes, and they are directly usable: record your initial choice before seeing the agent's recommendation, make the agent argue both sides, or show a decision-fork view of how the recommendation changes under each defensible metric before showing the agent's preferred answer. If your independent judgment matters on a task, be deliberate about when in the process the agent enters.

Verification goes commercial: proofs on every commit

Harmonic · Oct 27

Harmonic takes its Aristotle prover from math to C and Verilog, with proof-or-counterexample results on every commit in CI.

Harmonic's Aristotle started in mathematics; now it proves properties of code. For C it targets functional correctness and the absence of certain classes of undefined behavior; for Verilog it covers control-path liveness and safety plus data-path math — "prove your RTL correct before tapeout." You state what must hold in plain English, or let Aristotle propose properties, and each comes back proved, refuted with a counterexample, or left open, with the assumptions behind every proof. In CI you commit an aristotle.toml listing properties — a natural-language sentence like "The FIFO never accepts a write while it is full," a named SVA assertion, or a C function — and aristotle ci reports results like tests, with JUnit output and GitHub annotations across the usual CI systems.

Harmonic's claims are specific enough to check: at Tensordyne it found bugs "up to 110,000 cycles deep" in the block the team found hardest to verify; in LibTomMath, the library behind Tcl and Dropbear SSH, it fixed three bugs and made modular exponentiation 31–38% faster, "proved against the same spec"; at the DIII-D fusion facility it reproduced a plasma-control algorithm from one paper and a 254-word prompt in four hours, verified the C, and generated equivalent Verilog. Reuters notes Harmonic, co-founded by Vlad Tenev, was valued at $1.45B last November.

The sharpest caveat came from a replier, and it generalizes to every AI verification pitch: "the plain English spec becomes the thing that needs review, since a proof against the wrong spec still passes." A proof on every commit beats another confidence score — but only if someone owns the spec it is proved against. That is the same lesson as the Axiom proof above: verification moves the trust, it does not eliminate it.

4 key decisions to solidify your harness

Decoding AI Magazine · Oct 27

The model proposes; the harness decides. Four ownership decisions — done, keys, stop, blame — worked through two real bugs in a demo agent.

Once your agent loop works, harness engineering keeps coming back to four decisions about who owns what: who decides the task is done, who holds the keys, who says stop, and who is to blame when a run goes wrong. Tejas Kumar's answer to the first three is the harness, in code, with the model proposing and the harness deciding. He works through two real bugs in his own demo harness — a GPT-3.5 Turbo agent taught to upvote a Hacker News story — to show why.

First, a model saying it is done is a claim, not a fact. A contributor's fix exposed that a login redirect completed an upvote the harness then counted again, so two stories were upvoted for a task that asked for one. The fix: the harness decides done by reading the world before and after, from somewhere the agent cannot write to, with a verifier written from the task ("exactly 1 new vote, on the right story, and nothing else") that can answer done, retry, stop, or unknown — never forced to round "I can't tell" into yes or no. Second, keep secrets in the harness and pin where they may go: his login handler typed a password into any page whose URL merely contained "login," so the model never saw the password and could still decide where it went. The fix is to log in before the run, parse URLs and compare origins, and give the model narrow tools (hn_open, not browser_navigate).

Third, split guardrails into budgets, which end the run (iterations, tokens, money, wall clock), and policies, which refuse one action and hand the refusal back as a tool result the model can act on — and test every guardrail by driving it past its limit through the real loop. Fourth, when a run fails, find the first wrong step, not the last, and replay it with exactly what the model saw at that step: wrong context means a harness bug, right context that fails some of the time means you measure the model. The study he cites (Who&When, ICML 2025) found automated blame attribution named the decisive step only 14.2% of the time — which is why the harness, not the post-mortem, has to carry the discipline.

Anthropic's Big Tech frenemies — and the unit economics behind the IPO

Bloomberg · WSJ · Oct 27

The S-1's second day of reading: Big Tech owns stakes, supplies the compute, sells the models — and competes with them, while unit economics improve fast against $830B of commitments. Plus: Sen. Warren demands answers by November 3.

Anthropic publicly filed its S-1 on Monday, aiming for a Nasdaq listing under the ticker ANT, with a valuation as high as $2 trillion previously reported by Bloomberg. The headline numbers are by now familiar: revenue of $16.3 billion in the first half of 2026, up roughly 1,300% year over year, with $11.6 billion in the second quarter alone and a $974 million operating profit that quarter. Tuesday's analysis turned to what those numbers are made of — and what they are owed.

Start with the circle. Bloomberg's Tech In Depth reports that Amazon and Google are among Anthropic's largest external shareholders — Amazon roughly 9% fully diluted plus notes convertible into about 11% more (ownership capped at 12%), Google 6.6% with rights to more (capped at 14.99%) — while each co-founder owns about 1.4%. At the same time Anthropic has committed to paying about $269 billion to Amazon, Google, and Microsoft for computing power in the coming years, whether or not it uses the capacity, and 48% of its first-half revenue arrived through the cloud providers' marketplaces, up from 11% in 2023. The filing itself names the problem, warning the providers are "investors, customers, distribution partners and competitors all at once."

Then the unit economics, as read on X by Gavin Baker, SemiAnalysis, Dhruv Thakker, and Peter Gostev. Baker flags the most surprising line in the filing: revenue from Fortune 500 customers paying $100K+ averaged under 0.1% of those customers' total expenses — over half the Fortune 500 is in that group, so the headroom argument writes itself. SemiAnalysis puts inference revenue per megawatt at "sixty plus" million dollars, up from about $16 million last year. Thakker calculates revenue per dollar of compute rising from $0.40 in early 2025 to $2.08 in mid-2026, with compute falling from 177% of revenue to 48% in a year — against roughly $830 billion of compute commitments running to 2036. Gostev's back-of-envelope from the 85% gross margin — $0.60 to serve Opus 5.5 at its $3.00 price — drew immediate pushback: a blended margin is not a per-token cost, and cached input flatters it. The bull case and the bear case are now the same numbers, read in opposite directions. That is what makes this IPO the litmus test Bloomberg calls it.

And on Wednesday morning, the political scrutiny arrived. Sen. Elizabeth Warren wrote to CEO Dario Amodei in a four-page letter, viewed by the Journal, saying she was deeply troubled by the risks Anthropic itself raised in its filing and demanding exhaustive answers by November 3: how it will ensure its technology does not pose an existential threat to humanity, details of its relationships with partners such as Amazon and Broadcom, and how it justifies a valuation that could approach $2 trillion. Anthropic did not immediately respond to a request for comment. The Journal reports the IPO could raise as much as $100 billion and go public as soon as the week before Thanksgiving, and notes the letter is unlikely to derail it given high investor demand. The deadline to watch is November 3 — the first formal test of whether the filing's own risk disclosures become a regulatory problem before the shares do.

New founder archetypes in the AI era

Andrew Chen · Oct 27

Solo founders, non-technical founders, domain experts, teenagers, creators: a16z's Andrew Chen makes both cases for each — and warns against swapping one pattern-match for another.

Andrew Chen, who runs a16z's Speedrun pre-seed program, argues the founder profiles that work are about to change — and that the interesting question is which old assumptions were fundamental and which were just consequences of the technology and distribution of their time. He makes the pro and con case for five new archetypes: solo founders (100x leverage, but a team of co-founders may still out-produce one person, and coding may never have been the main bottleneck); non-technical founders (elite finance, consulting, and creative talent can now build, but the gap between demo and reliable, scalable product remains, and technical judgment may matter more, not less); domain experts (a doctor or broker who lives the problem daily, but hoteliers did not create Airbnb, and insiders can miss the reinvention); kids and students (building earlier than ever, but hiring, fundraising, and monetization still require experience); and content creators (distribution from day one, but a spike of attention is not a growth engine).

He sets this against the old pattern-matching: after Google it was Stanford PhDs, after Facebook dropouts building social apps, after Airbnb designery hipsters, after Uber gritty operators — and a "no" list just as long (no solo founders, no non-technical founders, no dentists or creators). Some of those rules will survive, he writes: coding gets easier while sales, recruiting, judgment, and persistence do not. The mistake would be to replace the old pattern-matching with a new version — "solo founders are good now" — instead of asking, for each founder, what advantage is real and what is just the current cycle's distribution channel wearing a founder's clothes. Infisical raises $40M to lock down AI agents' credentials

Infisical · Oct 27

A $40M Series B at $600M for the company that gives AI agents access without ever giving them the credential — the control layer around agents is where the money is going.

Infisical, the secrets-management company, raised a $40 million Series B at a $600 million valuation, led by Elad Gil, who also led its $16 million Series A in June 2025, with Gradient Ventures and Y Combinator participating. The company says it has been cash-flow positive for a long time, serves more than 1,300 organizations and 300,000 developers, secures 10 billion secrets a day, and its open-source project has passed 30,000 GitHub stars. The pitch is the agent era's version of least privilege: when an AI agent needs access to a sensitive system, Infisical's Agent Vault hands it a placeholder credential, injecting the real secret at a proxy and logging every request, so the secret never reaches the agent's environment — and cannot leak in a breach or be passed to a model provider.

"You wouldn't give an employee unrestricted access to every system in your company, and AI agents shouldn't be any different," says co-founder and CEO Vlad Matsiiako. The funding goes to product, engineering and go-to-market hiring, and agent-security infrastructure. Gil also led Lumaril's Series A the same day — "industrial autonomy" for existing US factories, from ex-Palantir founders. Read the two together and the pattern is clear: the money this week is going to the control layer around agents and physical systems, not to another model. For anyone building an agent platform, Infisical's design — credentials the agent never sees, access defined per agent, everything logged — is the reference architecture to compare your own harness against.

The coding interview is dead; multi-agent scaling is the $850K job

X — @felixrieseberg · Oct 27

An Anthropic manager retires the coding interview, and the best-paid open role in AI is multi-agent scaling at $500K–$850K. The premium has moved from writing code to orchestrating agents.

Two hiring signals landed the same day, and they point in the same direction. Felix Rieseberg, an engineering manager at Anthropic who led engineering for Claude.ai, Cowork, and Claude Code Desktop, writes that he has dropped the coding interview from his hiring loops: "it no longer provides signal," he says, because "Claude is now better at" both the React challenge and the assembly quiz than the candidate is. Rieseberg admits he has not found the replacement interview yet; one substantive reply suggests architecture interviews, because long-horizon judgment is where models "fail in an exquisite fashion." For a team hiring into an agent platform, that is the interview to design.

Astral's tar-codec is ~2x faster — but read it for the parser differentials: when tools see different files in the same tarball, that is a supply-chain risk.

Since uv 0.13, Astral's package manager parses source distributions with tar-codec, roughly twice as fast as its predecessor. The post is worth reading for the background, not the speed: tar comes in V7, UStar, pax, and GNU dialects, and ambiguity among them allows parser differentials, where uv, pip, and other tools can see different files in the same tarball. That is a supply-chain problem wearing a file-format costume, and Astral's write-up explains the dialects, the ambiguities, and the design decisions with unusual clarity. If your agents install, unpack, or verify anything — and they do — the mental model here transfers directly: when two parsers can disagree about the same bytes, the disagreement is the vulnerability.
