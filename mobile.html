Anthropic accuses Alibaba, Moonshot, and DeepSeek of industrial-scale distillation of Claude

Bloomberg Tech In Depth; Reuters · Sep 10

Alibaba's 151M-exchange distillation attack and Moonshot routing live user chats through Claude — the US–China AI fight just got its most detailed charge sheet.

Anthropic's latest Threat Intelligence report says it disrupted what it calls the largest illicit distillation attack it has seen: operators it linked to Alibaba generated more than 151 million exchanges with Claude between May and July, peaking at nearly 3 million per day from 3,500+ fraudulent accounts — aimed, Anthropic says, at extracting Claude's capabilities to improve Alibaba's Qwen models.

Kimi maker Moonshot and DeepSeek took a different route, per the report: instead of bulk queries, they allegedly funneled real, live customer conversations — sometimes containing sensitive data like locations and passwords — through Claude via illicit intermediary "transfer stations" in jurisdictions outside China, then used Claude's responses as training data. Xiaomi and Russia-linked cyber espionage campaigns were also named.

Two things matter here beyond the headlines. First, distillation itself is legitimate and everywhere: labs use it to compress big models into smaller, cheaper ones. The line Anthropic draws is between legitimate compression and illicit extraction that strips safeguards — "feeding model capabilities into their own military, intelligence, and surveillance systems." Second, this is now the sharpest US–China AI battleground: Anthropic explicitly tied the disclosure to Washington's chip-export debate and called the "window to act" narrow.

The so-what for builders: if you're shipping AI features on someone else's frontier model, your threat model now has two directions — misuse of your product by bad actors, and your own product being systematically mined for capability extraction. API rate limits and ToS are not a strategy; per-account anomaly detection is becoming table stakes.

Microsoft plans to triple its data center fleet by 2032

Bloomberg Tech In Depth; Reuters · Sep 11

12 GW today, ~38 GW by 2032 — Microsoft's planning target for data center capacity would eclipse all of New York state.

Bloomberg reporting (via sources familiar with the plans) sketches Microsoft's internal roadmap: owned-and-leased data center capacity growing from about 12 gigawatts today to roughly 38 gigawatts by 2032 — more electricity than New York state's projected peak summer demand of 33 GW that year. Capex, including leases, hit $145 billion last fiscal year.

The telling detail: only about 2 GW of today's fleet is AI-specific silicon. The rest is general-purpose compute. By 2032, about a third of the 38 GW would be AI-specific. Two readings: Microsoft believes general cloud demand is still the bulk of the build, and the AI share is set to roughly sextuple. The same reporting notes shortages already forced Microsoft to turn away some AI and cloud business — which is why a company that walked away from ~2 GW of projects earlier in 2026 is now sketching triple.

Microsoft called the numbers inaccurate without offering alternatives — treat them as a planning target, not a construction schedule. And note the accounting move: spreading long-term leases over 25 years instead of 15 lowers reported annual capex, which is exactly how you keep spending big while keeping the income statement calm.

For anyone building an AI platform: power and capacity are the binding constraints now, not algorithms. Your cloud vendor's roadmap is your product's ceiling.

Legal-AI startup Harvey closes $550M round, launches in-house model

Bloomberg; TechCrunch · Sep 10

$550M at ~$15.6B, up from $11B in March — and it's now training its own legal models on open weights instead of renting frontier ones.

Harvey, the legal-AI startup, raised $550 million at a $15.5–15.6 billion valuation — up from $11 billion just six months earlier — co-led by Lightspeed and Diffusion, with Sequoia, Kleiner Perkins, a16z, and Coatue re-upping. Revenue is reported above $400M ARR, and 80% of the Am Law 100 use the product.

Two moves in this round matter more than the valuation. First, Harvey acquired Guardrails AI — an AI security platform for agents, its fourth acquisition this year — betting that legal workflows will be agentic and need guardrails. Second, it shipped Harvey Tenet, its first in-house model, post-trained from the open-weight Kimi K3 (Moonshot's model, notably — the same company accused of distillation elsewhere in this edition) with Fireworks' help, and is pushing customers to post-train their own open-weight models.

That last point is the real story: Harvey is becoming a case study in how an entire industry can go deep on AI without depending on proprietary frontier labs. If your org strategy is "wait for OpenAI/Anthropic to solve our vertical," watch Harvey — vertical post-training on open weights is becoming a credible alternative.

Fear and loathing in the AGI era: a resignation goes viral

Bloomberg Q&AI; TechCrunch · Sep 10

An Anthropic researcher's viral resignation — 100M+ views, backed by the lab's own alignment lead — turned AI existential risk into a political moment.

The week's most viral story didn't come from a lab announcement but from a resignation. Jacob Coxon, a 27-year-old researcher who spent three years on pretraining at both OpenAI and Anthropic, quit and posted that both companies are "racing straight to self-improving superintelligence and gambling with our lives" — claiming the people building AI "earnestly believe that it could kill us all by the end of the decade." The thread crossed 100 million views.

What gave it weight: Anthropic's own people backed him up publicly. Alignment lead Evan Hubinger wrote that Coxon was "correct," putting his own estimate at greater than 10% chance of AI killing all humans within the decade. That came days after OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki warned that "no one is prepared for the consequences of a continued rapid rise in machine intelligence," and after AI safety researcher Paul Christiano was appointed to OpenAI's nonprofit board, saying the industry is not "currently on track to reduce this risk to an acceptable level."

The political class reacted fast: Bernie Sanders and Sheryl Crow amplified the concern, Rep. Anna Paulina Luna called for a special congressional session on AI, and Congressional Progressive Caucus chair Greg Casar called it "an emergency." Meanwhile, in the same edition, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent warned that "if they were to pull away from us on AI, then nothing else would matter" — the other pole of the politics: the race with China can't be slowed from Washington's perspective.

Bloomberg also flagged the data-center backlash as a defining midterms issue — local opposition to the buildout is becoming electorally real. And in the background: Anthropic and OpenAI are both nearing Wall Street debuts, which will supercharge development and shareholder pressure simultaneously. Expect this tension — safety panic vs. race dynamics — to be the dominant policy frame for the next year.

Model distillation: technique and threat model

Anthropic Threat Intelligence report (via Reuters) · Sep 10

How distillation works — and how it's allegedly being abused at industrial scale.

Distillation is both a core ML technique and, per Anthropic's latest threat report, a vector for industrial-scale capability extraction. The Reuters writeup walks through how Alibaba allegedly ran 151M fraudulent exchanges against Claude, and how Moonshot/DeepSeek allegedly routed live user conversations through "transfer stations" to mine reasoning traces.

Why it matters for you: if you're building an AI platform or agent fleet, distillation cuts both ways. You can use it to compress and cheapen your own models — and adversaries can use it to clone yours. The takeaway: treat your model's outputs as an asset to protect, with per-account anomaly detection and licensing guardrails, not just ToS.

Data center power economics: why gigawatts matter

Power and capacity as the binding constraints of AI strategy.

Gigawatts are the new unit of AI strategy. Reuters' writeup of Bloomberg's Microsoft reporting walks through how a cloud provider plans 12→38 GW by 2032, how little of it is AI-specific silicon today (~2 GW), and how capacity constraints already forced Microsoft to turn away AI business.

Why it matters: for anyone building an AI foundation platform, the binding constraint is no longer algorithmic — it's power, land, and long-lead infrastructure. Your cloud vendor's capacity roadmap is effectively your product roadmap's ceiling. Read it as capacity planning, not hype.
