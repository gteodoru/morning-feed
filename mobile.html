OpenAI claims a decade of complexity theory in one day — ω = 9/4, Smale's ninth, Hirsch dead

X — @SebastienBubeck · Nov 24

Nine manuscripts landed on GitHub on Tuesday claiming the matrix multiplication exponent is exactly 9/4, a strongly polynomial algorithm for linear programming, and a disproof of the polynomial Hirsch conjecture — if the proofs hold.

Late Tuesday, OpenAI's Sebastien Bubeck announced nine manuscripts added to github.com/openai/math, the output of an unnamed internal model. The headline claims: ω = 9/4 exactly — a matching lower bound to the 9/4 upper bound from October, closing a 50-year question (the floor of 2 was never in doubt; whether ω = 2 was). Also in the drop: VP ≠ VNP, the algebraic cousin of P vs NP; superpolynomial lower bounds for Hamiltonian cycle and the permanent; the FFT's true overhead is √log n; the Erdős–Rado sunflower conjecture; a strongly polynomial linear programming algorithm answering Smale's ninth problem; and a disproof of the polynomial Hirsch conjecture.

The caveats matter as much as the claims. The repo itself says the results are "at different stages of verification," with ~42% of top-line results formalized in Lean, that "some of the unformalized results could have issues," and that the papers are "raw research data, rather than polished academic papers." Treat each one as claimed, partially formalized, until outside complexity theorists weigh in — and note that the papers' authors defer "completely" to the math community on verification and credit.

The reactions split four ways: the lower bound is the shock (nothing was known about lower bounds for general arithmetic circuits); it won't speed up your GPU (ω improvements are historically "galactic" algorithms with constants too large to run — GPUs still do tiled O(n³)); wait for the eyes and the Lean; and the singularity camp. The honest read: the map changes even if the kernels don't — every algorithm whose cost is stated "in terms of ω" now has a fixed number. And the same tensor-geometry toolkit produced several of the claims, so they stand or fall together. Tim Roughgarden's gloss is worth holding onto: we already live in much of what Impagliazzo predicted for "Algorithmica" — machines finding proofs, specify-the-output programming — while VP≠VNP pushes back the other way on P≠NP.

Harness adapters: a small LoRA, trained inside your agent harness, lifts a 27B model from 39% to 55%

Hugging Face · Nov 24

Freeze the model, RL-train a small adapter inside a specific harness on your own task distribution — and the model reasons less, calls fewer tools, and does better first time.

Hugging Face's recipe, published by Lewis Tunstall: freeze a model and RL-train a small LoRA inside a specific agent harness (pi, Hermes Agent, OpenCode) on your own tasks. Qwen3.8-27B under pi went from 39% to 55% pass@1 on Terminal-Bench 2.1 while average tokens fell from 2.9K to 1.5K per task — "mostly… the model reasoning less, calling less tools, and performing better first time." The takeaway for anyone running agents in production: the harness is part of the model now, and a small, cheap, task-specific adapter can beat a bigger model swap. The recipe is public. Why "9/4 is tight" is the hard half — lower bounds, explained without the jargon

OpenAI / X — @SebastienBubeck · Nov 24

An upper bound is a recipe you can check. A lower bound says no recipe, however clever, can do better — and complexity theory has almost no strong lower bounds for general arithmetic circuits. That's why this half is the shock.

The schoolbook way to multiply two n×n tables takes about n³ multiplications. In 1969 Strassen found a trick that beats n³, and the exponent ω has been pushed down ever since: 2.81, 2.37, then OpenAI's October preprint below 2.258, then 9/4 = 2.25. You can never go below 2 — you have to write down n² answers. The open question for 50+ years was whether ω = 2.

An upper bound is a construction: "here is a recipe that uses n^2.25 steps" — you check it by checking the recipe. A lower bound is a statement about every possible algorithm, and complexity theory has very few strong ones. That's why Bubeck's "NOTHING was known for lower bounds" line lands.

How the paper argues it (from its intro; the proof itself is unchecked): a proof by contradiction across two problems. Start from a sorting network that costs at least w^σ volume per input bit. Suppose matrix multiplication could beat 9/4 — then such an algorithm could compress any tensor network, representing many copies more cheaply than one copy each. Compress a fixed number of times and you drop below the w^σ floor. Contradiction. The glue is the "Euclidean geometry of tensors" paper: it prices each wire by its geometric length — a bit shared between two sites costs their distance — turning "cheap routing" into a violated counting bound.

What changes in practice: nothing on your GPU — the gap between O(n^2.25) circuits and tiled O(n³) kernels is all constant factors. What changes is the map: if ω = 9/4, the decades-old hope that ω = 2 is dead, and every algorithm whose cost is stated "in terms of ω" — linear algebra, graph algorithms, LP solvers — now has a fixed number. The resource to watch: the repo itself, and the Lean formalization status per theorem.

The org is the bottleneck: 2–3 person AI teams, the Atlassian reversion, and what consulting actually sells

X — @emollick / @lennysan · Nov 24

Mollick hears of scattered 2–3 person AI teams "doing the work of dozens" — and the management fights to match. Atlassian tried all-builder teams with no PM. A month in, everyone had gone back to their old jobs.

Three data points in one day, and they all point the same way. Ethan Mollick: "scattered teams of 2-3 people using AI that are doing the work of dozens of folks… creating large-scale management fights." His follow-up: "There is no established playbook… these teams are often crossing department boundaries (marketing, legal, engineering, sales)." Lenny Rachitsky, from a talk with Atlassian's Sherif Mansour: teams of all "AI builders," no PM, no designer. "For the first few weeks, it was amazing… A month in, everyone had gone back to their old jobs." The PM's explanation: "Throughput's not our problem. Direction's our problem."

The replies supply the theory: roles carry more than tasks ("They removed the titles, not the work. Deciding what ships and saying no still had to happen"); diffusion is the problem ("Those 2-3 people rebuilt their own workflow. The dozens around them won't"); and politics decides it.

The resource that ties it together is Moshtagi and Kapoor's essay "Management consulting is not a thin wrapper on intelligence": consulting sells five things — analysis, judgment, coordination, credibility and accountability. AI is great at the first. Judgment is harder to train. The last three are about persuading competing managers and owning outcomes. Their prediction: AI integration creates demand for consulting the way business computing and the web did — but firms must build know-how clients lack, staff up in-house AI expertise, partner on deployment and stay neutral across model providers.

The takeaway for your two orgs: the constraint on AI adoption is not model capability or even tooling — it's direction, diffusion and who owns the outcome. When your 2–3 person team starts doing the work of dozens, the fight that follows is the work. Plan for it.
