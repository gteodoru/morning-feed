Claude cracks the 25-year mystery of the NIST elliptic-curve seeds

X — @sweis · Anthropic Research · Oct 16

An internal Claude research model found the NSA's unexplained curve seeds were just clock-seeded lrand48 output — in under half an hour, with runnable code to prove it.

The mystery: NIST's FIPS 186-2 curves (2000) — P-256 is among the most widely deployed curves on the internet (TLS, passkeys) — each carry a published 160-bit "seed" whose hash defines the curve's b coefficient. The NSA supplied the seeds with no explanation, feeding two decades of backdoor suspicion. The best story was a recollection that they hashed a joke phrase.

An Anthropic employee asked an internal research model to figure it out without pointing it at the passphrase clue. In under half an hour it found that the P-192/224/256/384/521 seeds aren't hashes at all — they are raw output of the C library's lrand48 generator, seeded with a Unix timestamp. P-256 is candidate #1842 from a run seeded at 1997-08-16 01:52:34 UTC. The tell: written as five 32-bit words, four of the ten seeds had the top bit = 0 in all 20 words (chance ≈ 1/5810) — lrand48's signature, since it returns only the top 31 bits of a 48-bit state. Recovering the truncated state is a textbook lattice problem, solved with LLL basis reduction over every bit window. Ten seeds (5 NIST + 5 ANSI X9.62) fit, and each recovered state regenerates all 160 bits of its seed. It also explained some base points — the first NIST base points ever explained.

What it means: Anthropic stresses this is "not a cryptographic break, new attack, or weakness" — if anything it makes deliberate weakening "much less plausible," because the seeds came from a simple, reproducible clock-seeded PRNG. The $36,864 community bounty (organized by Filippo Valsorda) goes to the Internet Security Research Group. The cleanest public example yet of a model doing original detective work end-to-end — hypothesis, statistical tell, lattice attack, verification — on a problem experts had abandoned. Note: it was an internal research model, not a shipping one.

How the Claude Code team reviews code when Claude writes ~90% of it

X — @ClaudeDevs · Oct 16

Merged PRs doubled, reverts fell 40%+ — and the team published the whole operating manual.

The numbers first: Claude writes ~90% of Claude Code's code. From June to September, merged PRs more than doubled while the share of reverted PRs fell more than 40%. Anthropic's monorepo went from ~2,500 changes/day in May to 30,000+ in September. The stated goal: most PRs merge with at most one human (who can be the author) without raising revert/incident rates.

The seven layers: (1) verify before the PR — runnable checks, a fresh-context subagent review, internal skills for bug-hunt/simplify/e2e; keep CLAUDE.md short; code owners write REVIEW.md. (2) AI review on every PR — several agents hunt, a separate group votes, style-only findings dropped, every finding must be answered. (3) Blocking security review. (4) CI. (5) Risk-tiered human approval — low risk needs no human once green, medium one, high two; a classifier sets the tier from the change, not the author; an agent's approval never counts as a human's; the person who prompted the change is a required reviewer. (6) Merge queue, feature flags, dogfooding. (7) Fast rollback, weekly on-call.

The tuning is the part worth stealing: past incidents and reverts become a daily eval for the reviewer itself. One PR went 50+ review rounds overnight (59% of repeat flags were about pre-existing code) — fixed with a 3-round cap, diff-only re-review, and no repeat findings. Humans keep five decisions: whether to build it, risky code, pattern-setting architecture, reading the evidence, and owning the outcome — "you land it, you own it."

The X discussion landed on the same point: @manuelaragones — "The number I'd steal isn't the 90%, it's reverts down 40% while PRs doubled. Review is the real bottleneck now." Same-day notes: gzip-compressing the conversation each turn, cloud sessions starting as you type, and Code Review GA on Team/Enterprise with Quick/Standard/Deep levels per repo.

Anthropic quietly pitches its own federal AI safety bill

Only Commerce, via a DC judge, could halt a model — and only for >50 deaths or >$1B in imminent risk. Piper: "Anthropic wants preemption."

Anthropic has quietly pitched Capitol Hill lawmakers on its own draft bill to regulate frontier AI safety, Politico reported Friday, citing three people with knowledge of the efforts. The shape of it:

• A model could be halted only for a foreseeable, imminent risk of more than 50 deaths/serious injuries or more than $1B in damage — from bioweapons, a model evading developer control, or cyberattacks. • Registered independent evaluators would report imminent risks to Commerce within 72 hours; only Commerce, via a DC federal judge, could suspend a model; every other agency would be barred. • Labs could pay the evaluators, as long as payment isn't tied to outcomes.
