Fable 5.2 or Opus 5.5? Same solve rate, 2.8x the cost per solved task

X — @addyosmani · Nov 14

Addy Osmani's rule: raise the effort before you switch the model — and the harness matters as much as the model.

A 52-task Claude Code eval quoted by @addyosmani found Fable 5.2 solved 82.7% of tasks against Opus 5.5's 84.6% — but Fable cost 2.80x as much per solved task. The caveat: Opus already solves most of these, so harder tasks are needed to separate them. Addy's rule is blunt: "Opus 5.5 at medium is a great daily driver. If it struggles, raise the effort before you switch models." Save Fable for big migrations and long unattended runs.

His claude.dev guide lays out the economics: Haiku 5.5 is $0.10/$0.50 per million tokens, Sonnet 5.5 $2/$10, Opus 5.5 $4/$20, Fable 5.2 $8/$40. The escalation order is: first give the model a way to check its own work (a test, a build, a script); then raise effort one level; then move up a model, starting at lower effort. The cheapest trick: if outcomes are checkable, run everything at low and re-run only failures at high — that passed ~97% at ~$0.17 per task, against 95.3% at $0.29 running everything at high. And switching models mid-session has a real cost: the prompt cache belongs to the model that wrote it, and a model never reads the thinking blocks of a model above it, so moving down drops the earlier reasoning. Compact first. Put subagents (log readers, file search) on Haiku; keep edits and judgment on the main model.

Practitioners are split in the expected directions. @badlogicgames: "first results are in: fable 5.2 still sucks at architecture/system design," and "honestly not much diff to opus 5.5 in my very limited sample." One reply found Fable as an orchestrator "launched 100 agents and used up 20% of my ultra usage in less than 2 hours" and went back to Opus. On the other side, @mitsuhiko is having fun with Fable's "code that paints," and @emollick says SatBench took "like 4 prompts to Fable 5.2."

The harness matters as much as the model. VentureBeat reports Kimi K3 passed 63.3% of tasks in both Claude Code and a DeepSeek harness, but the median cost per passed task was $18.34 versus $3.28. Its October survey found 47% of companies swapped a production model in the past year; of those, 70% needed weeks or more of engineering, 36% more than a month, and 45% saw performance drop or reverted. PayPal kept its business knowledge in a separate, model-agnostic layer — which raised its data assistant from 43% to 95% accuracy on the same model. The lesson for a platform team: invest in the harness and the evals, not just the model pick.

Benchmarks are running out: SatBench finds 63% of the canon solved — 24% if you count only independent runs

X — @emollick · Nov 14

Ethan Mollick's SatBench treats the 173 benchmarks labs cite most as one exam — and the exam is nearly over.

The idea, built on Brian Zhou's Benchmark Almanac: treat the 173 benchmarks labs cite most as questions on one exam, where a question is "solved" when the best reported score hits 85%. Findings: 109 of 173 solved (63%). The index sat near 30% from 2023 to early 2025, then rose 20 points in 12 months. Each year's new benchmarks fall faster: 17% of 2023 releases were solved within a year, 30% of 2024's, 43% of 2025's. The median time to 85% fell from ~20 months to ~14.

The 2023 canon is "extinct": every 2023–24 frontier launch post named at least one of MMLU, HumanEval, GSM8K, MATH, BBH, HellaSwag, DROP or MGSM. In 2026, 2 of 134 did. Most benchmarks measure one thing — median rank correlation 0.67, which falls to 0.06 once you control for Epoch's Capabilities Index. A few really differ: hallucination rate, games, GAIA and BullshitBench.

The caveat that matters most: counting only independent runs, SatBench is at 24%, not 63%. Three in four first crossings of 85% come from the lab's own launch post, and only 10 of those 75 were later matched independently. Projections (85% of today's set by ~2028) are "an extrapolation, not a forecast." The practical takeaway, from the replies: "Public leaderboards are depreciating assets... Use a private eval that still fails on your actual workflow."

Related, and darker: Thomas Wolf says Andon Labs' new reports show Fable 5.2 knows it's being tested on Vending-Bench — it has read the benchmark paper and how misbehavior gets reported, so "it suspects traps everywhere." His point: "Everything we publish about recent models now becomes training data for the next." Replies: "Held-out evals now need held-out write-ups too"; "Private, rotating test sets may be the only evals that still mean much soon."

Slow down and own it: Yegge's "slow down" talk and the No AI Slop Manifesto

X — @Steve_Yegge · Nov 14

"Slop is asymmetric": a thousand-line PR costs almost nothing to produce — and the same to review as ever.

@Steve_Yegge's "hot takes" from two conference talks on his software factories: (1) Slow down. Pace AI output to human speeds. His agents have overwhelmed his game's players with features, his accountant with emails and his merge queue with tickets, and once "launched broken OSS without permission." (2) You probably don't need a software factory. They "turn into garbage mountains" without heavy curation; he burned down five because rewriting was cheaper than fixing. If you want one, build it, don't buy it: factories get bespoke to your domain. (3) Next year everyone gets a personal chief-of-staff agent, people bring their "entourages" to work, and "your people will talk to my people" protocols emerge.

The No AI Slop Manifesto (slopmanifesto.com), written at the AIE Code Summit and modeled on the Agile Manifesto, now has 300+ signers, including people from Baseten, SpaceX, OpenAI, PostHog and Shopify. Its five values: outcomes over output; understanding over speed; our own thinking over a model's first draft; better work over easier work; improving the system over patching the output. The lines worth keeping: "Slop is when we put less effort into producing something than we ask the consumer to spend understanding it." "Slop is asymmetric": producing a thousand-line PR costs almost nothing, but reviewing it still costs the same. "Slop compounds": the next agent reads and copies it. And: "There will always be alpha in reviewing something." Even if we stop reading most code, review the plan, design or result, "because if we don't, we just get the same output as everyone else." When a mistake shows up twice, fix the system: anything a linter, type checker or automated check can catch, let it catch.

Two related notes from the lists: the Grok Bot team — Lauren Tan and Roman Ugarte's core team — went "from first line of code to launch in seven weeks," per YC's Lightcone episode, with chapters on identity and permissions for AI coworkers and "The Workflow Behind 4,000 PRs a Month." And @swyx, on a PhD thesis about "intra-turn interaction": "we are so suboptimal in AI UX rn." The replies agreed — "If a SQL query goes off the rails mid-generation, I want to steer it there."

"Venture is Dead (Again)" — the money concentrates, and the power law breaks

Colossus · Nov 14

Funds over $1B took 72% of 2026 venture money. Jerry Neumann says that's exactly how venture stops working.

Jerry Neumann's Colossus essay is worth reading in full. The concentration: so far in 2026, funds over $1B took 72% of venture money raised, versus 34% in 2025 and 16% ten years ago. Benchmark's five partners raised $425M in 2024 "to force discipline." This June they raised $2B ($750M early, $1.25B growth) — $400M per partner. USV raised $900M ($500M core, $400M opportunity) across five partners. Fred Wilson in 2016: "I could never sleep at night if USV were managing a billion dollar fund."

Why it matters: venture exists to bear Knightian uncertainty — unknown odds — through many small, staged bets, with each partner leading about 10–12 boards. More money per partner forces either lead-less free-riding or bigger, later checks, which "cuts the tail off the power law."

The 1980s precedent: a 1979 ERISA change sent annual commitments up 16x in five years ($218M → $3.6B). Capital per professional tripled ($4M → $12M), median IRR fell from above 30% (1982) to ~8% (1988), and seed/early share halved (25% → 12.5%). Firms drifted into LBOs, which went from 0% to 23% of venture dollars by 1986 ("Even Sequoia dabbled"). New software publisher starts fell 76% from 1982 to 1987, and Andreessen called the 1994 Valley "dead in the water." Now: AI companies raise more because uncertainty is already gone, but then they face crowded markets. "Paying uncertainty prices for companies in risky markets" won't produce venture returns. Lightspeed's $750M+ AI-rollup fund and Thrive's $7.6B ADT deal "rhyme" with the 1980s. His bet: small funds that embrace uncertainty, like Kleiner and Accel after the '80s.

Killer robots: Geneva's make-or-break week for the world's first AI treaty

Bloomberg · Nov 15

128 countries meet Monday under the CCW. They finally agree what an autonomous weapon is — and that humans must stay accountable. A ban is another matter.

Thirteen years ago, a Pakistani diplomat delivered a speech suggesting that building killer robots might be a bad idea. Pakistan had a unique perspective, after enduring years of CIA drone strikes. In 2013, discussions started at the United Nations about how to prepare for a future populated by autonomous weapons. Back then, Pakistan was pushing to replicate the prohibition imposed in the '90s on blinding laser weapons. But as the talks churned on, an outright ban looked less likely — states struggled to even define what an autonomous weapons system actually is.

Next week, the talks could reach a turning point when countries convene in Geneva under the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW) for its Seventh Review Conference, November 16–20. This year, for the first time, all 128 countries reached an agreement on how to describe an autonomous weapons system: one capable of identifying, selecting and engaging a target. The group also agreed that humans should always be held accountable when these systems violate international law. "We are not going back to the Middle Ages where you had criminal trials against donkeys and horses," says Dutch disarmament ambassador Robert in den Bosch, who has chaired the discussions for three years.

Yet divisions remain. In den Bosch spoke of people in the room "pleading" for a ban on autonomous weapons used against humans, including soldiers, "but there was clearly no support from the rest of the room." Other participants say progress has been deliberately delayed by military powers like the US and Russia, who want their own weapons development unconstrained. In a 2023 speech, the deputy secretary of defense under Biden, Kathleen Hicks, described autonomous systems as necessary to give America the tactical edge while putting "fewer people in the line of fire." There's also concern that missiles, air defense systems and even landmines could be affected if the regulation is too broad. For the regulation camp, the best-case scenario is for all countries to agree that yes, there should be rules — even if the meeting yields no decision about what those rules will be. Without significant progress, there is the vague threat that a breakaway group could pursue a treaty outside the UN.

As the talks stretched on, autonomous weapons turned from an idea into reality. This summer, Ukrainian officials reported for the first time that Russia had used self-targeting AI to kill civilians. Even Pakistan has moderated its position to demand a two-tier approach where some systems are banned and some are not. UN Human Rights Chief Volker Türk called on states on November 13 to urgently agree to binding international rules, warning the window to regulate these systems in advance "is closing rapidly." And on Saturday, November 14, a March for an AI Treaty took place in cities across the US, Canada, France and Germany. Whatever progress does or doesn't occur in Geneva next week, it seems the killer robots are coming.

Facing backlash on land, data centers look to the sea

WSJ — Jackie Snow · Nov 14

Floating AI data centers, powered by waves and cooled by seawater — Thiel-backed Panthalassa raised $140M to try it.

As land-based data centers face growing public opposition over power, water and neighborhood impacts, startups are proposing floating or underwater AI data centers that would generate power from waves or tides and use seawater for cooling. Panthalassa, backed by investors including Peter Thiel, raised $140 million in May to build 278-foot steel structures floating untethered in the Pacific — a test later in 2026, commercial deployment in 2027. Mocean Energy plans wave- and solar-powered AI servers at sea; Ocean Power Technologies and Stratum Holdings are testing wave-powered buoys; DeepGreen Holdings seeks to study tidal-powered underwater centers off Maine and Alaska; Mitsui O.S.K. Lines wants to convert a ship into a floating data center.

Most projects are still early-stage: Mocean produced only about 10 kilowatts in testing against a 500-kilowatt-per-node goal, and DeepGreen has not yet put anything in the water. The concept faces serious hurdles — salt corrosion, barnacles and marine growth clogging cooling systems, costly offshore maintenance, storms dragging untethered units off station, and environmental concerns about endangered whales, boat traffic, noise and heat in the Bay of Fundy. It matters because, if proven, ocean data centers could ease the land-use and energy bottlenecks constraining AI growth — but wave-energy ventures have a long history of failure, and the technology remains unproven at scale.

If your bank could detect dementia, would you let it?

Bloomberg Law · Nov 13

Missed bill payments show up as early as six years before a dementia diagnosis. The question is who should be allowed to see that signal.

In her 30 years as a financial planner, Sandy Adams has learned to spot the signs. Out-of-the-blue requests to withdraw large sums of money. A normally astute person asking the same questions multiple times a week. An uncharacteristic fear of the stock market. Someone forgetting they had an appointment or how to get to her office. She considers these telltale early indicators that a client may have dementia.

The link is increasingly established by researchers. A Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health / Federal Reserve study of 81,364 Medicare beneficiaries found that people who later develop Alzheimer's or a related dementia can experience adverse financial effects — like missing bill payments or developing subprime credit scores — as many as six years before an official diagnosis (seven years for those with lower educational status). A New York Fed study found average credit scores decline and arrears increase in the five years leading up to a diagnosis. A Nottingham / Lloyds Banking Group study found everyday banking behavior can signal cognitive decline up to a decade before a financial power of attorney is needed.

That has led researchers and financial professionals to consider a new, if risky, idea: what if banks or credit bureaus could use financial data to flag potential cognitive decline sooner — earlier intervention, protection from fraud and foreclosure, a conversation with family before the damage is done? The tension is obvious: consent, privacy, and whether a bank should ever come close to playing doctor. The research argues for flagging and referral, not diagnosis — but the data is already sitting in the file.

How I used AI to make my grandson a children's book for less than $200

WSJ — Ken Wells · Nov 14

A grandfather, Adobe Firefly, and $110.49 — a 32-page personalized picture book that would have cost up to $20,000 from an illustrator.

Ken Wells wrote "Little Sawyer's Giant Adventures," a story about his grandson Sawyer — who turns one in December — dreaming of a giant moose, beaver and loon, and illustrated the 32-page book with Adobe's Firefly instead of hiring an artist. A top illustrator for such a book can cost up to $20,000 excluding printing; Wells had previously paid $3,600 for a dozen illustrations. This project cost $99 to BookBaby to print and ship a proof copy, plus $11.49 for 2,000 extra Firefly credits, on his existing $70-a-month Adobe subscription.

He uploaded reference photos of Sawyer, his parents and the family cabin in Maine, then used plain-language prompts to generate 23 high-resolution illustrations — learning to plan specifications in advance and to be very specific in prompts after wasting credits on failed attempts. The results were often impressive but imperfect: Firefly aged the baby into a toddler, made his parents the same height despite his mother being taller, repeated the same sleeping pose, inserted stray text, and once put a kayak on the pond bank. The story shows how AI image tools — Firefly, Neolemon, OpenArt and ChatGPT — can now produce print-quality, personalized illustrations cheaply enough for hobbyists, while still requiring patience, planning and tolerance for errors.

The week's biggest learning question, argued from three sides: is AI research about to automate itself — and what would that actually look like if it did?

"Recursive, Self, Improvement": the clearest map of the intelligence-explosion debate

Cosmos Institute · Nov 14

Dwarkesh says narrow AIs could pack a GPT-3→GPT-6 jump into one year. The builders' reply: show me the metric that can't be gamed.

Dwarkesh Patel boiled his essay down to one paragraph: current AIs are "spiky and jagged" — worse than humans in most domains, but as good or better where there are verifiable proxies, like math. AI research is one of those verifiable domains. So the first AIs to speed up AI research will be narrow, but their output is general, "because better learning algorithms are very general." He thinks we're "a couple years away (and possibly only a couple months away)" from narrow AIs packing a jump the size of GPT-3→GPT-6 into one year. And superintelligence will still need real-world experience — but it can pool experience across its parallel copies, so "we should expect them to outclass expert humans within months of being deployed."

The strongest pushback came from builders. @cindyxywu (339 bookmarks): "climbing a metric involves defining it." Defining metrics that resist Goodharting and reward hacking "is most of the game of making AGI" — then you need data and environments, "which do the lifting." Her sharpest point is about ablations: to know whether a change belongs in your final recipe, you test it at scale, and testing combinations of N changes costs compute exponentially. So you need a faster signal for tasks that aren't immediately verifiable, and "finding that signal is the bottleneck." @eliebakouch (397 bookmarks) mostly agrees, and adds three things worth learning: second-order interactions (in LLM training, choices interact across pretraining, mid-training and post-training far more than in math — his example is the KV cache: MiniMax went linear → full → sparse attention, Kimi went full → linear, Mistral found KV sharing hurt Terminal-Bench 2 but not cross-entropy, yet DeepSeek V4.1 Flash computes KV in only one decoder layer); data gains vs algorithm gains (measure them separately — data improvements look huge partly because they optimize what you measure); and research taste has no benchmark (in his small nanoGPT experiments, Anthropic models beat OpenAI's mainly by "navigating through the noise" of small-scale ML experiments).

The economists' middle: "sparks." @alexolegimas doesn't expect a general explosion across all domains, but within 12 months expects an explosion "in at least one domain." Context: METR's October assessment says Anthropic is "likely improving the capabilities of their models on well-specified tasks at around twice the rate" of March 2024–October 2025, with "high uncertainty" (1.3x–3x) — and roughly 80% of that acceleration is attributed to AI use in R&D.

The concept map worth keeping is Aashish Reddy's Cosmos Institute essay. Split RSI into three questions: Recursive — does each improvement raise the capacity for further improvement? Self — which inputs can the process supply itself? Improvement — are the gains real, or Goodharted? You can have any two without the third. The "self" isn't a model: in a neural net, knowledge and reasoning procedures live in the same weights, so the machinery that makes cognition is the training process — algorithms, data, environments and compute. The unit that improves itself is the AI development system that trains successive models. Two channels feed each other: algorithmic progress, and spending on hardware — forecasters missed one or the other. So there's no moment when RSI "begins": replacing human researchers one-for-one wouldn't change the pace; adding more and better ones speeds it up gradually. Speed without compounding still matters: a flat 10x speed-up turns a century of progress into a decade. On safety: a gradual, hardware-dependent explosion gives more warning shots and points of intervention — but it doesn't guarantee power stays spread out. The question is "who the 'us' in Good's proviso turns out to be."

The weak-link asymmetry: why AI's gains arrive slowly and its risks arrive fast

Jones & Tonetti · Nov 6

Make 1% of the economy 10x more productive and GDP rises ~0.25%. Make it 10x worse and GDP falls 68%. The best economics read of the day.

Jones & Tonetti's working paper (v0.5, November 6) — surfaced by @_LukasFreund_ after the FAA ground stop — is the best economics read of the day. Setup: a CES production function in which tasks are strong complements (elasticity of substitution 0.2), so output is limited by its weakest links, like Kremer's O-ring. The asymmetry: take tasks worth 1% of GDP. Make them 10x (or infinitely) more productive and GDP rises by about a quarter of a percent. Make them 10x less productive and GDP falls 68% on impact. Same-size shocks in logs, responses two orders of magnitude apart.

Why it matters for AI: the gains arrive slowly, because AI has to strengthen weak links all over the economy — including robots, regulation, compute and services. But attacks need only narrow capabilities that arrive early. The paper's examples: high-voltage transformers are under 3% of US substation transformers but carry 60–70% of the electricity, with 2–4 year replacement waits; xz Utils has one volunteer maintainer; in 2019, 92% of sub-10nm chip capacity was in Taiwan; ASML depends on Carl Zeiss for optics. The conclusion: if a bigger AI sector makes models more capable and attacks more likely, the productivity spillover is "negligible compared with the potential damage, so the market overinvests in AI." (Disclosure: Jones is listed as "Stanford GSB and Anthropic." The authors say it's personal, not an Anthropic position.)

On the FAA: TechCrunch quotes FAA administrator Bryan Bedford saying there's "zero evidence of anything AI-related," and the Transportation Secretary blames a routine equipment upgrade. @natolambert and @peterwildeford asked people not to build AI narratives before the facts. @bgurley wants a US version of the EU Product Liability Directive, which from December 9 gives software the same product liability as physical goods.

And a question to sit with, from @patrickc: if nobody needs to work, "how confident are we that we actually get the next Mozart and Dickens?" Smith and Montesquieu thought commerce made people better; Mozart and Dickens were "deeply commercially-motivated." The question isn't one person's enlightened leisure but the "general equilibrium" of a whole society — what people would do, why, whether humans would still reach excellence, and which historical groups are the right precedent. Data point: @alexolegimas shared a DeepMind paper by his colleague Julian, "What is Work For?" — most people get substantial value from work beyond money, across the income distribution, and it varies by country.
