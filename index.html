Verification is the new gate: Addy Osmani on what replaces code review

X — @addyosmani · Oct 17

When code arrives faster than humans can read it, proving it works becomes the job — with a reply-thread checklist your teams can steal today.

Once code is generated faster than humans can read it, Osmani argues, "the bottleneck shifts to proving that it works." Human line-by-line review "is no longer going to work as a gate" — it has to become "a multi-layered verification system, built with fast deterministic feedback loops for your agents": end-to-end tests, feature maps (a machine-readable map of what the product does), property tests, and multi-agent adversarial review. Humans should focus on "product intent, risk assessment and evaluating evidence," scale approvals by risk, and "turn your test failures into living evaluations."

The replies are the most useful part — a stealable checklist:

- Keep "a separate acceptance suite it can run but cannot edit," because if the same agent writes code and tests, one wrong assumption passes both. - Have the AI reviewer list what it decided to ignore, and give that list to a different model family — "over half the misses were doubts it raised and then dismissed." - Send review findings back to the agent that wrote the code, "otherwise layer 1 finds the same thing every week." - Let the agent open the PR and wait on checks, but merging needs human approval of the exact commit SHA — and any new push voids it. - Let the agent deploy to staging, hit real endpoints and read logs: "Reading code tells you it looks right; a running system tells you it is."

Open questions: how to define "high risk" (file path, blast radius, or agent-flagged?), and how to keep adversarial reviews from becoming "the new merge queue." This is the direct operating manual for your own teams, Gabriel — the layer between "agents write the code" and "it ships."

A 12,000-line decode megakernel makes Kimi K3 much faster for everyone

X — @SemiAnalysis_; vLLM · Oct 17

NVIDIA and vLLM fused attention, MoE and NVLink communication into one decode path — 1.5–1.9x faster on the same GB300s. "The hardware isn't moving — the software is."

Decode — generating one token at a time — on a giant MoE model is dominated by small, memory-bound operations and cross-GPU communication, not big matrix math. Stock vLLM spent 130 µs and 23 separate kernel launches per Kimi K3 layer. NVIDIA's Benjamin Chislett and the vLLM team fused almost everything into custom CuTe DSL kernels: 13 kernels, 69 µs per layer.

The tricks, in plain terms: pick different kernels for different batch sizes; fuse the attention output projection with its all-reduce and the residual step, with GPUs writing partial results straight into NVLink-shared buffers; split router scoring across tensor-parallel ranks; run the shared expert on its own stream while prefetching weights; and decode context parallelism, which splits a long KV cache across GPUs.

The numbers (vLLM/NVIDIA): 842 tok/s at batch 1 with speculative decoding vs 488 stock (1.73x); 1.88x without speculation; P90 per-user interactivity up 1.8x on SemiAnalysis's AgentX workload. How it was built matters as much: coding agents helped write the kernels and fusions under a "fast-track" RFC that keeps changes inside model code, bounds scope, and requires systematic correctness and performance validation. Merged upstream Oct 16, opt-in; DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash and GLM-5.3 ports next.

The thread's framing: "the hardware isn't moving — the software is. Kernels are the new moat." A grain of salt from the replies: the headline tok/s/user number is one config point (crank batch size, eat the latency hit), and the 842 figure uses a fixed synthetic acceptance rate near the top of real-prompt measurements.

Leo Gao: neural networks are not already clean

X — @nabla_theta, @NeelNanda5; LessWrong · Oct 16

A rebuttal to the "neurons are already clean" claim: a neat pattern in activating examples isn't an explanation — simulate the neuron and measure downstream KL.

Context: this is a rebuttal to Nick Cammarata's claim (covered Oct 16) that if you look hard, most neurons have a simple pattern behind their activating examples — so polysemanticity was overestimated. Leo Gao grants the point — "we should update on it" — but says a nice pattern among activating examples doesn't prove you've explained the neuron.

Failure mode 1: the converse doesn't hold. Every example that activates the neuron may fit "dogs riding bicycles," yet not every dog on a bicycle activates it — it might only fire "on dogs on bicycles on sunny days." Failure mode 2: magnitudes matter. Weak activations carry information; his best guess is that ablating small activations "quite substantially degrades model performance."

The fix is simulation: write a graded rubric ("a dog literally on a bicycle is 4.5; a dog and a bicycle but no riding is 3.2"), replace the neuron's real values with the rubric's predictions, and see whether the model degrades. He scores by downstream KL divergence on a large dataset rather than correlation — "if an unexplained detail doesn't change the model's behavior, KL won't punish it; if it does, it seems completely fair to demand an explanation."

Neel Nanda: "Another good contribution to the 'no, we have not solved interpretability' discourse." Gao's closing warning: interpretability "has often gotten stuck because of a lack of rigor, focusing on things that look cool."

Agentic flooding: when removing friction breaks the service

X — @emollick; Hertie/GovAI (arXiv) · Oct 18

84 documented cases across 11 jurisdictions where AI-removed friction flooded government services — 87% of them just LLM-written text, no autonomous agents needed yet.

The paper (Schmitz, Hammond, Chan; Hertie School, Cooperative AI Foundation, GovAI): surges in the volume or complexity of requests that strain a service, enabled by agents cutting the cost of interacting with it. From 2,288 candidate services in 12 countries, 84 cases in 11 jurisdictions met three strict criteria — a plausible mechanism, an observed demand change, and attribution to AI by officials or a reputable source. Officials themselves attribute 69%.

Justice and legal services are most common (19 cases). Examples: Australia considering reintroducing FOI fees; German social courts attributing a 55% rise in caseload to AI-written claims (some letters ran over 4,000 pages); Dutch property-valuation objections; UK online money claims; Japanese energy-plan consultations blocked by IP address.

The mechanism is the surprise: 87% of cases are simply LLMs writing legally sophisticated text, with humans doing the rest by hand. Autonomous browser agents aren't visible yet. Most exposed: services that are financially attractive and whose demand was held down by friction or tacit know-how rather than by design — tax returns, court claims.

Responses split two ways: suppress demand (fees, rate limits, in-person requirements, blocking bots — fastest, already used in 17% of cases) or increase capacity (staff, AI processing, redesign). But friction "disproportionately deters poorer, less digitally literate, and otherwise vulnerable users." The paper offers a 13-factor risk matrix and recommends auditing exposure now, putting digital identity into the most exposed services, and getting legal clarity on response options in advance. Ethan Mollick's framing: "Status quo isn't an option."
