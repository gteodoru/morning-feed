AI is finding the holes faster than institutions can patch them

The Washington Sun; Google GTIG; X — @unusual_whales · Nov 17

Google found the first fully autonomous attack pipeline in the wild — and bank executives and the NSA told Congress the week's outages came from rushing patches for AI-found flaws.

Google's Threat Intelligence Group says "the era of autonomous attacks has arrived." Its Q4 AI Threat Tracker describes an attacker pipeline, found on an exposed staging server and dubbed exploit_agent, that diffs software patches, works out the flaw that was fixed, and builds the exploit — with no human in the loop. The concept to learn is patch diffing, or n-day exploitation: a security update tells attackers exactly where the hole was, and the gap between "patch published" and "exploit exists" used to be days or weeks of skilled human work. If it shrinks to minutes, patch fast or get hit.

Then, late Tuesday, The Washington Sun reported that bank executives and the NSA told members of Congress this week's outages — bank logins, the NSA's own classified network, the nationwide flight stop — "were caused by attempts to fix vulnerabilities identified by artificial intelligence." A briefed official: "These models are identifying massive vulnerabilities, and that increases the urgency to patch right away… They're trying to push through a lot of patches all at once — more than the system was designed for." Officially, JPMorgan blamed "software-related bugs," the NSA said "scheduled routine maintenance" caused "temporary degradation," and DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin said new technology was being pushed "on top of really old computer systems." The FAA canceled 20,000+ flights in its ground stop and has now halted router installations while it assesses what went wrong — a newly installed router at a radio site in southern Arkansas appears to have interfered with pilot-controller communications. Apple's own internal update knocked out the App Store, iCloud sign-in, Apple Pay and retail systems on Tuesday.

Keep the caveats in view: the Sun's account rests on three unnamed sources, and the official statements say "software bugs" and "routine maintenance." @krishnanrohit, building on @AndyMasley's data, cautions that failures are "at highs, but not unprecedented highs" — some of this is recency bias. Worth tracking who confirms the AI-patching explanation on the record.

The models behind it are getting more capable at exactly this work: OpenAI says GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol "mark another significant jump in cyber capabilities" and again meet the "Critical" cyber threshold in its Preparedness Framework, launched "with tighter safeguards for higher-risk cybersecurity assistance." Vetted defenders get looser access through Daybreak — NATO's NCIA uses it at up to 300 billion tokens a month, and ENISA used OpenAI models to find four vulnerabilities in software used across EU institutions, all now fixed. The week the "AI finds bugs" story turned into failures people could see — flights, bank logins, the NSA's own network — is the week patching speed stopped being an ops nicety and became a security primitive.

GPT-6.2 Astra and Sol: efficiency is the new frontier

X — @OpenAI · Nov 17

OpenAI's new models win on cost per finished task, not per token — but early testers are split on whether they're actually pleasant to work with.

OpenAI launched two models on Tuesday: Astra, the frontier model "for your hardest projects," and Sol, the "efficient, versatile model for everyday work." The pitch is not raw capability but efficiency — better scores at a fraction of the cost per task. On Agents' Last Exam, Astra beats GPT-6 Astra by 5.2 points "at less than a third of the cost" and Claude Fable 5.2 by 5.5 points "at just 5% of the cost." On FrontierCode 2.0, Sol lands within 0.5 points of Astra at about a fifth of the cost. Astra is 20% cheaper per token than GPT-6 Astra ($8 in / $40 out per million tokens).

The concept to learn: price per token vs. cost per task. A model can cost more per token and still be cheaper per job if it needs fewer tokens and fewer retries — which is why OpenAI quotes "fewer output tokens" and "% of the cost" rather than per-token prices. @swyx calls efficiency "one of the three most impt RL objectives rn." Independent numbers back the direction: Mercor's OpenWorldEngineer has Astra at 67.2% vs. Fable 5.2 at 54.6%.

The early verdict is split, and the split is instructive. @emollick found Astra "very uneven & weird," "harder to steer & work with than other models" — though when given an ambitious project it "could deliver well" — and says "I don't find myself reaching for the new 6.2 models." @swyx, by contrast, calls it "the best workflow automation model I've used" and runs much of his publishing pipeline on it. Sam Altman himself concedes "we have more work ahead of us on making the models nice to work with." @thorstenball (Amp) is not switching his staff pick from Opus 5.5, quoting the view that "the biggest gap between OpenAI and Anthropic models is their ability to make decisions on behalf of the user."

Two safety designations matter for anyone building on these models: both meet "Critical" for cyber and "High" for bio/chem, and Astra is OpenAI's first externally released model to meet the "High" threshold for AI self-improvement — defined as equivalent to "giving every OpenAI researcher an additional, highly capable research engineer." Mitigations include stronger research-environment containment and a "Control Room" system that "can halt further model output when intervention is needed." Meanwhile Alibaba launched Qwen4-Max, which it says rivals Anthropic and OpenAI, and Microsoft released MAI-Code-2 and MAI-Cyber-2-Flash. The frontier is no longer just about who is smartest — it is about who is cheapest per finished job, and who is easiest to steer.

Fold your subagents back into one loop

X — @emollick; Anthropic · Nov 17

Anthropic's case: one agent loop with skills and tool search beats subagent pyramids on quality, cost and latency — and the failure mode is Conway's law.

Anthropic's own guide to building agents, amplified by @emollick on Tuesday, is a direct challenge to how most companies are building them: fold most subagents back into one agent loop. Across "several enterprise deployments, a single agent with skills frequently outperformed both the one-prompt-for-everything design and the subagent design on quality, and often at a lower cost and latency per task." One customer measured roughly 2x faster runtime with a single loop.

The failure mode is Conway's law. Each team ships its own subagent, so the agent's architecture "becomes a copy of their org chart" — lossy context handoffs, "hundreds of overlapping subagents," the "Frankenprompt" (each team adds a sentence, so rules conflict), and local evals nobody owns. The replacement is progressive disclosure: the agent sees short descriptions of skills (procedures) and tools, and loads the full text only when needed — "tool search." That removes the main reason to split agents, which was context bloat. Anthropic says it "reduced Claude Code's system prompt for more advanced models by over 80%."

The target design: one model, one context window, an open-ended tool loop, with hard limits (turn and cost budgets, approval or "write" gates, validation) living in the harness and tools, and procedures living in skills. "One clear system prompt, 4-5 thin data tools, and firm output guardrails" covers most proposed designs. Subagents are still right for measurable context-bloat degradation, parallel work in fresh disposable contexts, or a single agent struggling with tool choice — and for verification (generator-verifier). If you split, split by context, not problem type, or you get a "telephone game."

How to migrate: build a shared end-to-end eval first — "Even a set of 10 golden examples is enough to start" — track routing accuracy while the orchestrator exists, then merge the most-used, most-chained subagents first. The dissent is worth hearing: @mareni_musashi calls the advice "nonsense" from experience — one agent "loses important focus in the context switching and often starts applying clearly defined rules" from the wrong domain — and @rockatanescu backs fresh-context specialist agents for security review. @thorstenball adds a related warning from the Amp meetup: "Elaborate pyramids of skills that are invoked deterministically by hand, by a human, seems like an anti-pattern" — skills should be discoverable by the model, not slash-commanded. Also launched the same day: experimental Code Mode for Claude Code, where Claude writes scripts chaining your tools together (opt in with CLAUDE_CODE_EXPERIMENTAL_CODE_MODE=1). For a tech lead running an agent platform, the question this raises is concrete: audit your harness for org-chart-shaped subagent sprawl, and check whether your evals exist before you merge anything.

Build durable, secure long-running agents

Decoding AI Magazine — Paul Iusztin · Nov 17

CodeClimb's recipe for agents you can leave running overnight: guardrails in code, not prompts; a metering proxy holding the keys; Inngest checkpoints; and a pause that costs nothing.

What would it take to trust an AI agent to work on your code overnight — without burning through API credits to reach its goal no matter what, exposing your credentials, or losing hours of progress when something crashes at 5 AM? Paul Iusztin's answer, built as CodeClimb (an overnight, eval-driven code search inspired by Karpathy's autoresearch), is a system design in four parts, and it is directly applicable to any long-running agent you ship.

First, guardrails the agent can't override. Don't put spend limits in the prompt — put them in a deterministic orchestrator. A TypeScript layer between the sandboxes checks state between rounds with plain if-else statements and ends a night on five conditions: metric thresholds reached, iteration cap (26 rounds), deadline (24h), night budget ($120), or plateau (9 rounds with no improvement). "For guardrails, deterministic code wins all the time." A monitor runs alongside each experiment and checks spend and time before every model call; an experiment is capped at $2.50 and 30 minutes.

Second, a proxy for credentials and allowed models. Route every model call through a metering proxy that holds the real credentials and acts as a firewall for which models a request may reach. The sandbox sees what looks like an OpenAI endpoint with a one-off token, valid for one session, carrying a dollar cap, revoked when the run ends. A request for any model not on the allow list is refused — so the agent can't quietly upgrade itself to a pricier model "for the greater good." The Anthropic key gets the same protection differently: Pi runs on the host, so its credentials never enter the sandbox.

Third, checkpoints that survive a crash. The night run becomes an Inngest function with one child function per experiment and evaluation; once a step finishes, its result is saved, and after a crash the function replays, skips what's done, and continues. Iusztin killed the worker mid-round and cut his internet for 30 seconds — both times the run resumed. Every experiment is also one commit in a local bare git repo, so any round can be checked out, diffed, or used as a seed.

Fourth, a human in the loop. When credits run out, Inngest's step.waitForEvent() pauses the night for up to 3 days until a night.resume event arrives — while it waits, the run costs nothing. The winning night: 26 rounds, 11.8 hours, $74.63, with a crash costing at most one $2.87 round, never all 26. The honest verdict: the durable, secure part works really well; the GraphRAG code itself got close to its node-extraction threshold while edge extraction stayed far off — and the biggest bottleneck was the quality of the dataset. "Look at your data!"

Trump's clampdown on the China chip loophole puts billions in loans at risk

Bloomberg; Reuters · Nov 17

Licenses now required for advanced chips to five countries and for overseas data centers renting compute to Chinese firms — and the banks that funded Southeast Asia's data-center boom are exposed.

The Trump administration moved on Monday to close the loophole that let Chinese companies use America's most advanced AI chips without buying them — by renting compute from data centers in Southeast Asia and elsewhere. The Commerce Department rule requires licenses for covered advanced-chip shipments to Singapore, Malaysia, Indonesia, Thailand and Brazil, and requires overseas data centers that supply "significant volumes of computing power" to firms headquartered in China, Russia or Iran to obtain US export licenses within 90 days. AI read my entire field — and found 872 verified errors

X — @CJHandmer · Nov 17

Claude recomputed 158,059 astrophysics papers and found 872 verified errors — peer review catches about one in five, and copied errors outnumber criticisms five to one.

Casey Handmer had Claude audit his entire field. Seeded from Caltech's TAPIR citation graph of 158,059 astrophysics papers, he screened 50,269 full texts cheaply, then gave each of the 200 most-cited papers an AI reviewer that "recomputes everything it could," blind to errata. A second reviewer upheld 56 errors; 13 had been fixed between preprint and journal, 56 went through — so "peer review removes about one error in five." Extending to 459 more papers brought the total to 872 verified errors. Copied errors outnumbered printed criticisms about five to one (367 papers copied an error, 68 criticised one), and half of the copied errors are still being copied seven years later.

The cost: about $2,720 at list price — roughly $3 per verified error — 4.9 billion tokens, and 44 hours of his time. His own caveats matter: "No person has checked any of them," and a third AI pass upheld 33 of 40 random samples. The replies asked the right question — "Who audits the audit?" — and made the sharper point: "Finding errors got cheap; getting the fix to the next citer did not." His tweet is far bolder than his site ("Academic publishing is dead"; ">1000x" speedup); the site's number is 872. Read the site, not the tweet — but the direction of travel is unmistakable: literature review at machine scale is now a $3-per-error operation, and the bottleneck moves from finding mistakes to propagating corrections.

Harness adapters: safe RL for efficient agentic development

Hugging Face · Nov 17

A small LoRA trained inside the pi harness took Qwen3.8-27B from 29.4% to 62.7% on SWE-bench Pro — tune the model to your harness, don't just buy a bigger one.

Hugging Face's RL method for tuning an open model to one specific agent harness. Train a small LoRA (rank 32) per model × harness with GRPO inside the unmodified harness, using exactly the tokens the harness saw; reward is the task verifier plus a small efficiency bonus for fewer tool calls. Result on Qwen3.8-27B inside the pi coding agent: SWE-bench Pro Hard-51 pass@1 from 29.4% to 62.7%, Terminal-Bench 2.1 from 39.5% to 55.8%, with 55–62% fewer tool calls per task. Takeaway: the "open models lose to frontier" argument assumes off-the-shelf use; a cheap, harness-specific tune is the counter-move, and it is directly applicable to any internal agent you run on open weights.

Energy-Based Reasoners

arXiv — via X — @AlexiGlad · Nov 17

A learned energy function gives looped reasoners a stopping signal — 345× less inference compute on Sudoku-Extreme, with 1.8M parameters.

A new arXiv paper (2611.08977) from a small team, amplified by @ylecun: looped reasoning models (TRM/HRM-style) repeat a recurrent block for more effective depth, but have "no explicit measure of progress" — they don't know when to stop or which direction improves the answer. Energy-Based Reasoners learn an energy function over latent reasoning states; each loop step is gradient descent on that energy, low energy means a good state, and a small gradient norm means stop (threshold 0.2). Results on small algorithmic benchmarks: 99.9% on Sudoku-Extreme "using 345× less inference compute than the next-best model," 99.4% single-sample, and 87.4% on Maze-Hard with just 1.8M parameters. The honest read: these are puzzles, not production workloads, and the replies rightly ask whether it scales beyond them. What to watch is the idea itself — a learned stopping signal for test-time compute. If your agents burn tokens on loops that don't know when they're done, this is the direction to track. Code: github.com/wwillchen/ebr.
