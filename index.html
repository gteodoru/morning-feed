Anthropic donates Agent Skills to the Agentic AI Foundation

X — @ClaudeDevs · Oct 22

The SKILL.md format joins MCP under the Linux Foundation — 70+ platforms, ~1M skills, ~280M installs — and the agent stack keeps consolidating into vendor-neutral standards.

A skill is a folder with a SKILL.md file (YAML front matter with name and description, then instructions) plus optional scripts, references and assets. Agents load it in stages to save context: at startup they read only each skill's name and description; when a task matches, they load the full SKILL.md; only then do they open extra files or run scripts. It's how you package "how we do X here" once and reuse it across agents. Boris Cherny: it "started as an experiment in Claude Code."

Anthropic transferred the name, logo, GitHub org and agentskills.io to the Agentic AI Foundation — the Linux Foundation project that already hosts MCP. The lead maintainers stay (David Soria Parra and Mahesh Murag), licenses stay Apache 2.0 / CC BY 4.0, so existing skills keep working. The roadmap: versioned spec releases, a formal proposal process, and security guidance for skill authors. Two spec details matter now: a `compatibility` field declaring what a skill needs, and an `allowed-tools` field (which tools a skill may call) that's still experimental and behaves differently across products.

AAIF announced four projects at AGNTCon+MCPCon: Agent Skills, OpenAI's Open Responses, Hugging Face's OpenEnv and Alibaba's OpenSandbox — 10 projects total. The agent stack is consolidating the way the web did.

The camps: governance ("MCP showed an open registry without curation becomes a graveyard of half-broken servers"; "prompts are the asset — govern them like code"), permissions ("a reusable skill shouldn't automatically inherit access to every tool"), and craft advice — the sharpest reply: don't ask the agent to draft skills (it "restates what the model already knows"); instead write down "the decisions and edge cases only you know," then let the agent format it.

Replay your agent's bug, fix it, add it to your AI evals

Decoding AI Magazine · Oct 23

Paul Iusztin's free course lesson: sample production traces, cluster failures, replay them with identical tool outputs — and grow regression tests via error analysis.

You tweak one line in your agent's system prompt. Did you just break something that worked last week? Even with observability and AI evals, reproducing the exact context of a failure is extremely hard. The fix: replays coupled with evals — sample production traces, group the failures, replay them with the exact same tool outputs.

The lesson walks through the error-analysis framework (popularized by Hamel Husain and Andrew Ng): record real runs, hand-label a sample pass/fail with a short critique, fix the obvious, cluster the rest by failure type, rank by frequency × severity, and write one cheap evaluator per cluster. Regression tests — not benchmarks or monitoring — are "the most important part of AI evals for a custom AI app."

The tooling: Kitaru (by ZenML Labs) captures agent runs and replays them with the same context by caching tool outputs — same input returns the same output; changed tools run from scratch. Sessions flow into a control plane; your coding agent does the analysis through Kitaru's MCP server and skills; every cohort becomes an experiment that gates later changes. Anthropic's field guide sets the entry bar low: "20-50 simple tasks drawn from real failures is a great start." And: always prioritize code checks over LLM judges — "a judge is always the last resort."

The repo is the full free course: 8 articles, 6 videos, 1 codebase building a Claude Code clone from scratch — this lesson is "AI Evals on Steroids via Replays."

Science, creativity, and the uncomfortable question of who gets to be a reviewer when the reviewer is a model.

Claude Opus 4.8 beat the best human peer reviewers — and that's the problem

X — @emollick · Oct 23

In a Harvard study, AI reviews beat paid top-rated human reviews on 124 social-science papers: 132/132 by an AI judge, 87% by blinded former journal editors.

The design is serious: every eligible submission to rapidPeer, Harvard's double-blind pre-submission review service — 124 papers, 134 human reviews from 2019–2026, written by well-paid experts, rated high quality by 88% of authors. For each paper, Claude Opus 4.8 at max reasoning wrote a review in two conditions: "matched" (no tools) and "tuned" (agent prompt + web search, ~12.4 searches per review). A different model family, GPT-5.5, judged each pair three ways, in both presentation orders, counting only unanimous wins. Three former journal editors also judged 10 blinded pairs.

Results: AI won 132 of 132 decided pairs by the AI judge and 87% by the human editors — 92% in theory, framing and communication; 99% in figures; 100% in sources, weaknesses and methods. The AI made no fabricated citations; 5.5% of its claims were judged incorrect vs 15.9% for humans. Weakness: without web access, "matched" AI won only 62% on sources.

The authors' real argument is the danger: AI pushes everyone toward "the conventional, orthodox, and majoritarian," making science "more homogenous and ordinary" — and reviewers' disagreements were partly a feature. Policy options range from requiring authors to iterate with a journal's published AI review prompt before submitting, to an editor-led process (author dossier + private AI review + human review only for hard cases) cutting decisions "within days rather than months." They also cite ICML 2026: 22.5% of reviewers told not to use AI reported using it anyway.

The camps: accountability ("a model with no reputation at stake has no reason to be the one who says no"), what was actually measured ("did it catch the broken stats, or just write nicer reviews?" — the paper concedes it didn't measure whether papers got better), and volume ("peer review was never designed to scale with the volume AI will now produce"). Jokes: "Reviewer 2 has been automated."

AI for science week: an open model that reads DNA, and 5M new gene families

X — the AI lists · Oct 22

Carbon-X (14.8B, Apache 2.0) nearly matches GPT-6.1 Sol on DNA benchmarks at ~1/1,800 the cost; Arc's COMPASS clustered 376M stretches of bacterial "dark matter" DNA.

Carbon-X, from Hugging Face (fine-tuned from Qwen3-14B), takes raw DNA inside a normal chat and answers in English: is this mutation benign or pathogenic, enhancer or promoter, what organism group, what comes next. DNA and text share one token stream (DNA cut into 6-letter "words"). Training: ~10B tokens with the body frozen to teach DNA embeddings, then 302B tokens mid-training, then fine-tuning and RL with verifiable rewards. Results: 66.7% average on five DNA benchmarks vs GPT-6.1 Sol's 67.5% and Opus 5.5's 60.9%. Their estimate: scoring all 2.4M uncertain variants in ClinVar would cost ~$300 vs $32K–$4M with frontier models. It runs on one 80GB GPU — sensitive sequences stay local — and ships with a Claude Code / Codex skill. Not clinically validated.

The launch also surfaced a policy fight: Clem Delangue and Thom Wolf used it to complain that frontier labs' "widespread but shallow" bio guardrails block scientists while "the bad guys are bypassing easily" — Wolf's assistant hit a bio safety filter summarizing his own team's notes on the model.

Meanwhile Arc Institute's COMPASS (Patrick Hsu's lab) fine-tuned the Evo 2 genome model on the DNA between bacterial genes, sorted 376M intergenic regions (1.3 terabases) into 5.14M clusters without alignment, and flagged 20,149 clusters of likely structured RNAs with candidate protein partners. Repeating patterns in token entropies recovered CRISPR, TIGR and VIPR arrays from sequence alone; teams of AI agents mined the database for new riboswitches and ribozymes.
