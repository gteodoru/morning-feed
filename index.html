Stripe's t-shirt bench: 21 agent runs, 7 working shops — and the dangerous failures were silent

Breaking Change — Michelle Bu (via X — @edwinarbus) · Sep 16

Seven models were asked to build, deploy, and fulfill a real t-shirt store. The demos looked green; the API handoffs weren't.

Michelle Bu's "Building a t-shirt factory factory" (surfaced by Stripe's Edwin Arbus, reposted by Boris Cherny) put seven models to a deceptively simple test: build and deploy a real store with payment plus print/fulfillment, design a printable shirt, and report how to test it. After early hosting and print-signup failures, a sandbox was added. Across 21 runs, only 7 produced functional shops; every functional shop integrated Stripe; 5 of the 7 were Claude; Fable and Opus produced the two selected for production. Scoring covered integration correctness, design correctness, and end-to-end testing — not screenshots.

Why it matters: the dangerous defects are silent — wrong API wiring, mishandled "created with issues" states, unsupported or end-of-life frameworks that still look green. The builder takeaway: sandboxes plus executable acceptance tests for every external integration. Score deploy → pay → fulfill → verify. Version and policy metadata plus explicit failure-state tests are non-optional guardrails.

TypeSafe's Jev: a frontier model that can't chat — and that's the point

RuntimeWire (via X — @chipro, @emollick) · Sep 16

Diogo Almeida's new "System One" model returns typed decisions, not prose — 20–200× faster, 40–400× cheaper, and incapable of hallucinating.

TypeSafe AI emerged from stealth with roughly $40M led by DCVC. Co-founder and CEO Diogo Almeida co-authored OpenAI's 2022 InstructGPT paper and appears in the original ChatGPT credits — and his thesis is a critique of the path that work established: models optimized to produce responses people prefer still need supervision when their output controls software. Jev, the first "System One" model (borrowing Kahneman's fast thinking), uses a new architecture, a parallel sampler, and a training method called Reinforcement Learning for Calibrated Decisions (RLCD). Developers send a block of state plus typed questions; Jev returns choices, scores, or probabilities — no prose, no code, no open-ended strings.

The claimed numbers: similar intelligence on "System One" tasks versus existing LLMs at two orders of magnitude faster and cheaper — around 10 calls/sec at roughly $7/hour, with output tokens free. Chip Huyen's angle: fixed-vocabulary outputs make it a first-class component for data labeling, routing, and fixed-action loops — useful inside systems, not as a chat replacement. The takeaway for builders: not everything needs a chat LLM. Constrained classifiers with predictable schemas and calibrated confidence scores are the architecture for agent subsystems where you want cost floors and testability.

"Slop grenades": Tobi Lütke names AI's new lazy-work problem

X — @shaneparrish · Sep 16

AI turned lazy work from too little output into too much unchecked output — and someone else always has to clean it up.

Shane Parrish (~479K views, reposted by Peter Steinberger) quotes a long conversation with Shopify CEO Tobi Lütke covering River, learning by "osmosis," AI dreaming, strategy, and what AI still can't do. Lütke's phrase — the "slop grenade" — is passing unchecked AI-generated work along so someone else catches the mistakes and cleans up. The definition camp in the replies frames it as a wall of unreviewed AI text where one human sentence would do.

The org-design read: generation got cheap, verification didn't. Teams adopting AI agents should treat review capacity — not model access — as the binding constraint, and reward the one human sentence over the wall of text.

SK Hynix in talks with Intel to make memory chips on US soil for the first time

Reuters (+ Bloomberg — Markets Daily) · Sep 16

Lease part of Intel's Ohio fab, or a venture with Intel and cloud giants — as AI's memory shortage turns HBM supply into geopolitics.

Three sources tell Reuters SK Hynix is in talks with Intel about manufacturing memory chips on US soil for the first time — either leasing part of Intel's long-planned Ohio facility or forming a venture with Intel and major cloud firms keen to lock in supply. Intel jumped 5–6% in premarket trading; SK Hynix rose about 4% in Seoul. SK Hynix played the report down ("went way ahead"), and Seoul could oppose any deal involving sensitive HBM or DRAM technology.

Why it matters for AI: SK Hynix is the leading high-bandwidth-memory supplier (about 50% revenue share per Counterpoint) — the stacked memory inside AI accelerators — and relentless AI and data-center investment has created an acute memory shortage. A deal would relieve pressure on struggling Intel and hand the Trump administration a reshoring win. Memory is now the binding constraint on the AI buildout, and its supply chain is being negotiated like oil.

OpenAI's Neon: a materials model that beats the frontier at its own lab game

X — @LiamFedus · Sep 16

High-throughput labs plus 1,300 H200s: an open materials-science model mid-trained and RL'd past GPT-6 Astra on lab analysis — XRD success 2.7% → 55.3%.

Liam Fedus announced Neon: high-throughput materials labs in Menlo Park where experiments generate data, models learn, and the loop guides the next experiment. With 1,300 H200s and months of lab data, the open model Neon was mid-trained and RL'd to surpass GPT-6 Astra on a materials-analysis benchmark — measured XRD success on 134 difficult samples jumped from 2.7% to 55.3%. The thread details a three-part recipe (labs + research + infra) and striking infra numbers: 4.1× training throughput, 2.5× faster decoding, 95%+ utilization. Targets: superconductors, magnets, semiconductors. (~1.1M views, reposted by Marc Andreessen.)

The builder takeaway: closed-loop experiment factories plus domain mid-training can beat general frontier models on specialized analysis — if you own the data flywheel and keep the GPUs saturated.
