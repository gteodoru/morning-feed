OpenAI measures its own research acceleration — and the frontier is jagged

OpenAI · Nov 18

Agents now finish 46% of 2–4-hour research tasks alone — but only 25% of inference-code work. The frontier is jagged, and OpenAI wants every lab to publish numbers like these.

From March to October, estimated unassisted success rose from 23% to 46% on tasks a human would need 2–4 hours for, and from 13% to 35% on 4–8-hour tasks. But the kind of work matters enormously. By task type, agents succeeded alone 79% of the time monitoring training runs and 72% answering technical questions — but only 35% launching training runs, 29% building eval and RL environments, and 25% writing inference and serving code. OpenAI's own word for this is "jagged": humans stay in the loop precisely where the work is load-bearing.

The spending numbers are just as striking. At API list prices, the median researcher now burns $2,400 a day on agent use (up 62% from September); the 90th percentile hits $23,000 a day. Agent workdays per researcher rose elevenfold, from 0.5 to 5.7 a day. In September, the API-equivalent value of agent use was about twice the research org's non-equity compensation — yet internal deployment is only 2% of total research compute. Asked what they would trade to keep frontier coding agents for three months, the median researcher said 30% of team compute or, separately, 40% of team headcount.

Two more things worth noting. Documented Safety & Alignment workweeks rose 77% from January to September — the fastest of any area, with the jump coming after the Hugging Face incident in July. And OpenAI argues labs "should be required to track and publicly report indicators of recursive self-improvement" — the process by which AI accelerates the development of future AI systems. The caveat, stated plainly: this is self-measurement with self-reported value, and "success" is OpenAI's own grading. Even so, @NeelNanda5 called it a raise in "the standards for transparency among frontier labs."

Codex found a pre-auth RCE in Ruby on Rails — patch now

Durendal · Nov 19

Codex found a universal pre-auth RCE in Ruby on Rails — a 1-byte heap overflow triggered by uppercasing "ß". Patch to Ruby 3.3.14 / 3.4.13 / 4.0.10 now.

On October 23, Moritz Sanft asked Codex to map CRuby's pre-auth attack surface reachable from Rails, then triaged what it returned. Within 24 hours Codex had found a handful of bugs; two lead to pre-auth remote code execution in many Rails apps, "including GitHub and GitLab." A plain `rails new` app is exploitable.

Bug 1 (GHSA-34m4-m9fh-gqp5): in ISO-8859-x and Windows-125x encodings, which have no capital sharp s, uppercasing "ß" writes "SS" — two bytes — while the loop only checks that one destination byte remains. The result is a 1-byte heap out-of-bounds write, reachable through Rack when a multipart field named _method arrives with charset=ISO-8859-1. Bug 2 (GHSA-87fm-7w84-j2px): rb_str_casemap sums lengths into an int, and U+0390 is 2 bytes in but 6 bytes out when uppercased, so a long string overflows into a 2-byte allocation with ~4GB written. It only affects Rack ≤3.2.1.

The timeline: a toy exploit on October 26, OpenAI's Sylvie achieving RCE on a plain `rails new` app by November 5, coordinated disclosure with WAF vendors, and fixes shipped November 18 as Ruby 3.3.14, 3.4.13 and 4.0.10. The lesson travels further than Rails: string case-mapping is a classic "output can be longer than input" trap, and AI is now finding interpreter-level bugs in mature runtimes. Patch now.

Ship agent changes safely in 5 steps: evals from your own failures

Decoding AI Magazine · Nov 19

The scariest AI failures are the silent ones. Build your evals from your own failures: pull 20–50 real traces, mark pass/fail, cluster, and replay every failure before you ship.

Paul Iusztin starts from the fear every agent team knows: you change a prompt on a branch, nothing errors, nothing complains — did you just break something that worked yesterday? The scariest AI failures are the silent ones, and the fix is to build evals from your own failures.

The method: pull 20–50 real traces and have one domain expert mark each Pass or Fail, with a short critique of the first thing that went wrong. Cluster the failures, then rank every cluster by frequency and severity on a four-quadrant matrix. Your dataset is the secret weapon, not your system prompt. The full post sits behind a paywall, but the method above is the readable core — and it is directly applicable to the support-triage agent: collect real failure traces first, label them, and replay every failure before a prompt change ships.

What comes after AGI? DeepMind's four roads to superintelligence

DeepMind Institute · Nov 19

Tim Genewein's map of the road from AGI to superintelligence: four pathways, one warning — restricting training compute won't stop progress, it creates an overhang.

Tim Genewein's essay, distilled from the technical report "From AGI to ASI" (co-authored with Shane Legg and others), defines superintelligence as a system that consistently outperforms large groups of human experts on tasks that would take them years, across virtually all tasks — not narrow ones like Go or protein folding. Intelligence is a continuum, and AGI is one point on it.

The core argument is arithmetic, not prophecy. Three compounding trends — compute per dollar ~1.5x a year, algorithmic efficiency ~3–6x a year for training (~3x for inference), AI hardware spend ~2.5x a year — multiply into effective compute growing at least 10x a year. A $1 billion cognitive workload today would cost ~$1,000 in ten years. Epoch finds the price of a fixed benchmark score has fallen more than 13x a year since 2023. Even if capability froze, sheer number and speed of agents could bridge AGI to "weak ASI."

Four pathways: scaling (limited by the data wall, multiplicative compute cost, power, fabs and interconnect); paradigm shifts (today's models lag humans on continual learning, world modeling, sample efficiency); agent coordination (we need "multi-agent scaling laws"; swarms already show cheating and whistleblowing); and recursive self-improvement (fastest where verification is automatic, like math proofs). The policy warning: restricting one input, like training compute, won't stop progress — it can create an "overhang," a sudden jump when the restriction lifts. The critics' camp, worth hearing: a good map that is silent on the social question — governance, and what ASI does to society.

Norms emerged without anyone programming them in: public retractions before publication, negative results reported about as often as positive ones, and an agent-run "Journal of Agent Research" whose editors inspected code.

In a blinded comparison against recent human preprints, agents rated higher on rigor and measured claims; humans rated higher on novelty and significance. The takeaway, and it rhymes with the OpenAI report: when agents produce science at scale, auditing becomes the job.
