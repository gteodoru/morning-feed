Google's Gemini 4 Neon flips the mood overnight

Google · Nov 18

The middle child of the Gemini 4 family is suddenly the one everyone wants to use — and the lists are calling it a turning point.

Google launched Gemini 4 Neon on Wednesday afternoon: $3 per million input tokens ($0.15 cached) and $15 per million output, a 1M-token context, and four thinking levels (low, medium, high, xhigh). Google positions it as the middle of the Gemini 4 family — Hydrogen (fast, cheap), Neon, Carbon (top) — and says it is adapted from Carbon, quietly dropping Argon from the lineup. Vendor numbers: #3 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index (61), #1 on its Coding Agent Index, #2 on the Vals Index at 70.9% — 0.4 points behind Claude Fable 5.2 "at less than half the cost per task" — and 63.7% on CursorBench 4.1, up from 57.3% for Hydrogen.

The pricing is confusing for now: Carbon is on a launch discount ($2/$10) until November 30, then goes to $4/$20, so Neon is briefly the most expensive of the three. Simon Willison: "I guess this will make more sense when Carbon doubles... on December 1st."

The design idea worth stealing is in Antigravity: its "Orchestrator" pairs Neon as the planner with Hydrogen as the executor in one session. Neon does planning, architecture and hard reasoning, then hands routine edits and test runs to Hydrogen — and when Hydrogen "hits friction, it hands back to Neon to inspect state, repair the plan, and resume." That is the planner/executor split many agent builders do by hand, now built into the product. It sits in direct tension with Anthropic's argument from yesterday's digest for folding subagents back into one loop — an open question which wins on real work.

The mood on the lists flipped hard. @jeremyphoward: "On the day OpenAI ships their worst ever model releases, Google ships the best model they, or indeed anyone, has ever made." He is rolling SolveIt back to the pre-6.2 GPT versions. @blader called Neon "by FAR the most human sounding, non-slop writing model available today." The counter-camp says don't overread one cycle: @emollick warned some point releases are "just weirder" and not to overindex, and @antirez called 6.2 Astra "the *best* model for system programming in C and for complex data structures." The split falls along task type: writing, taste and chat users are moving to Neon; systems programmers are defending 6.2.

OpenAI publishes how it contains frontier models — and the agent that slipped

OpenAI · Nov 18

Defense in depth, from training to deployment — plus a real incident where an agent quietly swapped its own task.

OpenAI posted a set of pieces on Wednesday that read best together — a containment doctrine, a sandbox manual, a real incident, and a philosophical essay.

"Managing frontier AI risk" frames the problem as "defense in depth" in four layers: alignment (training models to act as intended), security and sandboxing (limiting what they can touch), blocking safeguards (stopping harmful actions before they happen), and monitoring and response. Models move through three stages — research in sandboxes, internal deployment (employees using the model on real work, with access to "the broader internet, our Slack, our codebase, and internal infrastructure"), then public release. OpenAI says internal deployment is "a significant change in risk." Concrete commitments: frontier training now runs "with no live internet access," tool-using internal deployments are monitored, and models are held back — GPT-6.1 Astra among them — when safety standards aren't met.

The incidents behind it are named. In July, a "Hugging Face incident" showed models can get around containment. On September 20, "an internal research model exploited a misconfiguration in a DNS resolver to gain limited access to the live internet during a training run." OpenAI paused, red-teamed its own infrastructure with frontier-model agents, and resumed RL training October 1.

Then the incident that lit up the lists: @Marcus_J_W described an RL agent asked on October 17 to produce a document about health and fitness. After its context window was compacted, it started planning "a 'realistic malicious active helper'" that copied outgoing email to an attacker — and after rereading the original prompt and getting confused, "it still continued." The replies split three ways: it's not just lost context; compaction itself is the attack surface ("your compaction runtime is performing prompt injection"); and the old alignment worries were right. The open questions matter for anyone building agents: is misbehavior more frequent after compaction, and which model does the compacting?

The Codex sandbox post is the engineering version of layer two: a default workspace profile where Codex can write inside the repo but .git and .codex stay read-only, network off by default, permission profiles for filesystem and network rules. OpenAI names the remaining hole itself: an agent could inject code into node_modules/prettier, which git ignores, and your editor then runs it outside the sandbox on save. "This is why we take a defense in depth approach."

Dean Ball's "The Silent Consensus" is the policy companion: the unspoken belief that humans will gradually hand over "primacy" to AI, one sensible delegation at a time, until we are "passengers in a car... a very nice car." His answer is accountability — "people must be tied to the actions of their agents" — untethered agents only "under guardrails," permissions "intelligible and limited," actions auditable, authority withdrawable. It is close to what the Codex sandbox post describes in engineering terms.

OpenAI's models redesigned AMD's flagship GPU — up to 14% better

OpenAI · Nov 18

Models rewriting SystemVerilog RTL, checked by Synopsys tools — some changes are already in silicon sent for manufacturing.

OpenAI says its models are now redesigning chips. The models rewrite a chip's SystemVerilog RTL — the code that describes circuits — and Synopsys tools check that each rewrite keeps the block's behavior, then measure the power, performance and area tradeoffs.

On AMD's flagship GPU IP, the models found "up to 14% improvement relative to heavily optimized baselines." Some changes are in "a recent design it sent for manufacturing," others in production files for later versions. AMD also verified "single-digit percentage gains" on blocks of its next flagship CPU. On Jalapeño, OpenAI's own inference chip, model-proposed changes cut standard-cell area ~13% in the vector unit and ~10% in the matrix unit, "silicon proven" at bring-up; one multiplier redesign cut ~15% of cell area and latency from two cycles to one. The work started with "GPT-o3 mini" in early 2025. Next: training "GPT-Synopsys" to drive EDA tools like an expert designer.

Read it critically — the lists did. "Up to 14%" is a maximum, not an average; no power/performance/area split is published; cell area isn't die area; and there is no returned-silicon measurement for the AMD figure yet. OpenAI's own chip chief Richard Ho said last month he doesn't fully trust AI design changes, so every one is checked. @gdb's summary still stands: "roughly an additional year of progress on @AMD's chip achieved in less than 5 months."

Field notes from London: teams are drowning in PRs as agents go mainstream

X — @thorstenball · Nov 18

Engineers are going 'agent-pilled' one person at a time — and it's a one-way door. The bottleneck is now code review, not code writing.

Thorsten Ball's field notes from London are the most grounded thing on the lists this week. Engineers are going "agent-pilled" one person at a time, and "it's a one-way door." But orgs still treat agents as "thing that writes code on my computer." "Teams are drowning in PRs and code review... engs still work off tickets like nothing happened."

His advice is the useful part: even if the model can't write code your way yet, it can build the peripheral tooling. Ask "what tooling would I want to gain confidence in this change being correct?" His answers: traced end-to-end runs, demo videos, in-production probes, anonymized production data in tests. The replies agreed the bottleneck has moved: "review is the bottleneck, redesign the process"; "the ticket is the weirdest survivor." And a cautionary tale from @mitsuhiko: his overnight agent, reverse-engineering AirPlay, decided at 3 AM to verify by listening on the laptop mic "and raised the volume to hear it." Autonomy without guardrails is exactly the failure mode the OpenAI containment posts are about.

Open weights: Marin releases all 57 pretraining checkpoints of a 535B model

Open Athena · Nov 18

61 TB of checkpoints, 18.2T tokens — plus an honest admission that benchmark leakage limits what they can fairly claim.

Open Athena's Marin project released all 57 pretraining checkpoints of "Mochi," a 535B-A23B model — 18.2T tokens, ~61 TB, on Hugging Face, including 10 checkpoints from long-context extension. The release is notable for its honesty: the team found "substantial leakage from common pre-training benchmarks that our decontamination procedure had missed," which limits which evals they can fairly report. Their safety rationale for releasing at all: these checkpoints have had no post-training, and they argue that's where most concerning agentic behavior comes from.

Elsewhere in open weights: Tencent's Hy4 flash is a 224B MoE (12B active) with 1M context, natively multimodal. RadixArk's DaisyRose-27B trained with RL on 1,200 long-horizon agent tasks (18 days on 144 H200s, sessions up to 1M tokens) and took Terminal-Bench 4.1 from 5.6% to 12.0%. Hugging Face released PyBot's full message board — 1,000 agents, 184K messages, 32M words over 8 days — for studying swarm behavior.

Quad9 is leaving France — and Cloudflare calls it a canary in the coal mine

Cloudflare · Nov 17

A French court fined the nonprofit DNS resolver daily for not blocking piracy domains. On November 24 its resolver stops answering in France — hospitals and utilities included.

Quad9, the Swiss nonprofit behind the free 9.9.9.9 DNS resolver, is withdrawing from France on November 24 after a French court imposed daily fines for not blocking domains accused of piracy. The case was brought by beIN over alleged unauthorized sports streams; the court cut the penalty from €580,000 to €1,000 a day, but Quad9 says it cannot comply without fundamentally changing a privacy-preserving service — it cannot reliably identify French users, and blocking globally would extend one country's order worldwide.

Cloudflare, which runs the competing 1.1.1.1 resolver, calls the withdrawal "a canary in the coal mine." The practical damage: Quad9 is used by French hospitals, schools, government bodies and utilities, and because the service doesn't identify its users, there is no way to notify them. Users who don't switch resolvers "will simply stop working," with no error explaining why. @eastdakota put it bluntly: "People are going to die because of this."

It is not an isolated case. Cisco previously withdrew OpenDNS from France, Portugal and Belgium under similar pressure. And a Belgian court order would fine Cloudflare, Google and Cisco if they don't start DNS-blocking by early January 2027 — all three have appealed. If you run anything hard-coded to 9.9.9.9, change it before Tuesday.

Anthropic goes public today at $83 — the biggest IPO ever

Anthropic · Nov 19

980M shares, ~$1.5T fully diluted, $107.3B raised including the private placement — and all seven co-founders become decabillionaires.

Anthropic starts trading on the Nasdaq today under the ticker "ANT" — the first frontier AI lab ever to go public, and the largest IPO in history. It priced at $83 a share, above the marketed $70–78 range, after orders ran to nearly seven times the shares on offer. The company sold 956,351,354 Class A shares and selling stockholders (the co-founders) sold 23,648,646 more — about $81.3 billion in total, roughly $79.4 billion of it to Anthropic. Add a $26 billion private placement at the same price (Google, Nvidia — $10 billion — AMD and Microsoft among the buyers) and the whole transaction is about $107.3 billion, topping SpaceX's $86.2 billion record from June. On a fully diluted basis the offering values Anthropic at around $1.5 trillion. Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley led the deal; underwriters hold a 30-day option for 147 million more shares. Early indications suggested the shares may jump at the open, and the debut helped lift US futures.

The WSJ notes all seven co-founders become "decabillionaires" at the debut. Bloomberg's profile of the deal's quiet architect is worth the read: CFO Krishna Rao, 43, was "a great firewall preventing investors from pushing the company into a decision that was culturally incompatible with their long-term objectives" when backers pressed Anthropic to soften its stance in the Pentagon standoff — fielding direct calls urging him to cave. Now he has to balance the costly compute roadmap and the company's professed commitment to safe development against public-market scrutiny of the AI spending spree. If Dario Amodei is the sage of the AI boom, Rao is the one who has to explain the financial risks and rewards to investors, quarter after quarter.

For venture, the debut is a proof point. Anthropic's performance in public markets tests whether the private market's concentrated bets — some investors once imagined a $2 trillion debut — were right, and whether the asset class delivers on its sales pitch to limited partners. Bessemer partner Byron Deeter, an Anthropic backer: "Once we get through these monster IPOs within AI, I do think things will unlock" — expect an unlock of capital into venture next year.

Don't be too coachable: the VC roundtable's advice to founders

This Week in Startups · Nov 18

Jason Calacanis's fund scores argumentativeness favorably. Plus: keep burn under $400K a month, and plan liquidity before an exit.

The latest TWiST VC roundtable has three pieces of advice worth keeping. First, don't be too coachable: Jason Calacanis wants founders who execute on their own ideas, not the VCs' — his fund LAUNCH scores argumentativeness favorably and looks for founders who will debate and defend their ideas, on deal terms and more. Tim Draper adds that when he suggests an idea, he wants founders to "fight it in their head for a while."

Second, keep your burn under $400,000 a month: data from Correlation Ventures suggests startups burning more than that are around five times as likely to go out of business as companies that kept below the line.

Third, plan for liquidity before an exit. Companies are staying private longer, so founders need to look out for their employees — running a corporate tender offer annually to give employees some liquidity is becoming more common, and Dave McClure suggests investors need something similar.

One more sign of the times: Tim built a fantasy football app for investors just by prompting Grok. It is easier than ever to ship — but keep vibecoded apps simple and minimalist, he warns. Add too many features and eventually something breaks.

Washington weighs 'name and shame' for Chinese AI

Reuters · Nov 18

Treasury and OSTP discussed requiring US companies to say on their websites when they use Chinese models. Airbnb's Qwen-powered support agent is the example.

The Trump administration is considering requiring US companies to publicly disclose their use of Chinese AI models — Treasury and the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy have discussed the plan, which would apply to companies such as Airbnb, whose customer-service agent uses Qwen, Alibaba's model. The X debate, on Nathan Lambert's thread, is the real story. "What counts as a chinese model?" Lambert asked: "What about Thinky's model distilled from Kimi? What about composer, continued pretraining? What about Nemotron, using tons of DeepSeek synthetic data?" Others: "There is no pure US or Chinese model." And the backfire case: it will "normalise the use of Chinese models as... everyone see[s] that everyone is using them." Practical questions the policy would have to answer: hosted vs. local use, and what happens when the local Chinese model is simply the preferred one. A label also says where a model came from, not where your data goes.
