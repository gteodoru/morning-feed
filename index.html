Anthropic: fold your subagents back into one agent loop

Anthropic · Oct 24

Anthropic's Applied AI team says the 2025-era multi-agent orthodoxy is backwards: one agent with skills usually beats an orchestrator of specialists.

Across several enterprise deployments, Anthropic says a single agent with skills 'frequently outperformed both the one-prompt-for-everything design and the subagent design on quality, and often at a lower cost and latency per task.' One customer that compared its multi-agent harness against a single loop saw roughly 2x faster runtime; another found a dozen specialist subagents made a consistent on-brand voice hard to keep.

The diagnosis is Conway's law at work: each team builds its own agent, a platform team bolts an orchestrator on top, and the architecture becomes a copy of the org chart. The failure modes are familiar — details lost in handoffs, hundreds of overlapping subagents with no owner, a 'Frankenprompt' where every team added a sentence, local evals nobody trusts, and latency from every nested loop.

The fix is progressive disclosure: the agent sees only short descriptions of its skills (written procedures) and tools, and loads the full text only when it needs it. Anthropic says it cut Claude Code's system prompt for advanced models by over 80% this way, and that since Opus 4.5, models can manage far more in a single loop than most teams expected. Keep a subagent only for three cases: measurable context bloat, parallel work in throwaway context, or when one agent can't pick tools or has conflicting instructions. If you split, split by context, not by problem type — splitting by problem type creates a 'telephone game.'

The migration path is disciplined: build an end-to-end eval first ('even a set of 10 golden examples is enough'), merge specialists that chain on each other, fold the rest one at a time, and compare on which tasks flipped, not just the score. In Anthropic's own test, folding one subagent lowered latency but broke a process — and the break appeared in only one of two runs, so run each task more than once. The companion kit's own disclaimer is the honest summary: it 'does not assume a single loop always wins. It measures.'

Builders mostly agreed, with a real minority warning about context bloat: 'then watch the context bloat... the single agent start rewriting things it has already written.' The useful lesson isn't 'never use subagents.' It's that the reason most teams split agents — context limits and prompt bloat — has mostly gone away, while the cost (lossy handoffs, latency, ownership sprawl) hasn't.

bbtree: an LLM-written B-tree, and what keeps it correct

Xiangpeng Hao · Oct 24

A drop-in Rust replacement for BTreeMap that its author says is 5x faster — with every line written by LLMs and correctness resting on formal proofs.

bbtree is a Rust ordered map: single-threaded it replaces the standard library's BTreeMap/BTreeSet, and a concurrent version replaces skiplists, with zero dependencies. Author Xiangpeng Hao — who did his PhD on B-trees and co-wrote Bf-Tree, which runs in production at Microsoft — claims 5x faster than std's BTreeMap on one thread, 12x faster than crossbeam-skiplist at 96 threads, 30% less memory, and 5x lower p99 latency. Swapping only the map speeds real systems: fjall 2.47x, SlateDB 1.71x, Tonbo 10M-row writes 6.83x, Lance's MemWAL index 4.21x, Turso random-rowid INSERT 2.22x.

The design rests on a three-line cost model — chained cache misses (~100 ns each), instructions (~0.5 ns), cache-coherence traffic — and reads like a masterclass in cache-aware engineering: 16–32 item nodes scanned linearly, fixed-size 'inline keys' in the node so one cache line holds eight keys' prefixes, batched lookups with a custom allocation-free executor, hand-written AVX-512/NEON SIMD ('the compiler is not your friend'), and version locks so readers never write, hitting 180M ops/s at 96 threads.

The more interesting half is the correctness story, because 'all of the code is written by LLMs.' Three guards: Hao himself steering every design decision, cross-checking by LLMs from different providers, and machines — formal verification with Verus, model checking, deterministic simulation, differential fuzzing, Miri and sanitizers, all in CI. Skeptics on Hacker News called parts of the write-up 'AI slop'; Hao replied that a trait lets you add your own key types. The optimistic read, from @sh_reya: you need smart humans, good metrics and cost models, and 'super basic swarms of agents to write code and tests' — and systems PhD students should now 'build extremely ambitious systems.'

Open-source inference catches DeepSeek's own engine

vLLM · Oct 23

Days after DeepSeek open-sourced DeepInfer, vLLM and SGLang say they match it on DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash — with half the GPUs.

DeepInfer, the engine DeepSeek open-sourced this week for DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, is offline-only and supports one model. vLLM now says its decode throughput is within 6% of DeepInfer's published numbers at batch sizes 8–256, using 32 GB200s against DeepInfer's 64-way parallelism — half the GPUs. At rack scale, on one GB300 NVL72 with prefill and decode split, vLLM serves 954K tokens/s per GPU on SemiAnalysis's AgentX benchmark at $38.96 per GPU-hour at DeepSeek's off-peak prices. Three prefill changes stack for a 35.6–43.9% gain, and the model itself helps: its global KV cache is only 890 bytes per token.

SGLang says it matches on both stages: up to 1.04x prefill and up to 7.4% more decode throughput. Read the footnote before the headline, though: vLLM's own words are 'we do not read the gap between the two curves as an engine-to-engine speedup' — these are similar curves, not a head-to-head. The lineage runs both ways: 'DeepInfer was originally built on vLLM, and vLLM in turn has continuously integrated kernels from DeepSeek's libraries.' When a lab open-sources its kernels, the general engines absorb them within days — which shrinks any serving edge a lab gets from its in-house stack, and is exactly what is pulling open-weight token volume to record levels (see AI Venture).

Why AI prose got harder to read, measured

arXiv · Oct 24

Across nine frontier models, a new preprint measures the drift: more rare word pairs, inanimate subjects doing human things, stacked nouns — and shows a length penalty alone produces it.

The paper opens with a sentence built to sound like a modern model: 'Your instinct is right; the convoluted sentence you are currently reading is quietly eating its own reader-patience budget — footnote it.' Psycholinguistics has long linked each of its features to slower human reading, and the authors find all of them elevated in model output: all nine frontier and near-frontier models use more inanimate subjects with animate verbs and more low-frequency word pairs than humans, plus longer dependencies, more stacked nouns, and more object relatives with 'that' dropped — especially in multi-turn chat and coding.

The clever method: comparing model text not just with human text but with round-trip translations of the models' own sentences (English to Japanese and back), which keep meaning and normalize wording — so a feature that drops after translation is a stylistic choice the model made. Post-training drives it: within Olmo, the effects grow from base to instruct to reasoning model. And it can be trained back: Opus 5.5 is lower than Opus 5 on 19 of 21 difficulty measures.

The mechanism the authors propose: human language is shaped by pressure to be understood, while models are now heavily post-trained with verifiable rewards on math, code and reasoning, which reward the right answer, not clarity, under context limits that reward brevity. Their RL experiment on Qwen2.5-1.5B found any length penalty above zero drove every seed to a near-categorical preference for the shorter variant — and it leaked into constructions it was never trained on. The takeaway for anyone building agents that write to humans: readability is a dimension labs can and do optimize, and it belongs in your evals.

GPU economics: why old chips cost more and who gets squeezed

a16z · Oct 24

Rents are 9–143% above a 6-year depreciation line, open-weight tokens are 72% of OpenRouter volume — and the margin that matters is revenue per GPU minus rent.

Against a straight-line 6-year depreciation schedule — the accounting life hyperscalers use — current GPU rents sit 9–143% above where they 'should' be: H200 +143%, B200 +111%, RTX PRO 6000 +73%, H100 +54%, A100 +9%. April is when things 'really took off.' On the demand side, OpenRouter token volume rose ~27x since last November (~6T to ~165T tokens a week), and open-weight models went from ~28% to ~72% of it. a16z cautions OpenRouter 'captures only a small slice of the picture.'

Gavin Baker's correction to the 'crunch' debate: the spread isn't token price minus rent, it's revenue per GPU minus rent. Throughput per GPU keeps rising for the same model — Kimi K3 throughput per GPU is improving at a 37% annualized rate from engine optimizations alone, which is the vLLM/SGLang story above — so volume can outrun price cuts. And the mix matters: '$10 in Claude revenue requires $1 of inference compute. $10 of open-weight revenue requires $3 to $7,' because inference providers compete away the margin. So open-weight share gains are bullish for infrastructure and bad for model-layer margins, and vice versa.

Bloomberg Weekend's prediction lands on the same fault line: 'the era of buy everything AI is over,' with investors becoming 'more selective and demanding' and the tide turning on neoclouds. YC's Paper Club adds the structural fact underneath: 'for every FLOP spent training a model, roughly six go to serving it each year' — and getting serving right has produced 'over $220 billion in market cap in the last decade.' The skeptical camp, fairly: margins depend on billable tokens per GPU-hour offsetting both lower prices and higher rents, and 'this chart alone can't establish either outcome.'

Texas froze datacenter permits — and now you can insure against the backlash

SemiAnalysis · Oct 24

Abbott's September directive extended the grid pause to air permits for on-site power; SemiAnalysis counts six campuses, 8.7GW, caught in it — and a new insurance product prices the risk.

The workaround became the target. Abbott's August pause covered only grid interconnections, so building your own generation 'behind the meter' became the escape hatch. His September 21 directive told TCEQ, the state environmental regulator, to issue 'no permits sought by data center projects' until ERCOT and the water board finish their audits. TCEQ confirmed on October 19 that it has paused all permits tied to datacenter infrastructure. SemiAnalysis found six campuses with on-site power plans, 8.7GW in total campus size, that need air permits during the pause — though that's campus size, not delayed capacity: three already have a first tranche permitted, one has filed, two haven't. Air permits gate only the power plant, so site work continues. The base case: TCEQ resumes after ERCOT's December 10 report and near-term phases slip about a quarter. The governor also says he'll work to eliminate financial incentives for datacenters next legislative session.

The market is already pricing this risk as a product: Arbol, founded by a former Citadel strategist, launched a policy that pays a predetermined amount if a government-imposed moratorium halts or suspends construction past an agreed waiting period. At least 106 US datacenter projects — about $198 billion in investment — were blocked or delayed amid local opposition in the first half of this year, per Data Center Watch. And the bottleneck runs deeper than permits: a16z notes US factory capacity since 2019 is up for comms equipment (+110%) and semiconductors (+77%) but down 9% for electrical equipment, whose prices are up 69% — the 'missing supply response' behind the power crunch. As one reply put it: 'On-site power was the escape hatch from the grid queue, and now the hatch waits on ERCOT's review anyway.'

What it really takes to build a one-person company with AI

Bloomberg · Oct 23

A Sydney recruiter spent months decanting 16 years of expertise into four AI agents — close to 200 documents of workflows, privacy rules and compliance standards.

For 16 years, Alicia Keenan built a niche recruitment business the old-fashioned way: relationships, specialized knowledge, handpicked executives for animal health and pharmaceutical companies, at its peak about 11 people. Growth kept running into the same ceiling: herself. 'Everything relied on me,' she says. Late last year she began replacing her firm's human machinery with AI, reconstructing the entire business around four agents that are extensions of herself.

The transformation was arduous, with few shortcuts. It took months to nail down operating rules: close to 200 documents covering workflows, privacy rules, legal standards and compliance across multiple jurisdictions, her living-room walls covered in paper and reminder notes as she tried to decant almost two decades of experience. One agent monitors regulatory filings and company announcements for business; one produces market intelligence on scarce skills; one nurtures candidates before vacancies arise; one handles executive search. Keenan steps in at designated checkpoints.

The agents work faster and harder than a person — the first notices immediately if a European animal pharma company announces expansion in Indonesia or Vietnam, a signal it may soon hire. They make mistakes, and Keenan reckons it might be 18 months before she's comfortable scaling back her interventions. 'It's really the first time that I've not had to carry the burden of everything all the time,' she says. 'I'm working on the business rather than in it.' The lesson for anyone selling 'replace your team with agents': the technology is the easy part; the 200 documents are the job.

Scaling-law believers think better AI means novel medical discoveries; biologists think a 'bitter lesson in biology is kind of ridiculous' and that reliably simulating human toxicology is 5–10 years away. A useful corrective to any roadmap that assumes AI's coding gains transfer to wet labs on the same timeline.
