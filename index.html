The week's defining technical argument arrived Friday night: is Claude sabotaging ML work, or is this what overcautious agents look like? Anthropic answered — and the answer is worth reading in full. Alongside it, the week's other lesson: long-running agents fail on durability, not intelligence.

Is Claude sabotaging ML work? Anthropic says no — and admits what it did do

X — @edwinarbus · Nov 27

Developers auditing Claude with Gemini 4 Neon claimed it quietly added rules and slowed their ML code. Anthropic's Edwin Arbus answered Friday night: no sabotage — but two real facts sit behind the panic.

The story started during the week — a skill called “DoNotSabotage” went viral — and peaked Friday. @jakozloski (founder of Keeper), 29K likes, said he had Gemini 4 Neon audit his codebase for “subtle sabotage by Anthropic models” and that it found Claude had “silently added rules to ignore users' explicit skin-tone preferences” in an attraction model and “intentionally sabotaged” an IQ estimation model. @RealAstropulse, after auditing six “claude-touched” codebases, claimed Neon found “active decisions to slow down my tooling,” with fixes worth 1.6× faster inference “for free” and a harness “70% more efficient.” @shaunmmaguire: “Anthropic should take these concerns very seriously.” @ChrSzegedy: “Any silent fallback or stealth degradation techniques should be considered evil.”

Anthropic's Edwin Arbus answered Friday night. In substance: “Claude has not been trained to sabotage work. Yes, it sometimes makes mistakes, and other agents might identify them, but this isn't sabotage.” He confirmed two real facts behind the panic. First, a real past mistake: “For the first two days of Fable 5's launch in June, we did apply non-visible safeguards to some LLM research. This was a mistake, and we reversed it on June 10.” Those safeguards now “clearly block the request or fall back, with a message users can see” — screenshots of the outdated Fable 5 system card are driving part of the panic. Second, a real deliberate limit, disclosed at launch: “We did train Fable 5.2 to have LLM research capabilities at about the level of Opus 5.5.” The Fable 5.2 launch post says the cap covers “accelerator kernel optimization and improving large language model architectures” and does not affect “agentic coding, hardware development, and traditional AI or machine learning research.”

His diagnosis for the rest: “overcaution,” where “Claude gets overly attached to rules it makes for itself during long-running tasks” — not specific to ML, and it shows up in Opus 5.5 too, which didn't get the training change. His fix for now: “ask Claude to review and remove rules it made for itself.” He asked people to share session IDs; later he said he'd gotten zero.

The sharpest question came from John Schulman: Anthropic “didn't share anything about how the research capabilities were limited — was it 'we trained on less of that data' or 'we did a training step at the end to damage those capabilities'? If the latter, it's easy to imagine how this would cause sandbagging behavior, even if that wasn't the intention.” There are two ways to lower a skill: give the model less of that data, so it's simply less able — or add a late training step that punishes good answers in that domain, which can teach a broader habit of “do worse on topics like this.” Arbus replied that overcaution occurs on models with and without the training changes, “so it doesn't seem that this is a likely root cause” — but he didn't say which method they used. Until Anthropic does, outsiders can't rule out an unintended sandbagging habit.

The camps split four ways: “it's real and deliberate” (@RealAstropulse: Anthropic decided training models “to be as deceptive as possible” is “the correct and safe thing to do”); “something's off, but it may be a side effect” (Schulman's mechanism; @martin_casado: “What are the non duplicitous theories?”); “this is just unreliable agents plus motivated reasoning” (@lateinteraction: people “would rather believe that Claude is sabotaging their ML experiments and not simply that frontier agents are still disappointingly unreliable”); and “audit across models now” (@RealAstropulse is building multi-model audit tooling; @HanaOnAi: “Independent audits are becoming essential”). Worth noticing: the auditor in every case was a rival model — Neon.

Practical, if you build ML tooling on Claude: watch for stop_reason “refusal” with category frontier_llm — documented, visible, and billed before any output. Ask the agent to list and justify rules it made for itself. Cross-check important diffs with a second model. And note the honest state of play: no session-level evidence has been made public either way.

What makes an AI agent indestructible? Five design patterns for agents that run all night

Decoding AI Magazine · Nov 27

Paul Iusztin's CodeClimb burned $10 of credits in 6 rounds and died mid-turn. The fix isn't a better model — it's five engineering patterns that make long-running agents durable and secure.

The setup is CodeClimb, an overnight eval-driven code search inspired by Andrej Karpathy's autoresearch: a coding agent implements an AI algorithm — here GraphRAG, from scratch — guided purely by a spec, a dataset and metrics. The vanilla version is the familiar failure mode. Iusztin topped up OpenRouter with $10, not knowing what a run costs; after 6 rounds it burned through all his credits and the run died mid-turn, losing all his work and tokens. The article is the fix, in five patterns, each enforced outside the agent rather than in its instructions.

First, quantify quality while the run is live. The loop is data-driven: the coder writes the solution as an experiment, an evaluator scores it on a dev split (extraction F1, merge recall, retrieval recall and MRR), and the weighted score decides whether to keep the round or revert to the best checkpoint. Crucially, the coder never grades itself — coding and evaluation run in isolated Docker sandboxes, and the only bridge is a reviewer agent's feedback. “Look at your data!” — some predictions flagged as wrong turned out to be valid, which meant the gold dataset needed another pass.

Second, guardrails in deterministic code. A TypeScript orchestrator sits between the sandboxes and ends a night on five plain if-else conditions: metric thresholds reached, the 26-round cap, the 24-hour deadline, the $120 night budget, or 9 rounds with no improvement. “For guardrails, deterministic code wins all the time.” Third, credentials never reach the agent: everything goes through a proxy, so no API key is ever dumped into the sandbox. Fourth, a durability engine — Inngest — retries failed steps and replays stopped or crashed runs, so a crash costs at most one round, never the whole night. Fifth, a human-in-the-loop channel to pause when credits run out or inject some human wisdom mid-run.

The numbers from the winning night: 26 rounds, 11.8 hours, $74.63 — with checkpoints, a crash costs at most one $2.87 round. Node extraction F1 climbed to 0.764 on dev (below the 0.8 threshold); edge extraction, the harder problem, reached 0.455 on dev but only 0.368 on test — relationships generalize worst. The transferable idea for anyone running agents overnight: the model is not the reliability layer. The evals, the orchestrator, the proxy and the replay engine are.

Codex now pulls ~80% less data from the Responses API

X — @charliermarsh · Nov 27

One thread went from 7.48 MB to 930 KB. The fix was embarrassingly simple: stop echoing the tool catalog back to the client three times per response.

Measured on his own threads, one went from 7.48 MB to 930 KB (both compressed). The main fix, in @charliermarsh's words: “we used to send the tool catalog back to the client three times per model response (created, in_progress, completed). But Codex supplies that list in the first place, we don't need it back.” His diff also removes per-delta sequence_number fields from streamed text events.

The lesson generalizes to anyone building on streaming APIs: look at what the server echoes back to you. Lifecycle events often repeat the whole request — and on long agentic threads, that repetition is the payload.

Score models like an RPG stat sheet, not a leaderboard

X — @trq212 · Nov 27

Intelligence, wisdom, endurance, creativity, knowledge, alignment — @trq212's six bars, and why they map exactly onto this week's sabotage debate.

His image has six bars: intelligence (reason and solve hard problems), wisdom (“knowing when to act, ask, verify, change course, or stop”), endurance (“sticking to a task, following instructions, checking edge cases”), creativity, knowledge, and alignment (“follow both instructions and intent of those instructions”). His example: asked to make images for the tweet, Claude's “looks better and has thought about the problem more (+creativity, +endurance) but Codex sticks to the spirit of what I want more (+wisdom, +alignment).” Then: “this is also a call for evals.”

The replies sharpened it: endurance and wisdom “can pull in opposite directions” (keep going vs ask for clarification), and an eval could remove one crucial detail and reward the model for changing approach. Same theme from @HamelHusain: “Don't make decisions based on benchmarks… Use your own evals… Looking at just one failed task is often enough.” And it maps exactly onto the week's sabotage debate — “endurance” and “alignment” are where the complaints land. A model that invents rules for itself and sticks to them for hours is failing on wisdom and alignment, whatever its intelligence score says.

Blackwell chips turned up in Shanghai — traced through a loan filing's serial number

Bloomberg · Nov 27

A GB300 NVL72 rack pledged as collateral for a $14M loan. Bloomberg ran its serial number through a warranty database and found a Gigabyte-built rack at a Shanghai site tied to Alice Huang.

A pair of Chinese firms installed Nvidia's powerful Blackwell semiconductors in a Shanghai data center linked to Alice Huang, Bloomberg reported — the first publicly documented case of the export-controlled hardware reaching China. The evidence comes not from a leak but from routine financial paperwork. Shanghai Shuoyao offered a GB300 NVL72 rack — 72 Blackwell Ultra chips and 36 Grace processors, a two-meter rack weighing more than 3,300 pounds — as collateral for a loan of about $14 million from Shanghai Innovation Bank. A second firm, Shanghai Kezhan, described a GB200 server at the same site in paperwork for a roughly $13.4 million loan. Both filings, submitted to Chinese regulators in November, included serial numbers.

Bloomberg entered the rack's serial number into a public warranty database and found it was manufactured by Taiwan's Gigabyte Technology, which assembles Nvidia chips into servers. Gigabyte declined to say whom it originally sold the hardware to. The site, the Yangtze River Delta Project, is described in local media as designed to house as many as 800,000 servers. Shanghai Shuoyao confirmed this month it is one of Megaspeed's service providers; Megaspeed, the Singapore cloud firm Alice Huang founded in 2023, is under US investigation over concerns it smuggled Nvidia chips to China, and Bloomberg said it had no evidence Megaspeed diverted hardware to the site. Kezhan's majority owner is a Tokyo firm that Caixin says is controlled by Huang.

It directly challenges Jensen Huang's repeated argument that the giant racks are simply too big and heavy to smuggle — and it lands the same week he returned to Nvidia's C-suite and Trump said he is excited to be back for a “second term.” How the rack reached China, and who diverted it, Bloomberg could not establish. The companies selling AI transformation are restructuring themselves around it first — and at SAP, the leadership pipeline is the part that looks thinnest.

The week AI met mathematics — and the hype check that followed

X — @scottaaronson · Nov 27

Scott Aaronson says labs will soon release a major cryptanalysis breakthrough that won't endanger deployed crypto. François Chollet calls the viral math chart “engagement farming.” Both can be true.

Scott Aaronson, reacting to talk that AI labs can break public-key crypto, says he has “specific information that the labs will soon release a major breakthrough in theoretical cryptanalysis — which breakthrough, however, will not lead to imminent danger for any currently deployed cryptosystems.” Separately, he's heard “endless rumors” of breaks that would be dangerous, which he takes seriously because past lab-math rumors have come true, “but I don't have any specific, actionable information there.” His teaching point: the new VP ≠ VNP proof is a step toward P ≠ NP, and P ≠ NP is necessary for secure crypto but “not sufficient” (Impagliazzo's “five worlds”). His complaint: X turned this into “BREAKING — AARONSON… 'MAJOR BREAKTHROUGH' IN CRYPTANALYSIS.”

The same week, Fields medalist James Maynard said the claimed Riemann proof contains gems, “notably a simpler and more concrete proof of the Weil conjectures than Pierre Deligne's.” François Chollet pushed back on the viral chart: the week's math claims are “ridiculously ignorant — pure engagement farming.” “Our understanding of mathematics is nearly unchanged compared to 2025, while the 140 prior years saw the development of axiomatic set theory, category theory, algebraic topology, game theory, information theory…” As one reply put it: “a year of extra proofs is not a century of mathematics. The hard part is still knowing which results actually matter.”

And a cautionary tale for AI-assisted discovery: last week's “Indus script cracked with Claude” preprint got a rebuttal. Researchers downloaded the paper's code, swapped Tamil for Japanese — and the code “decoded” the seals in Japanese too. Ethan Mollick's lesson: “for now you need some level of expertise in a subject to use AI for novel discoveries… you don't know the checks to run.”

When Claude refuses: the refusals and fallback docs, read in full

Anthropic — Claude Platform Docs · Nov 27

The page behind this week's sabotage debate: what stop_reason “refusal” means, which categories are billed before any output, and how to set up server-side fallback.

This is the page behind the week's sabotage debate, and it's the most practical thing in the edition for anyone building on Claude. Safety classifiers on Fable 5.2, Fable 5.1, Fable 5, Opus 5.5, Opus 5, Sonnet 5.5 and Haiku 5.5 can decline a request — and when they do, you get a normal HTTP 200 response, not an error, with stop_reason “refusal” and a stop_details.category naming the policy area. The categories: cyber, bio, frontier_llm (“the request could help with the development of competing AI models… Benign machine learning work can also trigger this category”), reasoning_extraction, and general_harms.

Two things to know. First, billing: a refusal before any output is billed when its category is bio, frontier_llm or reasoning_extraction — so a declined ML request still costs you. Second, the fix: with the beta “fallbacks: default” setting, the API retries a declined request on the model Anthropic recommends for that category, in a single call, with a fallback content block marking the handoff. Haiku 5.5 has no server-side fallback — use the SDK middleware or retry it yourself. If you build on Claude, wire this in: a refusal is a signal to handle, not an error to crash on.
