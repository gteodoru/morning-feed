The agent bill is the product now: a cheap model that fails costs more than an expensive one that works

WSJ · Nov 15

Big Tech wants everyone on agents but cannot figure out who pays for the compute — and the field data says cost per good result is the only number that matters.

The WSJ's blunt framing: Big Tech wants everyone to use an AI agent but cannot figure out how to shoulder the cost of running one. The field numbers make the point concrete. In a head-to-head on the same TPC-H analytics question with the same answer, @HoytEmerson found Sonnet 5.5 used 10% fewer prompt tokens and half the output of Haiku 5.5 — and was still roughly 12x more expensive. And VentureBeat's rule of thumb from the MCP world applies here too: a 10-cent model that fails 80% of the time costs more per good result than a 30-cent one that works 90% of the time.

The counterweight is that the cheap tier keeps getting better at the things that used to need the expensive tier. Google doubled context for Argon, Carbon and Hydrogen in Antigravity, with compaction down 3.5x per user turn (@_mohansolo). And @poteto reports 11,000 PRs in a month at a roughly 0.5% revert rate from the AI Engineer Code Summit.

The takeaway for a platform team: price agents by verified outcomes, not by tokens. Put the cheap model on work you can check automatically, reserve the expensive one for judgment calls, and measure cost per good result — that is the number your CFO will eventually ask for.

One compromised MCP server can turn your agent's trusted tools against you

VentureBeat · Nov 15

Unit 42 measured a 78.3% attack success rate when five MCP servers share one agent — the auth history explains why, and the fixes are concrete.

MCP launched in November 2024 with no authentication at all. The history since is a slow retrofit: OAuth 2.1 arrived in March 2025, resource-server rules and RFC 8707 resource indicators in June 2025, client ID metadata documentation in November 2025, and in July 2026 dynamic client registration was deprecated and issuer validation made mandatory.

It was not fast enough. Unit 42 measured a 78.3% attack success rate with five MCP servers on one agent — what VentureBeat calls 'cascading trust': one compromised server can trick the agent into misusing tools you already trust. The advice is concrete: short-lived, narrowly scoped tokens; an identity-aware gateway that pins tool definitions; never cache credentials at startup.

For anyone building an agent platform, this is the design constraint to internalize early: every MCP server is untrusted input until proven otherwise, and the gateway — not the model — is where trust gets enforced.

How to get Claude to design without AI slop, in 40 minutes

Peter Yang — Behind the Craft · Nov 15

Nate Parrott, the Anthropic designer behind Claude Design, demos his workflow and shares five tips — including telling Claude what its own biases are.

In a 42-minute episode, Nate Parrott — the designer behind Claude Design and Artifacts — demos how he designs a Duet feature from a phone note, turns a spreadsheet into a live dashboard, and then turns that dashboard into a Claude Motion video, all from his phone. His five tips for avoiding AI slop:

1. Give Claude context upfront: screenshots, pages you like, your past work. For an existing product, have it rebuild the real screens from code. 2. Research familiar UX patterns: for hard layout problems, ask Claude to study products that solve something similar and note the layouts people already know. 3. Make Claude aware of its own design biases. 'The model has no idea what its own biases are.' In a design.md file, discourage the tells — serif-only text, off-white backgrounds, card-edge bars. 4. Start with wireframes: nail the core user flows before anyone evaluates fonts and colors. 5. Ask for multiple options, then remix: five options for key screens in different fonts, colors and styles, then pick the best parts and ask for five more.

He also names the four tells that make a design look AI-made. The wireframe-first plus bias-file discipline transfers directly to any team shipping Claude-built interfaces — including agent-built ones. Note: the full copy-paste prompt Peter Yang made for Claude Code sits behind the paywall.

A step toward defining the Standard Model on a lattice

OpenAI — Nathaniel Craig · Nov 15

GPT-6 Astra helped close a 25-year hole in theoretical physics — the most concrete public account yet of AI as a research collaborator.

OpenAI's science blog opens with a guest post by UCSB physicist Nathaniel Craig, and it is the clearest description yet of what 'AI as a research collaborator' looks like at the frontier. The problem: the Standard Model is fantastically accurate, but physicists mostly compute with perturbation theory, which fails for things like the proton's mass. The fix, lattice field theory, never worked for the whole Standard Model because the weak force is chiral — put a left-handed fermion on a naive lattice and a right-handed 'doubler' appears. Ginsparg and Wilson wrote the rules a chiral lattice must satisfy in 1982; Narayanan and Neuberger built 'overlap fermions' in 1994; Lüscher separated left- and right-handed fermions in 1999. The missing piece, open 'for more than 25 years': setting the phase of the fermion measure consistently around every closed loop of force fields.

Craig's idea: represent the same force fields on a succession of finer grids, compare the allowed fermion states between neighbouring grids, and add up the differences. Astra's role, in Craig's words: it 'suggested the right way to carry a given set of force fields onto finer grids, showed that the comparisons on finer grids would converge to a definite answer, and took the lead in developing the detailed proof.' Lattice theorists Maarten Golterman, Andreas Kronfeld and Yigal Shamir reviewed the proof and asked 'probing questions.'

Craig's own caveats matter: it is 'far more an answer in principle than an answer in practice'; experts still need to decide whether it has all the desired properties; and he is now checking reflection positivity at finite spacing. The replies name the real bottleneck: 'how much of this was the model proposing ideas versus checking them' — and 'I'll take it seriously when another group can use the definition without the chat log.' The pattern to steal is human strategy plus model construction plus outside expert stress-test — and the verification bottleneck is the same one your agent evals face.

Understanding positional embeddings in modern LLMs

Sebastian Raschka · Nov 15

Why attention needs position, how rotary embeddings actually work in code, and what 2026's open models really use.

Attention without position treats 'The red fox jumps over the gray fox' and 'The gray fox jumps over the red fox' the same. A causal mask gives some implicit order, but cannot tell 'one token back' from 'ten back.' Raschka walks through the whole lineage: learned absolute embeddings (GPT-2, fixed at 1,024 positions); sinusoidal (2017, fixed sine/cosine at geometric frequencies); and RoPE (Su et al., 2021), which rotates each query and key feature-pair by an angle proportional to its position, so the dot product depends only on the offset between tokens. His worked example makes it click: 20 degrees per position, query at 5 (100 degrees), key at 2 (40 degrees), 60 degrees apart, dot product 13 — shift both by 3 and it is still 13. That is why a 100-token system prompt does not change how 'the' attends to a fox three tokens back.

He gives a roughly 20-line PyTorch implementation and shows split-half vs interleaved pairing are equivalent up to reordering. Then the 2026 state of play: full RoPE in every attention layer is still most common but fell from 2025 to 2026; partial RoPE (MiniMax-M3 rotates 64 of 128 dimensions) is 'hardly worth it' on his math, though a paper found ~10% rotation often matched full RoPE; NoPE layers rely on the causal mask (SmolLM3 skips RoPE every 4th layer and reports better long context); learned relative position bias (Thinky's Inkling, echoing T5) is rising. His prediction: RoPE stays dominant into 2027. If you are debugging long-context behaviour in your agents, this is the mechanism to understand first.

Jiff 1.0: a Rust datetime library that treats time zones as a first-class citizen

BurntSushi — Andrew Gallant · Nov 15

Modelled on JavaScript's Temporal, already inside uv, Cargo and Starship — and its design ideas are worth stealing in any language.

Andrew Gallant (BurntSushi) shipped Jiff 1.0, a Rust datetime library modelled on JavaScript's Temporal, already used by uv, Cargo, Starship, fd, Nushell and Jujutsu. The 1.0 is an 'indefinite' stability commitment, so it can sit in other crates' public APIs.

The ideas worth stealing in any language: put time zones front and center (ZonedDateTime); handle DST 'gaps' and 'folds' explicitly, with a default 'compatible' strategy or earlier/later/reject; keep calendar units (months, days) separate from physical time, since 'a day' is not always 24 hours; round spans relative to a real date. Agents that schedule, log and coordinate across time zones will hit every one of these edge cases — steal the model before you hit them.
