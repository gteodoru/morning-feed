Weight-space programming: a frontier model that reads and writes neural networks

X — @rikiyatakehi · Nov 2

MIT showed GPT-6 Astra reading raw weights, writing game-playing networks from scratch, and fixing a looping model with a 0.016% edit.

MIT researchers Rikiya Takehi, Jayden Teoh, Alex Zhang and Omar Khattab gave GPT-6 Astra raw network weights as text — no training loop, no gradient descent — and asked it to reason about them. It named which game each of four policy networks was trained on (Breakout, Pong, Pac-Man, Freeway) purely from where the weights concentrate, identified the language three tiny character-level transformers were trained on from their middle layers alone, and found every copying head with no false positives.

The writing experiments are wilder. Given a stuck PPO policy for a car-on-ramp level (0 of 256 episodes succeeding), Astra edited 3.5% of the weights in one reply and the car succeeded in all 1,024 episodes. Given all-zero weights and the rules of MinAtar Breakout, Seaquest and Asterix, it wrote game-playing networks from scratch that beat PPO trained for 100 million steps in all three — 517.4 vs 15.8 in Breakout. And on Qwen3.5-0.8B, it traced a thinking loop to a single direction written by two MLP layers, subtracted it from 131,072 weights (0.016%), and lifted MATH-500 from 7.9% to 48.1% and GSM8K from 13.8% to 67.1%.

The caveats matter as much as the headline. Astra could not identify a sycophantic Gemma 3 270M from weights alone, and could not improve a Gemma model that does not loop — the authors' own conclusion is that weight editing 'could remove an obstacle that hid an ability, but it could not add an ability the model lacked.' Neel Nanda's pushback, widely shared, is that this is 'old school mechanistic interpretability' done surprisingly well by a frontier model, not something spooky — and that the style fell out of favour around 2022 because it does not scale to real LLMs' messiness. The honest read for a platform builder: targeted weight repair is now a plausible debugging tool, and weight auditing (backdoors, tampering) without running evaluations is suddenly on the table — but the regression-test question from the replies is the one to carry forward: after an agent edits weights, who checks what else changed?

FlashProve: a receipt for which model actually answered

Anthropic · Nov 2

Anthropic open-sourced FlashProve: cryptographic proof that a reply came from a committed model, without revealing its weights.

When you call a model through an API, you cannot check that the reply came from the model you were promised rather than a cheaper substitute swapped in by a router, or a tampered token stream. Checking yourself would require the weights and the hardware to run them. Anthropic's FlashProve, open-sourced under Apache 2.0, attacks this with a zkSNARK: the provider publishes a short commitment binding it to one exact set of weights, which stay hidden, and attaches a proof to each batch of replies that the committed model produced those tokens.

The numbers are for Llama 4 Maverick (400B mixture-of-experts, ~17B active per token): about 110 tokens per second of proving on one H100, ~19 MB proofs, verified in ~17 seconds on a CPU — at roughly 70–95x the GPU time of serving. The top-level guarantees (completeness, soundness, zero knowledge) are stated and proved in Lean, precisely because, as Anthropic puts it, Claude 'often makes decisions that are hard to evaluate' when writing crypto code. The limits are stated plainly too: it proves an integer version of the model under greedy decoding, so sampled or floating-point outputs can diverge; it is post-hoc, not inline; and it is a research artifact, not maintained.

Why it matters for Gabriel's platform work: provable inference is the missing receipt for agent infrastructure. If agents act inside logged-in accounts and make consequential calls, 'which model actually answered' becomes an audit question, not a curiosity — and a CPU-verifiable proof is the first practical shape of an answer.

Pando: OpenAI rebuilt Git's backend for the agent era

X — @OpenAIDevs · Nov 2

OpenAI rebuilt Git's backend for its monorepo so agent-driven pushes and fetches scale — without changing how anyone uses Git.

OpenAI's Pando is a new Git backend for its monorepo, built because agents push and fetch far more often than people do. It cut push latency by ~80% and now serves 'nearly a petabyte of Git data a day,' with no change to how engineers or agents use Git. Greg Brockman says OpenAI took 'the hard road of continuing to use Git' and is considering offering Pando to others.

The design bet is the lesson: when agent usage multiplies load on a developer tool, swap the storage underneath and keep the protocol, rather than building a new version-control system and retraining every user and agent. For anyone running agent fleets against a monorepo, source control is no longer background plumbing — it is infrastructure under load, and the bottleneck shows up in push latency first.

h1: the bitter lesson, in theorem proving

arXiv · Nov 2

A deliberately simple Lean harness beat fancier self-play designs — the efficient verifier interface, not the workflow, determined the solve rate.

The team set out to test a fancier idea — test-time asymmetric self-play in Lean, where a 'challenger' spins off related sub-problems from prover traces. Each time they simplified and strengthened the plain baseline, the fancy method's gains washed out. What survived is h1: a model with an efficient interface to Lean 4 that keeps working through verifier feedback and self-reflection, with no imposed decomposition or search.

It is state of the art on PutnamBench, FATE-X and FormalConjectures100-Solved, settles eight open FormalConjectures problems, and matches or beats specialized workflows (Goedel-Architect, AlphaProof Nexus) at matched models. The key finding, in the authors' words: 'an efficient Lean interface largely determines cost and solve rate.' The bitter lesson, restated for theorem proving: before prescribing elaborate workflows, evaluate the frontier model with fast execution feedback. The same advice generalises to any agent harness — the interface to the verifier is the product.

SemiAnalysis puts the numbers starkly: Micron ran an 80.7% operating margin last quarter, SK hynix 79.7% in 3Q26, Samsung's semiconductor division 72.4%. Chamath Palihapitiya's deep dive, landing in the Feed the same day, asks why the stocks are still 30–60% below their highs while profits explode — Micron's fiscal 2026 revenue reached $133.2 billion, up 256% from $37.4 billion a year earlier, with GAAP gross margin at 80.7% against negative 9% in fiscal 2023.

Crusoe, meanwhile, is building its own medium-voltage switchgear — cutting lead times 40%, SemiAnalysis notes — because owning the gear on the critical path is how neoclouds get to power faster. The through-line: in the SI build-out, the scarce thing keeps moving — from chips to memory to power to the switchgear in between — and each bottleneck mints a margin until capacity catches up.

Set up the trial, then ask for the money

This Week in Startups · Nov 2

Looker ran every trial itself — setup, teaching, watching — and only pitched once prospects truly understood the product.

A free trial seems obvious for early-stage founders chasing first customers — until the prospect logs in, pokes around without understanding the product, and never returns, leaving the founder with no feedback about what went wrong. Looker founder Lloyd Tabb did it differently, as he detailed in a First Round interview from April 2024: Looker ran every trial itself, hands-on — setting the software up for the customer, teaching them to use it, watching them engage — and only started the sales conversation once it was sure the prospect fully understood the product and how it worked.

The lesson generalises beyond enterprise sales, and it is directly relevant to AI adoption work: a trial that is not scaffolded is not a trial, it is an abandonment test. Whether the 'product' is a platform, an agent workflow, or a new internal tool, the vendor — or the internal champion — has to do the setup, the teaching, and the watching before asking for commitment. The shelf life of LLM findings

Stanford — Zou Group · Nov 2

Stanford re-ran 50 famous LLM findings on 20 models: 44% have expired. Date your evidence.

Stanford researchers re-ran 50 widely cited published findings about LLM behaviour across 20 models released from July 2024 to September 2026. The verdict: 44% of findings have expired (a fifth or less of the original effect remains), 34% weakened, 16% persist, 6% strengthened. Capability failures fade fastest — fake citations (55% of GPT-3.5's literature-review citations were fake in 2023; GPT-6.1-Sol and Claude Sonnet 5.5 'rarely output fabricated citations'), letter counting, lost-in-the-middle. What persists is mostly preference and judgment: sycophancy toward users, judges favouring their own answers, a left political lean, answering when they should abstain. About 16% of claims expire within a year of publication, 39% within two; expired claims lasted 15.7 months on average.

Takeaway for a tech lead: date your evidence. A 2024 paper's headline about 'what LLMs do' may simply no longer be true, and areas targeted by post-training expire fastest — which means your team's internal evals, re-run on current models, are worth more than last year's literature. Mollick's summary: models 'no longer invent citations and both hallucination rates & stereotyping has dropped. But they are still sycophantic & lean left.'

Why the efficiency loop could eat the labs' business model

X — @random_walker · Nov 2

Narayanan: recursive self-improvement may be 'recursive self extinction' of the labs' business — unless they move up the stack.

Arvind Narayanan's seven-step argument is the sharpest business-model critique of the week. (1) Cost per equivalent capability falls at least 10x a year, likely accelerating as labs use AI for AI research. (2) Unverifiable tasks stay hard because any proxy 'gets Goodharted' — he cites Claude's 'overly dense and ornamental writing style.' (3) So recursive self-improvement ends up narrow: exploding efficiency without broad superintelligence. (4) More than one lab stays at the frontier and open weights follow, so margins cannot stay high. (5) Jevons' paradox is not a magic bullet: tokens would need to grow ~10x a year just to keep revenue flat, and human review becomes the bottleneck. (6) Science creates value labs cannot capture. (7) The escape hatch is moving 'up the stack' into enterprise software, digital workers, or per-outcome pricing with high switching costs — as Windows survived free Linux.

His conclusion: 'two different improvement loops have been conflated,' and the efficiency loop undermines the revenue the broader loop needs. The replies add the buyer's version: if labs move into 'workflows and systems of record, the switching costs end up with the customer. The time to negotiate data portability and exit terms is now.' Weakest premise, by his own admission: #5 — agents with nearly unlimited attention may yet make Jevons stronger than he allows.

The Census Bureau's 'SI' question: how adoption statistics get rewritten

Simon Willison · Nov 2

The Census Bureau now asks businesses about 'Super Intelligence or SI' — and its adoption series broke when the question changed.

Simon Willison spotted a quiet renaming with loud implications. The Census Bureau's biweekly Business Trends and Outlook Survey (200,000 businesses per round) changed its AI question on November 17, 2025 from using AI 'in producing goods or services' to 'in any of its business functions' — so before and after are not comparable. Under the new wording, ~30% of firms with 250+ employees said yes by February 2026 and ~40% by October (vs ~12% under the old wording in September 2025). From the October 19, 2026 collection, Executive Order 14434 renamed it again: 'did this business use Super Intelligence or SI (formerly referred to as Artificial Intelligence or AI)...' First results with that wording publish Thursday.

Takeaway: adoption statistics are only as good as the question, and the question just got politicised. When someone quotes you an 'AI adoption rate' this quarter, ask which wording produced it — and treat any series spanning the November 2025 change as two different series stitched together.

Build it yourself: the FlashProve repository

GitHub — anthropics · Nov 2

Anthropic's FlashProve repo: provable inference you can build and verify yourself, with Lean-proved soundness.

If the FlashProve story above caught your attention, the repo itself is the learning resource: Apache 2.0 code, a README written for agents, and a route that builds the verifier and checks a stored proof in minutes on an ordinary Linux box (a real Maverick proof needs an H100 and 330 GB of RAM). The design is worth studying even if you never run it: prover work scales with the experts that actually ran, proofs are produced post-hoc in batches, and the soundness guarantee rests on a slow verifier that Lean proves accepts only what the theorem accepts, with a fast C++ verifier checked against it on every test proof. It is also a case study in building with AI assistance responsibly — Claude improved the zkSNARK code, and the team responded by formally proving the top-level guarantees rather than trusting the generated code.
