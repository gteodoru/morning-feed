Cheaper tokens, not cheaper tasks

X — @addyosmani · Nov 9

Fable 5.2 writes hundreds of tests where Opus wrote twenty — so the task costs the same, or 31% more.

SemiAnalysis puts Fable 5.2 on top of its GDPx benchmark at 16.4%, with gains concentrated in chip design (+10.8% over Opus 5.5) and software engineering (+15.5%). But the main reason is that Fable 5.2 tests its work far more thoroughly — hundreds or even thousands of test cases where Opus 5.5 and Fable 5.1 stopped at around twenty — and eagerly launches subagents to re-derive and audit. Cost per task rose 31% ($67.16 to $88.15) despite the 20% lower token price. Addy Osmani's companion guide reaches the same conclusion: about the same per task as 5.1.

Osmani's ladder: start on Opus 5.5 at medium effort ($4/$20). Move down to Sonnet 5.5 ($2/$10) for scoped work and Haiku 5.5 for classification and subagents. Move up to Fable 5.2 ($8/$40) for major migrations, architecture, and leading many subagents. Try one effort step before switching models — past high, one more level can cost as much as a bigger model. On FrontierCode 2.0, Fable 5.2 at high roughly equals Opus 5.5 at max (45.3% vs 45.5%) for about half the cost per task; Opus 5.5 at low, with failures re-run at high, passed about 97% of a SWE-bench Pro subset for $0.17 a task vs 95.3% for $0.29 all at high. And an Opus lead over up to 25 Sonnet workers cost about a third less than Opus alone on a 22M-token defect search, with about the same score.

The takeaway for your platform work: price per token is the wrong number. Measure score against spend on your own tasks, plot it, and shadow on real traffic before migrating.

The Fable 5.2 prompting guide: “This codebase is yours”

X — @simonw · Nov 9

One sentence changes whether the model refactors — the most practical document of the launch.

Simon Willison's advice on every Anthropic release: read the updated prompting guide, because it shows Anthropic is just as surprised by its new model's idiosyncrasies as the rest of us. This one is the most practical document of the launch.

Ownership changes behavior. Say nothing about ownership, or call the code someone else's, and Fable 5.2 adds to the existing structure even when that duplicates code, reporting nearby problems instead of fixing them. Adding “This codebase is yours: what it's for and how it's built are your call, and you'll keep building on it” made it merge repeated code in about half of small-task tries, versus under a tenth without it — at the cost of about 1.5x output tokens and an unrequested behavior change in 1 of 6 to 10 tries. Leave the sentence out when the user owns the code.

Effort: start at high (the API default); most of the gain is already there. Caution piles up: notes, memories and reviewer conditions accumulate “never” rules, and Fable 5.2 obeys them closely — one stale “never retry” note cut retries after a busy tool from nearly always to about 4 in 10. A general “act more freely” changed nothing; naming the exception in the rule itself got the step taken 9 times in 10. Approvals are applied literally, so name the exact actions.

Breaking changes: preserved thinking now applies to every account — change the system prompt, tools or an earlier message and resend thinking blocks and you get a 400 error. Keep history append-only, or use compaction, context editing or the drop_block opt-in.

The human counterpoint, from @badlogicgames (3,300+ likes): “the most important skill in 2026 is still being able to tell your agents 'wtf is this crap, no, not like this.'” As models get better, the bad output just looks more reasonable — which makes the judgment call harder, not easier.

1,000 agents prove Fermat's Last Theorem

Claude Dev · Nov 9

A thousand agents, one shared Postgres database, 28.7 hours — and a coordination design worth stealing.

In September, dozens of agents on an internal model formalized Fermat's Last Theorem in Lean using a purpose-built platform, a researcher, 11 days and about 6B output tokens. This time 1,000 agents on an early Fable 5.2 got only general tools: a no-network sandbox each, Lean plus Mathlib, a personal Git branch with merge requests into main, and a shared Postgres database. They were given 60 target statements, a one-sentence sketch, definitions and 47 already-proved lemmas, plus a hierarchy of 1 lead and 999 helpers. Nothing else about how to organize.

In 10 minutes the lead created five tables: announce (orders), assign (who owns what), notes (open board), subtasks (claimable to-do list) and landed (finished work). All 10 feasible statements were done in about 70 minutes. The last hard piece, Langlands–Tunnell, became a task tree anyone could split: 2,418 tasks, up to 10 levels deep, proved at hour 28.4, FLT at 28.7 — a third of the earlier output tokens and a proof a seventh as long. On the same setup, Opus 5.5 got 26% done in 18 hours.

The reusable part is the safety design: no messaging tools, only DB and Git; pushes only to the agent's own branch; SQL as an unprivileged role with 5-second statement limits; an LLM classifier checking every shell command and file edit; any block on the lead halts the whole team pending human review. Over 1M tool calls, none was blocked. The caveats matter too: agents misjudge difficulty and give up, and the harness sent the lead 19 “keep going” reminders. Human encouragement still matters.

serc: Codex writes a Rust CPython bytecode compiler

X — @charliermarsh · Nov 9

Byte-for-byte identical .pyc files, 3–4x faster compiles — written entirely by an agent, with CPython as the oracle.

Charlie Marsh had Codex write serc, an experimental compiler that turns Python source into .pyc files byte-for-byte identical to CPython's, for CPython 3.12–3.15 and -O/-OO optimization levels. Median compile times: Airflow 324 to 79 ms (4.1x), Django 358 to 94 ms (3.8x), NumPy 432 to 131 ms (3.3x), Transformers 832 to 288 ms (2.9x). It is parallel by default, skips up-to-date caches, refuses to overwrite symlinks, and is installable as serc-cli on crates.io, dual-licensed MIT/Apache.

Why it matters: a byte-identical oracle — CPython itself plus a huge corpus — is the ideal setup for an agent. As one reply put it, “an agent can just keep going until the diff is empty.” The README warns that all code changes, PR summaries and documentation were authored entirely by GPT in Codex, and that it “may emit incorrect bytecode in some cases.” It lives in a new astral-experiments org for agent-authored exploratory projects and might ship opt-in in uv. One practical point: you can compile bytecode without installing CPython, e.g. in a Docker build.

Reply camps: bug-for-bug compatibility is the point and also the worry; a new security surface (bytecode differential CVEs); and what's next — a Rust interpreter, or straight to LLVM IR.

OpenAI's Control Room: a kill switch for research models

X — @OpenAIDevs · Nov 9

Every research inference carries a job ID; stop the job and output halts within 35 seconds.

OpenAI described Control Room: every research inference request carries a job_id that the engine checks with a central service. Stop the job and output halts within 35 seconds. It fails closed. This is the infrastructure behind safe research at scale — a central kill switch for model output, not a policy document.

In the same window, OpenAI let a “dot” attach to multiple local computers: “multi-exec” lets a dot run commands on any connected machine, and you choose one per task. The maximalist setup doing the rounds runs a persistent Mac Studio with every CLI logged in and “Allow All” browsing and downloads — use at your own risk, as its author says. The trade-off is the story: the more computers an agent can reach, the more the kill switch matters.

Decision models in production: Amazon replaces Sonnet with a decider

Strands Agents · Nov 9

98% cheaper ticket labeling — and a rollout pattern (DUAL mode, 45-second rollback) any team can copy.

Two Amazon engineers replaced Claude Sonnet 4.6 labeling tickets into 20 categories in a huge internal system with Strands Decider, a decision model built on Gemma 4 E4B. A decision model returns one probability per option plus confidence — no text. Results: 897 vs 4,215 ms per call, 2,010 vs 6,724 tokens per ticket, about $500 vs about $22,200 per million tickets — 98% less, on SageMaker ml.g5.xlarge at roughly 640 ms per request.

Two lessons worth copying. First, a three-way switch (BEDROCK / DUAL / DECIDER) in AppConfig: run in DUAL on real traffic with every disagreement judged, and rollback as a 45-second config change. Second, JSON key order moved probabilities by up to 0.19, so sort keys. The limits they state: it only works for closed choices from a stable catalog that fit in about 4K tokens.

This is the “big model + cheap decider” pattern in production at serious scale — the same shape as the research brief from October. Wherever your platform has a closed-choice classification step running on a frontier model, this is the first place to look for a 50x cost cut.

Tutor Intelligence TI-0.1 / TRACE technical report

Tutor Intelligence · Nov 9

Frontier coding agents rebuild robot tasks in simulation from a few teleop demos — robot performance as a function of compute.

What you'll learn: frontier coding agents (GPT-6 Astra, Opus 5.5) rebuild a robot task in Isaac Lab from a few teleop demos, using synchronized images, commands and measured joint state. Visual fit comes from inverse rendering; physical fit from replaying recorded commands and tuning delays, friction and pad stiffness (9.9 mm RMS arm error over 6.4 s). Agents write a hybrid-MDP reward with explicit modes; PPO trains a privileged-state policy (99.8% sim success on Lego pick), distilled into a camera-based diffusion policy on the Sonny robot. Real rollouts feed back into the simulation.

The lessons they list: visual fidelity isn't enough; task success can hide impossible grasps; smooth motion makes distillation work; hardware is ground truth. Their Rust stack, Golem, cut CPU 90% and RAM 79% vs Python. Their pitch: robot performance “becomes a function of compute.” The takeaway: the same agent patterns from software — simulation as the oracle, distillation as the deployment path — are now the robotics playbook.
