OpenAI proposes a perplexity "speed limit" for AI — and Microsoft answers

OpenAI — Jakub Pachocki · Nov 22

OpenAI published its internal pretraining curve and proposed a shared "speed limit" measured in perplexity. Microsoft answered within hours with its own number.

Sunday around midday, OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki published "Perplexity and Intelligence" — and, for the first time, a frontier lab put its own capability curve on a common axis and invited rivals to do the same. The essay discloses OpenAI's held-out log-perplexity (cross-entropy loss on its internal codebase) for every model since GPT-3.5: ~0.99 at GPT-3.5 (fluent conversation), ~0.91 at GPT-4 (chain-of-thought becomes productive), "below 0.8" brought transformative capability in cybersecurity and mathematics, and at ~0.76 models "started producing groundbreaking results" on open math problems — resolutions "coming out of the model one after another in a single Slack thread felt deeply surreal." Pachocki forecasts "further transformative capabilities as we enter the ~0.68–0.7 log-perplexity window" — and admits he doesn't know what they will be, or whether we're even testing for them.

The proposal: shared norms for evaluating and red-teaming models before first use, shared transparency standards, and "common limits, based on perplexity and other measurements, that developers are committed to not crossing until our ability to understand and monitor AI behavior achieves a certain concrete milestone (such as the development of a successor to CoT monitors)." He sets no number. Sam Altman called it "the most important thing OpenAI has published in awhile"; Greg Brockman picked out the line that alignment and monitoring progress "is unlikely to be fast enough to allow the full potential acceleration in pretraining."

Within hours, Microsoft answered the call: Mustafa Suleyman reported MAI-2 Base at 0.819 log-perplexity on OpenAI's own eval set — "same ballpark as the GPT-5.6 Sol base model" — and flagged that cross-lab comparison will need bits-per-byte, not perplexity, since tokenizers differ. The camps split four ways: "finally a quantifiable pacing standard" (@yonashav, @peterwildeford, @willdepue); "the chart is the story — pretraining never plateaued" (@rasbt, @swyx); "who audits the number?" (tokenizers, held-out data, defection); and "you can't regulate intelligence" (@tszzl, @ylecun). TechCrunch's framing is the sharpest: to establish a speed limit, you first have to agree on how to measure speed. Watch whether Google, Anthropic, Meta or xAI report numbers — and whether anyone proposes an actual threshold.

When AI writes all the code, engineers think harder

Peter Yang — Behind the Craft · Nov 22

"I've never had to think harder than I do right now." The job moves up the stack — architecture, testing, and managing agents on a token budget.

Microsoft EVP for Copilot Charles Lamanna, in conversation with Peter Yang, on what engineers should do when coding is nearly free: the best engineers "don't hand write code anymore but also never had to think harder." Codex makes Code Mode cheaper by caching the tool catalog

X — @charliermarsh (OpenAI) · Nov 22

Codex stopped re-sending a multi-megabyte tool catalog on every call. The fix: send it once per session. The lesson applies to every agent harness.

In Code Mode, the model writes JS that calls tools — so it needs the full tool catalog (names, descriptions, arguments), which can run to several megabytes. Before, Codex serialized, sent and deserialized that list on every Code Mode call: "multiple MBs just to invoke text(1)." Now each snippet still runs in a fresh V8 isolate, but the host process is reused: the catalog is sent once per session with a revision ID and reused while tools don't change. That's 10 ms per call, and "the model can run dozens of these snippets in a single turn" — "this happens billions of times a day." The lesson generalizes to any agent harness you build: per-call overhead compounds brutally at scale; cache anything that's per-session, not per-call.

Context layers are a database problem

X — @sh_reya · Nov 22

Retrieval is I/O-bound like a database — except a context layer is lossy by design, so every retrieval choice is a correctness choice.

Retrieval for LLMs is I/O-bound like query processing: you speed it up by sending less data or sending it faster, as an index does. But a B-tree returns the same rows as a full scan, while a context layer is "lossy by design" — so every retrieval or summarization choice is also a correctness choice, and the only fix is company-specific evals. In a follow-up, Shankar is bearish on file systems as the context layer, citing Bachman's 1973 Turing lecture on navigational databases being displaced by Codd's relational model: expect "logical/physical data independence" to win again. If you're designing the context layer for your agent platform, this is the frame to argue with: retrieval is a database problem, and the evals are the product.

What comes next after Anthropic's historic IPO

WSJ — Kate Clark · Nov 22

The largest IPO ever made 5,000 employees paper millionaires and turned a $61.5B Series E bet into ~15×. Now the Valley waits for the copycats — and the over-rotation.

Anthropic completed the largest IPO in history, raising $81 billion — and the WSJ's Kate Clark asks what comes next. More than 5,000 employees became millionaires on paper; backers like Lightspeed and Iconiq sit on stakes worth more than $25 billion at the IPO price. Some of that capital will recycle back into the ecosystem, seeding the next generation of AI startups — and, as Sapphire Ventures' Jai Das put it, "The Valley is probably going to over-rotate and waste some capital." TechCrunch tells the other half of the story: how three backers got to roughly 15× paper returns. GIC met CFO Krishna Rao in early 2025, then invested more than $4.5B across the Series F ($183B), Series G ($380B, co-led with Coatue) and the pre-IPO round ($965B) for about 1% of the company. Baillie Gifford's Lilian Li studied Anthropic for nearly two years before investing $1.5B across three rounds. The lesson is about timing more than picking: the same company, bought two years later, returned roughly a fifth of the multiple. Anthropic shares traded around $93 in premarket Monday, about 12% above the $83 IPO price.

Apple's war over AI agents

Bloomberg — Mark Gurman (Power On) · Nov 22

Apple locks in each year's software roadmap a year in advance. Rivals ship weekly. Gurman's verdict: the company isn't organized to win the agent era.

Mark Gurman's argument in this week's Power On is structural, not technical: Apple is at serious risk of losing the AI agent war because of how it develops products, not because it lacks resources. Inside Craig Federighi's software organization, work runs on an internal release calendar — A through G — with the roadmap for a major OS largely decided by late summer or early fall of the preceding year. Apple then spends roughly 12 months executing a plan set long before it reaches users. Rivals ship new models and capabilities multiple times a week; Apple ships one major upgrade a year, concentrated in a three-month fall window.

The gap shows in Siri AI: still slow to respond, limited third-party integration, on-screen awareness that Gurman calls "a total crapshoot" — he asked it to add a meeting from a text thread with the person, place and time all visible on screen, and it asked him for the date, time and name. Multistep control over Apple apps arrives in December, third-party apps next year. Apple confirmed this week it is building a more advanced agent — a job listing describes one that "browses the web, works across apps, runs commands, writes and debugs code" — but Gurman doesn't expect a launch before late 2027. By then, rival agents will have accumulated users' accounts, preferences and routines, and switching away may be as hard as leaving the iPhone ecosystem itself. Apple's remaining cards: its privacy reputation, the App Store, and friction it is adding for third-party agents on the Mac — setting up ChatGPT's computer-use on macOS 27.3 takes 14 separate steps. Riemann hypothesis, day three: what we actually know

X — @littmath · Nov 22

Almost surely correct, nobody understands it yet, rival claims not credible — and a huge amount of work remains.

Three days after the Lean-formalized Riemann hypothesis proof landed, mathematician Daniel Litt gives the clearest summary of where things stand: the proof is "exceedingly likely to be correct"; "no human understands it yet," though there's no obvious obstacle and a lot of activity; competing human claims of alternate proofs or debunks don't seem credible; and "a huge amount of work" remains — there's an RH for any system of polynomial equations, and in general we don't even know the L-function converges on the critical line. And Patrick Heizer, reposted by Yann LeCun: "there is nothing like Lean for biology; you just have to literally run the experiments." The open question from the replies: if no human understands it yet, is it really a proof — and how long until someone can explain why it works?

Two essays frame the weekend's biggest debate — what pretraining loss actually measures, and whether the progress charts match reality — plus a repo for the builders.

"Compression and Intelligence" — Ryan Greene

Ryan Greene — greene.sh · Jul 25

Pretraining is literally compression — and that identity is why a loss number can serve as a yardstick for intelligence.

Start here to judge OpenAI's perplexity proposal on its merits. Greene's argument: pretraining is literally compression. Cross-entropy loss is the average number of bits a model needs to encode the next token, so a better predictor is a better compressor — and that identity is exactly what Pachocki leans on. He distinguishes two ways of counting the bits: offline compression (use a pretrained model's weights to encode data; LLMs beat gzip, but an honest accounting charges for the weights) and online compression (train from scratch as you go; the total code length is the area under the training-loss curve, which ties compression to data efficiency). Intelligence, in this frame, is the capacity to reuse and extract structure to predict novel data — and offline compression lines up "nearly linearly" with real-world benchmark performance. The takeaway for your work: a perplexity yardstick is strong precisely because it tracks the "intelligence substrate" across eras where benchmarks saturate — and weak exactly where Suleyman flagged it, in its dependence on dataset and tokenizer, and in what it ignores (test-time compute). Written for Ray Solomonoff's 100th birthday; the deepest short read on why the loss curve matters.

"The Most Important Charts in AI are Wrong" — Ramez Naam

Ramez Naam · Nov 21

Internal usage data says AI handles ~9–16 minute tasks — not the hours the famous charts imply. The gap tripled in four months.

Read this alongside Greene as the counterweight. Using OpenAI's and Google's own internal-usage data, Naam finds AI completes ~9-minute tasks at 80% success inside Google and an adjusted ~16 minutes at OpenAI — versus METR-style extrapolations that run 33× (OpenAI) to 41× (Google) higher. The original AI 2027 scenario overshoots by more than 100×. The gap roughly tripled from June to October, and OpenAI's data shows inputs (AI usage) "soaring" while outputs are "rising somewhat." His ask is the same as Pachocki's in spirit: more transparency, starting with Anthropic. Read together, the two essays frame the real debate: one says loss keeps falling and capabilities jump in ways nobody predicts; the other says real-world task length is growing far more slowly than the charts claim. Both rest on lab-published data — which is exactly why the transparency argument matters.

nospeaker: make a MacBook trackpad talk

GitHub — os6/nospeaker · Nov 22

Drive the trackpad's haptic motor through a private Apple framework and it buzzes like a voice. A masterclass in lateral thinking.

A repo that makes a MacBook's Force Touch trackpad talk with the speakers muted. It drives the haptic motor through Apple's private MultitouchSupport framework, whose vibrations are lists of sine tones played up to ~4,000 times a second; macOS's own voice renders the line, and every 10 ms linear prediction extracts the two strongest formants, loudness and pitch, so the motor "buzzes like a voice." Measured usable range ~100 Hz–4 kHz, cleanest near 1 kHz; Apple's public haptics API stops at three fixed taps. Needs a Force Touch trackpad and macOS 12+ on Apple Silicon. Worth opening for the sheer audacity — and as a reminder of how much capability hides behind private APIs. One reply: "thats one way to defeat the air gap."
