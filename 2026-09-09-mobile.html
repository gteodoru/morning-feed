“No Moat”: a full game built with Astra in 2 hours

Peter Yang · Sep 9

Peter Yang built ‘No Moat’ — a roguelike deckbuilding game where you play an AI founder recruiting teammates and fighting rivals — with OpenAI’s Astra in two hours. The title is the thesis: when anyone can ship a game before lunch, there is no moat in building.

Astra is the model to watch here: OpenAI’s latest release is reportedly its first to cross a ‘critical’ cybersecurity threshold — able to find and exploit real-world zero-days — and it’s already good enough to carry a full game build in a single sitting.

Why this matters for you: the demo-to-product gap is now measured in hours, not months. For anyone running a games or software org, the question is no longer ‘can AI build this’ but ‘what do we do when everyone can’. Taste, distribution, and judgment are the remaining moats — exactly the things that don’t autocomplete.

Coding agents that run without a server to babysit

Paul Iusztin · Sep 9

Paul Iusztin is building Decode, a coding agent designed to run remotely — triggered from your laptop, a cron job, or a webhook — with no server of yours to maintain.

This is the unglamorous hard part of the agent era: not the model, but the runtime. Long-running agents need somewhere to live, something to wake them, and state that survives. Decode’s bet is that the winning pattern is event-driven agents — cron and webhooks in, code out — rather than another chat window.

If you’re bringing AI cloud agents into a company, this is the architecture conversation worth having early: who hosts the agent, what triggers it, and where its memory lives.

Claude Fable 5.1: up to 45% cheaper agentic work

Last Week in AI · Sep 9

Anthropic’s Fable 5.1 (and Mythos 5.1) cut the cost of agentic workloads by up to 45%, with stronger agentic performance, enterprise data kept on customer clouds, and reported lab-verified gains in bio tasks like protein binder design.

Agentic loops burn tokens the way trucks burn diesel — a 45% price cut changes the unit economics of every agent you ship. The enterprise-data-on-customer-clouds detail matters just as much: it removes the objection that has kept regulated companies on the sidelines.

Worth noting alongside: two new benchmarks, FrontierChallenge (scientific workflow completion) and Thinkingbox (agents in stateful business workflows), both attacking the same problem — demos are easy, reliability is not. If you’re evaluating agents for production, watch this space, not the launch videos.

Anthropic’s $80B week and the rise of the neoclouds

Chamath Palihapitiya · Sep 8

Anthropic signed a six-year, $45B agreement with Nscale and a $35B agreement with Lambda — in five days. The recipients are ‘neoclouds’: GPU-first clouds that barely existed three years ago and are forecast to approach $400B in revenue by 2031.

Why the hyperscalers are losing this workload: AI training runs thousands of GPUs in lockstep for weeks (nothing to share), and packing GPUs together pushes rack power from 10–15 kW to 40–250 kW — new networking, new electrical, often liquid cooling. Neoclouds strip the software layers and sell raw performance: 50–70% below hyperscaler list prices for on-demand GPU hours, plus earlier access to scarce Nvidia chips.

Nvidia is actively feeding this channel — $2B each into CoreWeave and Nebius this year; CoreWeave’s contracted backlog hit $104.2B at the end of Q2. And the category is producing hybrids: after SpaceX acquired xAI, Anthropic contracted all of Colossus 1 (300+ MW, 220,000+ Nvidia GPUs), making SpaceX an AI developer and a neocloud at once.

The bear case, and it’s a real one: neoclouds borrow billions at a premium to buy depreciating hardware, then rent it to customers who are simultaneously building their own capacity and custom chips. Chamath’s 88-page deep dive on whether the economics survive is paywalled — the free essay above is the best primer on the category I’ve seen.

Founders should spend 50% of their time hiring

This Week in Startups · Sep 8

Investor David Frankel — quoting Bezos — says founders should spend half their time assembling the team. As an investor, he says, hiring quality is the thing he watches most.

The details are the good part. Frankel’s ideal founding combo: a CEO who’s a great salesperson and a CTO who’s ‘a bit of a magician’ — and he studies that dynamic closely. Kruze Consulting’s Scott Orn adds a timing rule: the moment the term sheet is signed, ‘pivot your brain’ to recruiting, because it takes founders longer than they expect to figure out who they actually need. And Snowflake’s Frank Slootman: only hire on a ‘hell yes’ — if it’s not a hell yes, it’s a hell no.

Worth sitting with if you’re leading a team through change: the bottleneck is rarely the technology roadmap, it’s whether the right people are in the room. (The post includes the 20VC video clips.)

DeepMind’s AlphaGenome Atlas: 9 billion DNA variants, precomputed

Bloomberg · Sep 9

DeepMind released AlphaGenome Atlas — predictions for all 9 billion possible single-letter changes in the human genome, packed into a 1-petabyte database that scientists can query in seconds, free for researchers.

The backstory: only 2% of the genome codes for proteins; the other 98% was dismissed as ‘junk’ until large genetic studies linked non-coding variation to cancer, diabetes, and heart disease. AlphaGenome predicts how DNA changes affect gene function, and Atlas precomputes all of it — plus pointers to which biological processes and cell types each mutation touches.

DeepMind is running the AlphaFold playbook: free tools for science, license fees for commercial users (including sister company Isomorphic Labs). The open question, with Demis Hassabis stepping down as CEO in August: does Google keep funding fundamental research at this scale?
