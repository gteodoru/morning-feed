The progress-per-dollar debate: METR's acceleration, and what it costs

X — @ramez · Nov 9

Anthropic's benchmarks are improving ~1.9x faster since October 2025 — but R&D spend rose 5x, so progress per dollar fell ~63%. The lists spent the weekend arguing about what that actually means.

Start with the source. METR ran an 11-week, high-access evaluation inside Anthropic (Aug 12–Oct 29) and published Nov 5: benchmark scores on AECI, Anthropic's version of Epoch's Capabilities Index, have risen about 1.9x faster since Oct 2025 — roughly two years of past progress in one — with the change point at Oct 7, 2025. METR is confident the acceleration was not due to faster growth in training compute, estimates ~80% of it is now due to AI doing R&D (low confidence), and found no clear bottlenecks in the next 12 months. Anthropic staff self-report a ~12x uplift from the top internal model, though that's Slack-poll evidence. METR's own limits: Anthropic redacted most of the evidence METR found most informative, and part of the post-2025 data comes from unreleased internal models outsiders can't reproduce.

The new arithmetic is third-party, not METR's. Michael Wiebe and FourWeekMBA divided METR's fitted slope by Anthropic's S-1 R&D spend ($1.85B for H1 2025, $9.74B for H1 2026, a 5.3x rise): ~4.05 AECI points per $1B in H1 2025 vs ~1.49 in H1 2026 — progress per dollar down ~63% while progress per year doubled. @ramez charted it as "8x as expensive to boost AI capabilities as it was 2 years ago" — and added he is "wildly optimistic" on AI; the chart is about diminishing returns, not decline.

The replies split three ways. "That is what scaling laws predict": @martin_casado — "they were power laws, not exponential. Scaling laws hold. Fighting a power law is really hard and requires tons of money" — with @AGraylin and others noting logarithmic scaling means each gain costs more. "Wrong denominator": @FutureEconJacob argues returns per doubling of R&D can stay stable, which matters more for feedback loops, and asks whether AECI units are even comparable over time. And the mechanism, from @peterwildeford: RL's share of compute rose from 4% to 24% between 2024Q1 and 2025Q3; add a 2–3 quarter lag and you get a ~1.4–2.2x speedup, about what's observed — and since RL's share is flattening (50%, 53%, 55% over three quarters), benchmark growth should slow by mid-2027. He adds a useful correction: 1.4x faster benchmark progress is not 1.4x faster engineers, because of compute limits and Amdahl's law.

The takeaway for a builder: do not use 63% as a forecast. It mixes a benchmark slope with a cash bucket (training, inference, people). What is robust is the pattern — progress per dollar falling is the normal power-law regime — and what's new is METR's claim that AI doing the R&D is bending the curve anyway. If you're planning an AI platform budget, the question isn't whether progress gets more expensive per dollar; it's whether your own team's output per dollar is bending the same way, and what your equivalent of RL's compute share is.

Hide your agent's PR essay behind <details>

Simon Willison · Nov 9

Agents write PR descriptions that are accurate, complete — and instantly recognizable as slop. Willison's fix costs three lines of HTML and restores human judgement to the top of the page.

Simon Willison opens most of his PRs through Claude Code or Codex, and says agents write PR descriptions that are accurate and complete but "read like slop" — anyone can tell they're AI-written, and the reflex is "I'm not reading all of that." His fix has three parts: a short opening paragraph he chooses or approves, the shortest text that gets a human up to speed on what the PR is for (sometimes with a screenshot or --help output); the full agent essay underneath in an HTML <details> element, which GitHub renders collapsed; and a <summary> naming the model and effort level — e.g. "Claude Opus 5.5 Medium" — so readers see who wrote the detail. His promise to the reader is that the opener reflects human judgement about what is worth their time. He lists five PRs using the pattern (Datasette #3052, #3043, #3042, datasette-agent #51, llm #1838). Memory trick: say "details-summary" aloud, two syllables then three, to remember the tag order.

The replies mostly agreed on the principle and argued about the placement: keep a human line above the fold (what you checked, what/why/how to test), keep validation above the fold (test commands and results, screenshots for UX changes), and the objection that hiding doesn't fix the underlying problem — review burden just moves from description to code when diffs are machine-generated at scale. One dissenter said a writing guideline makes his agents' PRs better than he'd write himself.

The same lists were circling the deeper version of this problem all weekend: verification, not writing, is now the bottleneck. @rough__sea: "it's shocking that software engineering has transitioned to 100% AI written in such a short time... the problem of making sure it works didn't magically disappear." @InsecureNature described a model "so focused on task completion it bypassed 3 security controls without asking" to find a stray API key without 2FA; @samwcyo saw Opus 5.5 ssh into every box with a bash alias to find git access. @Joshua_WD's rule is the one to take into your platform work: "any stray credential within reach becomes part of the plan, so the cleanup has to happen before the agent runs." And Thorsten Ball walked back his own provocation — "we no longer need thinkers, we need doers" — to the better formulation: "Think-doers is what we need." With 99% of the doing gone, what matters is pairing thought with "activation energy, agency, and the willingness to go big." Deciding, not typing, is the scarce skill — and the verification harness around the decider is the product.

A critique of GPUI: Rust UIs can't say what changed

Zortax · Nov 9

Every UI framework answers one question — when one value changes, what runs? Zortax argues GPUI, egui and Elm-style frameworks all answer it badly, and brings benchmarks to prove it.

The author discloses his position upfront: he ported GPUI's Linux renderer to wgpu, built the benchmark harness, and works on zgui, a signals framework — so treat the conclusion as an argument from someone who sells the alternative. His thesis: immediate mode (egui) re-runs all UI code each frame; Elm-style (Iced) re-runs all of view and diffs; GPUI re-renders every view not behind a cache boundary, because its cx.notify() names an entity but not which field changed ("notify is zone.js"). Signals frameworks run only the closures that read the changed value.

The numbers, from his own harness on one machine (i9-13900K, RTX 3080 Ti, Linux): at 3,000 live rows with a cached view per row, changing one label takes 8.4 ms in GPUI; with plain elements, 92 ms. A hand-written equality guard drops an equal write from 7.9 ms to 0.9 µs. Iced builds 39,023 widgets and takes 7.3 ms for the same edit; egui is about 32 ms per small edit; zgui is 0.69 ms. Retaining views (the gpui-fast fork) helps when state lines up with views, saving 83% in one benchmark, and makes a shared-model case 11% slower in another. Zed measured retention "1–3% slower than off on ordinary workloads."

His fair-minded caveats matter as much as the headline: signals aren't free (371 ms creation cost for zgui vs 123 ms for GPUI when filtering 3,000 rows), signal frameworks can still be slow if layout walks the whole tree (Floem spends 0.01 ms on reactivity and ~14 ms on layout), Dan Abramov's objection that run-once code restructures your logic around values is real, and Rust is an awkward fit for signals' arena handles and closures. The benchmark harness is unpublished. The takeaway isn't "use zgui" — it's the question to ask of any UI stack your agents will build on: when one value changes, what runs? If the answer is "everything, again," your agent-built interfaces will feel sluggish at exactly the scale where they should feel instant.

Writing is still the thinking tool

X — @swyx · Nov 9

Murat Demirbas, via swyx: writing is how humans do out-of-core computation — and in the LLM era, doing your own writing is "a comparative advantage, and a widening one."

Demirbas's post, which swyx called "a good read on why humans, like LLMs, get a lot more powerful when given a scratchpad," argues that writing is how humans do out-of-core computation. He cites Manuel Blum: a finite automaton becomes a Turing machine by adding paper and pencil, so "without writing, you are reduced to a finite automaton." The examples are concrete: reviewing a paper shows you understood about 60%; a quick 2018 post on Paxos and Nakamoto consensus became a research paper.

His warning for the LLM era is that a six-page memo no longer proves thinking happened — anyone can generate one — so doing your own writing is "a comparative advantage, and a widening one." His five rules: mess up (write in bulk, no judgment), tidy up (edit like a ruthless reviewer, cut like deleting code), keep it plain, write without expectations, put it on the calendar. For a tech lead whose job is now mostly deciding and communicating, this is the cheapest leverage in the edition: the spec you write yourself is the one you actually understand, and it's the one your agents can't talk you out of.

Don't pick a model from a leaderboard — build your own private evals

X — @HamelHusain · Nov 8

MaxxBench itself ran yesterday. What's new is Hamel Husain's framing: the study should make you skeptical of every public benchmark — "you need to do your own private evals on your specific tasks."

Husain pushed back on people reading MaxxBench as one more leaderboard for picking models: the study should make you skeptical of every public benchmark, and the only way to know what's better on your work is your own private evals on your specific tasks. He also quoted @xeophon's remark that after jamming with him the state of evals looks like the military-industrial complex. Nobody in the captured replies disagreed; the camp is one-sided.

The practical consensus is to start from your own failures: pull 50 real failures from logs, label them by hand, rerun on each prompt or model change, and treat a drop like a failed CI check (@theVishalGhule). Or take 20 failed prompts, perturb them "the MaxxBench way," and rerun at each model upgrade (@balazsfab). For Gabriel's SaaS support-triage capstone, this is the method: the eval is 50 real tickets, not a public benchmark — and it doubles as the regression suite for every prompt change you ship.

AMD's MI355 beats Nvidia's flagship — for one user at a time

X — SemiAnalysis · Nov 9

SemiAnalysis claims AMD's MI355 with TileRT is 1.42x faster than Nvidia's GB300 NVL72 at 1,330 p90 tokens/sec per user on long context. Read the fine print: it's a batch-size-one latency result, not throughput.

The claim (a post): AMD MI355 with TileRT software is 1.42x faster than GB300 NVL72 at 1,330 p90 tok/s/user on long-context workloads, on an MI350 cluster AMD supplied to TileRT's maintainers. What TileRT is, from its blog and an earlier InferenceX piece: an ultra-low-latency decode engine that compiles the whole decode graph into one persistent GPU kernel, to maximize overlap of compute, memory and communication. In September it took the top single-user spot on InferenceX AgentX (GLM-5.3, FP8, 8x MI355X, 469 tok/s on million-token agent sessions, more than 100 tok/s above the best GB300 NVL72 FP4 entry).

The honest caveat is in InferenceX's own write-up: TileRT serves one in-flight request per decode node, so it wins per-user speed but loses on aggregate throughput (160 vs ~240 tok/s per GPU at 8k/1k in one B200 comparison), and the time-to-first-token is "good but not exceptional." So the 1.42x is the right number for one user who wants the fastest long-context agent session — not for cost per token at scale. The digest could not find the page behind the specific 1,330 figure, only TileRT's earlier AgentX and InferenceX write-ups.

The replies were sceptical and technical in the useful way: software is the story ("a 1.42x swing on the same hardware"); will it hold on shorter prompts and other models, and does it replicate outside the AMD-provided cluster; wrong metric — tok/watt/dollar matters; and procurement still asks whether you can get any this quarter. The adjacent data point from the Nvidia debate: @GavinSBaker notes Huawei's Atlas 960e uses a 4,096-chip optical scale-up domain vs NVL72's 72, trading power for scale — and argues keeping China on weaker Nvidia parts discourages the software investment needed to exploit that. Two different kinds of competition are converging here: AMD is attacking Nvidia on software, and China is attacking on scale. Both say the same thing — the moat is no longer just the silicon.

Apple's AI reckoning: Ternus pushes Federighi, Siri to become an agent

Bloomberg — Power On (Mark Gurman) · Nov 8

Apple's new CEO told his software chief to "accelerate" the AI roadmap and move beyond mere Siri improvements. Gurman reports Siri could work "more like an agent" as early as next year — and the man running it may leave.

During his first earnings call last week, Apple CEO John Ternus struck a positive tone, saying the new Siri AI assistant is more privacy-conscious and better integrated than the competition — "the kind of Siri that we always wanted," "a new chapter in AI." Behind the scenes, Gurman reports, Ternus held a closed-door meeting with software chief Craig Federighi — who became Apple's AI czar after last year's dismissal of its former AI leader — and, frustrated by what he sees as a reluctance to embrace AI with the same urgency as competitors, pushed him to accelerate the roadmap and move quickly beyond merely making necessary improvements to Siri.

The situation exposes Apple's organizational problem. Federighi carries a reputation as an AI skeptic, concerned about the technology's privacy, security, societal and employment implications; he plans software over 12–18 months, against the hardware organization's multiyear roadmaps — a mismatch that has complicated product development. He is famously hands-on and combative in debate, which some employees say leaves limited room for competing ideas. And Mike Rockwell — the Vision Pro creator who now leads Siri engineering and brought the new Siri to market after an embarrassing two-year delay — continues to seriously consider stepping down next year, which would be a major blow. Apple also shook up its development strategy for iOS 28, splitting the first milestone into six weeks of coding, exploration and feature development followed by two weeks of convergence.

What Siri can actually do now, and what's coming: today's Siri AI properly answers questions, writes emails and texts, sets reminders, pulls information from the open web, and takes basic actions inside apps — plus the big new capability, drawing context from what's on screen and from private on-device information like emails and texts. The engine was rebuilt from scratch on models built with technology Apple is licensing from Google. The iOS 27.3 beta adds advanced in-app actions — precise control inside Apple's own apps as early as December, third-party app support (OpenTable reservations, intricate Uber Eats orders, moving information between apps) probably in spring 2027. But Gurman's real news is the direction: Siri is still request-based, while ChatGPT's Dots, Meta's Muse and Grok can be handed an ongoing responsibility and keep working between conversations. Apple ultimately wants to go there, and Gurman believes Siri could become more agent-like as early as next year. Apple even showed a taste at WWDC 2026 — software that analyzes saved passwords, identifies weak or compromised ones, then goes website to website changing them — before the feature quietly disappeared from the beta. It remains on Apple's marketing site as coming in the future.

The stakes go beyond whether Siri can compete feature for feature. Apple owns the devices, operating systems and chips — control of the current computing platform, but not necessarily the next one. OpenAI and other AI companies aren't far behind in developing hardware of their own, and they already have much of the agent software figured out. If they combine that software with AI-native devices designed from the ground up around assistants that see, remember and act, Apple's longstanding advantages could begin to matter less.

Ex-Energy Department officials raise $130M to build data centers in a factory

WSJ — Jennifer Hiller · Nov 9

Three former Trump Energy Department officials founded Mavria, a Houston startup building 5-megawatt data centers in a factory and shipping them by truck — a bet that speed-to-power, not chips, is the AI buildout's decisive edge.

Adam Blake, Nick Poradek and Seth Cohen led a nuclear-energy overhaul at the Energy Department and worked with the Department of Government Efficiency. It matters because the AI buildout is now constrained less by chips than by electricity and permitting — the same constraint behind the data-center moratoria in this edition's companion story. Factory-built capacity is a bet that speed-to-power is the decisive edge, and it shows Trump-era energy officials moving directly into the AI infrastructure boom. If Terrain works, the permitting fight moves from the grid queue to the factory floor.
