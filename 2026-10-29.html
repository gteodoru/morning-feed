Gemini 4 Carbon: Google's two-week turnaround to the top — and a model almost nobody can try

Google DeepMind · Oct 28

Two weeks after Argon, Carbon is #1 on Artificial Analysis's new v5 index — but it goes first to a subset of cyber defenders, not to developers.

Sundar Pichai announced Gemini 4 Carbon on Wednesday afternoon: "I did say we'd be releasing our models periodically." Google pitches two things — long-horizon agentic work and cybersecurity — and frames cyber defence as a lifecycle, with a benchmark for each stage: finding flaws (SEC-bench Pro, 88.2%), validating them by exploiting them (ExploitGym, 54.8%), and fixing them at the root cause (CWE-bench, 71.0%). Internally, Google says Carbon runs "for days" through dead ends on multi-step engineering tasks like root-causing subtle bugs across large codebases, and reports a 2x+ uplift over Argon in generating secure code.

The independent scorekeepers, both with pre-release access, tell a more qualified story. Artificial Analysis puts Carbon #1 on its Intelligence Index v5 at 59, top on 4 of 9 evals, and second on its coding-agent index at 59 — only 3 points behind Claude Code with Opus 5.5 at 62, a notable jump for Google, which has "historically underperformed in this area." But AA also notes Carbon's hallucination rate on AA-Omniscience rose from 4% to 11% even as accuracy rose 10 points, and on knowledge work it still trails Grok 4.7, Muse Wonder and Kimi K3.1 Pro. Arena has it #1 in Text Arena, #2 in Agent Arena, and #1 on "Steerability" — can it course-correct when you push back? — at a median $1.80 per agent task, 72% less than Opus 5.5 (Max).

Google's alignment claims, from internal testing, are the part worth reading closely if you build agents: cheating on Terminal-Bench 4.0 fell to 0.0%, high-severity misalignment alerts in live coding dropped almost 80%, unsupported claims roughly halved versus Argon, overreach down 26% and under-service down 29%, and Carbon stopped every attack in Gray Swan's prompt-injection benchmark. Price is $2/$10 per million tokens at launch, rising to $4/$20. Rollout is the catch Nathan Lambert made loudly: Carbon goes first to "a subset of cyber defenders" through Google's Fairwind Program, with US government voluntary pre-release testing underway. "Don't bother announcing your scores if we can't use it," he wrote. For now, treat Carbon as a signal about Google's shipping rhythm — a new frontier model every two weeks — and about where the frontier is being measured, not as a tool you can put in a harness today.

Particle-1: RL-tune an open model to use 45% fewer tokens at the same accuracy

Particle · Oct 28

Suhail Doshi's new lab post-trained DeepSeek V4.1 Flash with staged RL so it stops reasoning after the task is already done.

Particle-1 is DeepSeek V4.1 Flash post-trained to solve software-engineering tasks with fewer tokens, live via the Particle API and OpenRouter. Across Terminal-Bench 2.1, ReactBench, ADE Bench and DeepSWE 1.1, Particle's own evaluations (16 rollouts per task) show 28–52% fewer output tokens and 34–52% lower cost per task than the base model, with pass@1 within the base model's confidence intervals: on average $0.22 per task versus $0.34, and a 72.6% pass rate versus 71.2%. Off-domain scores barely move. No third party has replicated the savings yet — everything here is Particle's own evaluation, and the digest is explicit about that.

The method is the lesson, and Particle documents it unusually well. Environments in Harbor format, generated by its synthetic-data system Datamaker, give the model a task and a way to verify the result. The reward is solve the task and use fewer tokens for reasoning and tool calls. Training is staged — "Cascade RL": stage 1 all coding, which already cut tokens ~20% on data-engineering tasks it had not trained on, then stage 2 data engineering. The stack is Miles (RadixArk's open-source RL framework) with SGLang and Megatron optimisations, a rank-64 LoRA on the experts in all layers — about 1.25% of parameters — and GSPO, which clips whole sequences instead of individual tokens; token-level loss "oscillated and eventually diverged."

Where do the savings come from? Comparing runs where both models passed: less deliberation for the same actions; stopping once the task is verified — in one calendar task ~94% of the savings came after the correct file was already written; fewer trial-and-repair loops; and more focused exploration. Reasoning tokens are ~90% of the savings on three of four benchmarks. And there is a dial: scaling the LoRA's strength on a legal contract-review task cut 24.6% of tokens with all 8 reports passing at 1x, 60.2% with one or two misses at 4x, and 78.7% with only 3 of 8 passing at 8x — "aggressive scaling compresses useful qualifications along with redundant analysis." Aaron Levie's frame for why this matters: agents will consume "1,000X more tokens" than people do today, so efficiency is infrastructure, not a nice-to-have. For Gabriel's platform work, the transferable idea is co-adapting model and harness: verify early, stop early, and reward the shorter verified path.

Vibe-coded games are fun now — Simon Willison's October inflection point

Simon Willison · Oct 28

Claude Opus 5.5 can now do the hard part — game design. Four browser games, none taking more than four hours of his time.

Simon Willison calls November 2025 (Opus 4.5 / GPT-5.1) the moment coding agents became "reliable daily drivers." October 2026, he argues, is the same thing for games. Claude Opus 5.5 improved at 3D modelling, music generation and — crucially — game design. Models could make something that looked like a game for a while; the challenge was making one "that's actually fun to play... I didn't think the models could do it. Then I tried telling Claude Opus 5.5 to 'Make it a LOT more fun'... and it did."

The four games, all HTML/JS in the browser and vibe-coded "in the classic sense of the term — no looking at the code at all": Tree Appreciator, a three.js spot-the-difference game built in one shot in plain Claude chat; Emberwick, a top-down fantasy RPG built up step by step until a prompt to "quadruple the size of the playable area, introduce fun game mechanics, add a plot that isn't too cliche" produced a 15-minute story whose ending moved him; Battle Drones, a Command & Conquer-style RTS with 2–4 player multiplayer, seven maps and a mode where LLMs play each other; and Quarter Sun Cove, a pumpkin-farming game where "surprise and delight the user frequently" produced raccoons, an elephant seal running the weigh-off, and superstitions that sometimes come true. He ran the same prompts through GPT-6 Pro: "It looks great, but I don't think it plays nearly as well," and GPT-6 Astra "isn't quite there yet."

The takeaway generalises beyond games, and the replies saw it: "fun is the requirement nobody can specify. If an agent can iterate toward fun, it can handle the vague product asks that stall real teams... it is ambiguity tolerance." His working method — start in chat for the proof of concept, move to Codex or Claude Code after, tell models to use three.js — is a usable pattern for any ambiguous product prototype. Verification still matters: one studio has a fresh agent that only sees screenshots sign off every visual change, never the builder's reasoning. And he expects a split among players over AI-made games, and will not enter traditional game jams: "I don't feel like I've earned that right."

Agent infrastructure: a runtime that hibernates 75,000 agents, Macs on demand, and why your Claude Code bill spiked

X — @ShadajL, @thorstenball, @bcherny · Oct 28

Infinity treats agents like async tasks, not threads. Meanwhile Amp can spawn real Macs, and Anthropic found no single cause for heavy Claude Code usage.

Infinity, from Shadaj L. and the Hydro project, is the most interesting infrastructure idea of the day: "Infinity does for agents what async did for threads." Agents run slow tools concurrently, yield while they wait, and "cost nothing until the next event arrives." Between turns an agent is "pure data, just its conversation history with no task, no stack, and no open connection," so it hibernates for free — hence "75k agents in the memory of a Raspberry Pi" — and the same code runs on a laptop or AWS Lambda. It ships a Reactive Agent Protocol that delivers tool results "even to agents that currently have no process at all," an MCP compatibility layer, and "Infinity Code," a coding agent that runs builds and tests in the background and edits in parallel sandboxes. The digest read the README only, not the docs or code, so treat this as a design worth studying, not a vetted system.

Around it, the cloud-dev shift keeps accelerating. Amp agents can now spawn Macs ("macOS Satellites") to build, test and run iOS/macOS apps, with self-hosted orbs no longer needing a workspace and a new Android app. Thorsten Ball: "the jump from 'a bit of friction' to 'zero friction' is bigger than from 'a bit of friction' to 'a lot of friction'... You say 'give me irrefutable proof this works' and the agent goes and works for 45min." YC open-sourced QM, its internal Slack agent harness, and E2B can now give each person or channel an isolated sandbox with GitHub CLI, AWS CLI, Claude Code and Codex preinstalled.

And a practical cost note from Boris Cherny at Anthropic: after reviewing every session ID users sent about Claude Code token burn, "We didn't find a single root cause or any regression." Heavy usage traced to "one or two heavy sessions (xhigh effort, /goal, Workflows, Agent Teams, long SDK jobs)." His tip, worth passing on to any team running agents: "Subagents inherit [effort level] by default and it can add up fast. /usage also shows where a session's tokens went." Mitchell Hashimoto's follow-up on his benchmark teardown is a short course in the same theme — terminals are stateful protocols, and a 64 KiB per-frame drop can silently lose lines, split escape sequences, or drop a mode change with "nothing resyncs them short of a full RIS." The harness, again, decides what counts as done.

The benchmarker's dilemma: how to read Artificial Analysis v5 without being fooled by it

Nathan Lambert · Oct 28

"Hill-climbing is easy, knowing what to hill-climb is hard" — and a single index now dictates winners in a trillion-dollar market.

Nathan Lambert's meta-review of Artificial Analysis v5 starts from an uncomfortable premise: an index has to reduce to one number "matching vibe checks, community sentiment, and scientific analysis, while being forward looking enough so that it's not outdated in 3 months," and with this much money involved, "you dictate the winners and losers of a trillion dollar market." V5 weights Knowledge Work 25%, Coding 25%, Science 25% and General 25% — and that weighting choice is itself the story. Change it and the winner changes.

His reading of the open-versus-closed gap is more measured than the headlines: "Open models' demise from this seems greatly exaggerated, but I do think the previous index was flattering the open weight models." Open models are furthest behind on the science evals, but "the value proposition was never to compete truly at the frontier" — it is about driving high-demand inference to commodity hardware prices and letting companies own and specialise intelligence. He expects GLM 6 or Kimi 3.5 "in 2-3 months" to jump on this index "again surprising many observers." Why open models lag: the best ones "benchmax a little," the worst "a lot," they are narrower, and many gains now come from RL environments "with distributions close to the evaluations," which takes a sophisticated RL stack.

On private benchmarks — the direction Carbon's launch leans on — he is directionally supportive but blunt: "you're putting complete trust in the private benchmark builder" and you lose the community finding bugs. His fix is more indices with different biases, and more trust in non-profits, academics and government institutions like CAISI for fairness, even while startups like Artificial Analysis and Arena are the ones he trusts most for accuracy on the frontier. The practical takeaway for a tech lead choosing models: never decide on one index. Read the eval mix, check who had pre-release access, and ask what the index rewards — because the labs are optimising for exactly that.

AI credit anxiety: Alphabet tumbles 10%, Microsoft rises, and the debt funding the buildout strains

Bloomberg · Oct 29

Revenue rose 25% to $127.6B — and the stock still fell, on search decelerating and a second straight quarter of negative free cash flow.

Alphabet reported quarterly sales and profit that fell short of expectations, and the market's verdict was swift: shares fell about 10% after hours and in premarket trading, putting the company on track to lose more than $400 billion in market value if the losses held. Revenue rose 25% to $127.6 billion, but search advertising grew 12%, down from 17% the prior quarter, and missed estimates by about $1.4 billion. Google Cloud more than doubled to $30.7 billion — corporate demand for AI computing is real — but it was not enough. Free cash flow went further into negative territory at $9.1 billion, a far bigger loss than analysts expected, after $51.4 billion of quarterly capital spending. Management maintained a $195–205 billion 2026 spending outlook and said 2027 spending would rise significantly again. "Investors are starting to scrutinize monetization timelines versus the capital expenditure burn rates," said Jeremy Saltzberg of Check Capital Management. "Some investors are no longer giving all hyperscalers a 'free pass' to spend without limit."

Microsoft was the counter-example in the same session, up about 3.5%: surging Azure sales showed AI spending paying off, with a big cash-flow beat and strong cloud guidance. The contrast is the point — the market is now discriminating between AI bets by their cash returns, not their ambition. Software felt it too: ServiceNow sank 18% on its outlook, Fortinet plunged 25%, and Salesforce, Palo Alto Networks and CrowdStrike all fell more than 4%.

Underneath the earnings, Bloomberg's Markets Daily reports a newer anxiety gripping Wall Street as it bankrolls the data centres and chips behind the boom: early signs suggest the credit market is approaching the limits of how much more debt it can absorb. Yields are soaring into double digits on bonds and loans funding the buildout; bankers are finding it harder to offload risk to insurers and other investors. The cost of insuring against losses on the debt of companies from Broadcom to SpaceX and Oracle has spiked to levels more typical of junk-rated borrowers. "At the beginning of 2026, the highest-rated data center bonds offered yields below 5%," Morgan Stanley's Carolyn Campbell said. "Today similar bonds are yielding closer to 7%." Citadel Securities warns even the biggest names could get ensnared as they put balance sheets behind scores of projects. In what could be an AI mega-deal, Marvell is in talks to buy Nvidia-backed data-centre optics maker Lumentum, valued at $83 billion — Marvell fell about 9.5% on the reports, Lumentum rose about 4%.

The Wall Street Journal's Heard on the Street column, published Thursday morning, argues the selloff may overstate the damage. Alphabet, Asa Fitch writes, is tech's closest one-stop AI shop — a frontier model, a fast-growing cloud, in-house chips and a large advertising business — trading at a lower forward earnings multiple than Microsoft, Amazon and Meta. He argues the search threat is overstated: AI search tools have been well received and Gemini has surpassed one billion users, while a slower, business-first rollout of consumer AI agents may prove prudent given security and testing risks. The strain he does not minimise is capital: planned spending of about $200 billion this year, forecast to rise to $312 billion next year, partly offset by $265 billion in cash and marketable securities, with rising depreciation and likely negative free cash flow as drags. That is the same question the credit market is asking, in a different form: breadth buys time, but only cash flow buys the buildout.

Seed VCs say pro rata is not up for negotiation

This Week in Startups · Oct 28

Lessin, Doherty and Fielding side with Day One Ventures in the Valar Atomics lawsuit — founders on X mostly took the startup's side.

When Valar Atomics sued its seed investor, Day One Ventures, over pro rata rights, many founders and even investors on X took the startup's side. On this week's TWiST VC roundtable, all three guest investors made the case for pro rata. Sam Lessin of Slow Ventures: the entire seed investment business model "doesn't work without pro rata rights" — when a founder asks him to give his up after a round or two, his response is always the same: "You'll be hearing from my lawyers." Jenny Fielding of Everywhere Ventures questions why an agreed-to stipulation in a contract should be any easier to "take back" than shares themselves. Day One's account, from founder Masha Bucher, does not line up with Taylor's — the panel does not adjudicate that, it defends the principle. The practical lesson for founders is unglamorous and worth hearing before the term sheet, not after: pro rata is not a favour the seed investor owes you; it is the mechanism that lets a small fund stay in a winner, and trying to unwind it by lawsuit is how a cap-table dispute becomes a reputation dispute.
