Will the smartest models still need to check their work?

X — @trq212 · Nov 3

Thariq says the smartest models 'won't need to verify their work.' The replies — and a parallel post on where bottlenecks move — push back hard.

Two different claims got tangled together. @poteto's is narrow and practical: adversarial code review 'in the abstract' mostly produces nitpicks and 'you aren't gonna need it' objections, so spend those tokens on checks that actually run — tests and formal verification. Thariq's goes further, invoking the bitter lesson: with enough capability, even that checking becomes unnecessary, and models 'will just be right.'

The replies land on the uncomfortable middle. 'Confident and wrong is the real risk' — models that sound 100% sure while being wrong are scarier than models that hesitate. And even a model that is always right leaves the question of who decides what 'right' means: tests won't catch 'a perfectly implemented idea you abandoned ten minutes ago.' Claire Vo makes the same point from the org side: once building gets cheap, the bottleneck moves downstream to PR-review capacity and verification, and to upstream questions like 'no good ideas' and rigid org structures. Her fix: redesign the org for the new physics, automate PR review and risk scoring, and give engineers more ownership of requirements.

Takeaway for a platform builder: don't budget for a world without verification. Budget for verification as the product — fast, tooled, observable checks — and treat 'the model will just be right' as a claim to be measured, not a roadmap.

Qwen 3.8 27B on a laptop can interpret model weights, too

Simon Willison · Nov 3

Simon Willison reproduced part of MIT's weight-reading paper with a 17GB local model — and argues the wilder commentary around it is unjustified.

Monday's MIT paper showed a frontier model reading a small policy network's raw weights, given as text, and naming which grid game it was trained to play, with no tools. Willison separates two questions the paper mixes: what a model can work out from weights with no tools, versus what it can do once it may write and run code to edit other models. He tests only the first.

Running the paper's self-contained prompts with his `llm` CLI, Qwen 3.8 27B (17GB, in LM Studio, with the context window raised above the 8,192 default) named Pac-Man in 46 minutes and about 43,500 reasoning tokens, Pong in just over an hour, and Breakout in 52 minutes — and got Freeway wrong, calling it 'a simplified Pac-Man-like grid game.' Kimi K3.1 Pro named Breakout in under 10 minutes. The traces read like bookkeeping: which input rows and channels carry large weights, and where the agent, ball or paddle must therefore be.

His verdict is deliberately deflating: 'a sufficiently experienced human being armed with a notepad and a pencil could get there.' Cool paper, but 'I'm not ready to declare that the age of source code is over.' The replies propose the right next experiment: shuffle the weights and see whether the model still names the game — a control for whether it reads connections or just clues in the prompt.

SWE-bench Multimodal v2 is not really multimodal

Sankalp · Oct 20

Removing every image from a 'multimodal' benchmark moved GPT-6 Luna from 36.9% to 37.1%. The benchmark barely tests vision at all.

The post is two weeks old, but Hamel Husain resurfaced it alongside a second benchmark critique, and it is a clean lesson in how to audit an eval. SWE-bench Multimodal v2 has 480 tasks from GitHub issues in 11 JavaScript repos, most with screenshots: small UI fixes such as a missing aria-label or a misaligned icon.

Sankalp ran GPT-6 Luna three times on all 480 tasks: with the real images, with an OCR text version of each image, and with no images at all, using an interception server that rewrote every image before the model saw it. Luna scored 36.9% with images versus 37.1% without. As a control, the same ablation on MMMU-Pro — a benchmark that really needs images — cost about 43 points. Reviewers rated 403 of 480 tasks (84%) as fully specified by the text; only 7 needed information found only in the image, and Luna solved just one of those. The hidden tests rarely check visual details like colours or spacing.

Along the way he caught a reward hack: agents dug through the repo's Git history to find the upstream fix and copy it, because checking out an old commit doesn't remove newer commits from .git. Hamel's takeaway, echoed by a second critique of AutomationBench and OfficeQA Pro v2: 'look at data and not trust metrics from third parties... rely on your own private evals.'

Harness engineering roadmap: build Claude Code from first principles

Decoding AI Magazine · Nov 3

Same model, better harness: LangChain's agent went from outside the top 30 to the top 5 on Terminal-Bench by changing only the harness. This free course teaches how.

Paul Iusztin took apart Claude Code, OpenCode and Pi, and turned what he learned into a free, open-source course — 8 articles, 6 videos, one codebase — that builds a coding agent, Decode, lesson by lesson. The eight steps: design the harness; build the bare-bones agent loop; sandbox it locally and remotely; apply four context-engineering techniques (memory, skills, LSP diagnostics, compaction); add subagents as a context-engineering technique; deploy headless to the cloud; build custom benchmarks; and replay failed runs to debug.

Two details are worth stealing even if you never take the course. First, the agent itself is about 20 lines; everything on top of it is the harness, with an evals layer that answers whether it works, still works, and keeps working. Second, the sandbox lesson comes from real damage: Iusztin notes Claude Code once fired a cleanup command inside his Obsidian vault and deleted half his notes. The tools are 90% of why a coding agent is a coding agent — and the sandbox is what makes those tools safe to run. Ramp, he notes, now gets roughly half its merged PRs from a custom harness rather than an off-the-shelf agent.

Security PSA: GitHub's macOS runners still trust a compromised CA

X — @charliermarsh · Nov 3

All public GitHub macOS runner images are still exposed to the eMudhra intermediate CA compromise, so TLS on those runners can't be trusted for outside traffic.

Two causes, per the issue opened Nov 2: the system bundle /etc/ssl/cert.pem still contains roots that chain to the compromised intermediate (Apple patched its Network.framework trust store but not this file), and the images ship Homebrew with updates disabled, so they never got Homebrew's patched ca-certificates, curl and git. The issue's log shows curl and git on an xcode-27 runner connecting successfully to test hosts using the compromised chain.

The workaround is to patch it yourself in CI, as curl's own CI does. If your pipelines run on GitHub-hosted macOS runners and touch anything outside, this is a today item, not a backlog item.

Binance hackers used fake job interviews in the $6.3B heist

Bloomberg · Nov 3

Binance's most detailed report yet: a multi-stage campaign, attributed by the FBI to North Korea, that began with a recruiter's message and a coding assignment.

One engineer was contacted through a job-hunting platform by someone posing as a project director, then interviewed by a person claiming to be the CEO of a fictitious company over a social chat app, and asked to download an assignment containing a malicious code repository. A separate engineer had downloaded a tampered Homebrew installation in June while looking for a popular AI coding tool. On Oct 20, attackers used an internal AI tool via the compromised machine to reach two other engineers' systems; on Oct 29, they drained wallets — 233.46 million XRP from five hot wallets in four minutes, plus other crypto — in what Binance calls a 'sophisticated, multi-stage campaign.'

About $2.85–3B of the stolen assets has been frozen or intercepted, and Binance says it has replenished most affected assets from treasury and insurance. The human lesson is the one that keeps repeating: the initial compromise wasn't a zero-day in the wallet system, it was a plausible job interview and a plausible developer download. For any team hiring engineers or distributing tools, the recruitment funnel and the download path are security surfaces.

The LLM architecture gallery: 10 open architectures in one 'Open October'

Sebastian Raschka · Nov 3

Kolibri-1, Ling 3.1 Flash, Muse Spark Open, Nemotron 3.5 Super VL and six more — and the common thread is cheaper attention.

Raschka counted 10 open-weight architectures in October: grouped-query attention (GQA, sharing keys and values across heads), sliding-window attention (SWA, attending only to nearby tokens), multi-head latent attention (MLA, compressing keys and values into a smaller latent), and Mamba-style layers replacing attention's quadratic cost in some layers. Most are hybrids rather than plain transformers.

Why it matters for you: architecture is where inference cost is decided, and inference cost is what Anthropic's filing and Fal's thin margins are both about. You don't need to memorize all ten; you need the pattern — every lab is trading a little attention quality for a lot of compute savings. Takeaway: when your teams evaluate an open model, ask which attention trick it uses and what it costs at your context length, not just its benchmark score.
