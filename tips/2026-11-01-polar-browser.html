Polar: the AI browser that does knowledge work inside your logged-in accounts

Added Nov 1 · original article

Polar is the second-wave AI browser: not a search chatbot, but an agent that does knowledge work inside the accounts you are already logged into — sales, recruiting, ops, research — on a schedule, for hours at a time. Launched July 29, 2026 with a $5.7M Madrona-led seed, it is also the sharpest case yet of the logged-in-agent privacy problem: cookies stay local, but page context, screenshots, and prompts leave the device.

What it is

Polar (polarbrowser.com, from Recursive Intelligence) is a macOS-only, Chromium-based AI browser built for knowledge-work automation rather than general browsing. Founder and CEO Kevin Jiang previously worked on Perplexity's Comet browser, with co-founders Vishaal Ram and Howard Zhong; the company announced itself July 29, 2026 with a $5.7M seed round led by Madrona, with participation from ex-GitHub CEO Thomas Dohmke, Phia founder Phoebe Gates, Modal CEO Erik Bernhardsson, and others. It replaced the team's earlier Composer agent in May 2026.

The product pitch is 'you type, Polar does': agents click, type, and navigate inside websites where you are already signed in — no per-service connectors to build. You hand off tasks from any tab (the current page becomes context), save prompts as reusable workflows, and schedule them hourly, daily, or weekly with the same context carried into every run. The company says agents can work autonomously for many hours: 4.5 million web actions initiated during seven months of testing, some tasks running 15+ hours — figures that have not been independently verified. Target work: research, recruiting, sales, ops, running a business. Pricing is freemium with limited daily AI credits; paid plans start at $20/month. The site's testimonials all come from ops, sales, and hiring users, and the company itself says most users treat Polar as an automation layer while keeping another browser as their daily driver.

What people are saying

The launch coverage agrees on the thesis, and Jiang stated it plainly to TechCrunch: the first wave of AI browsers tried to change default search and chat with tabs — booking travel, answering questions — and that never created enough recurring demand. The second wave is agents doing work: Polar joins Perplexity's Comet (now agent-oriented), The Browser Company's Dia, Fellou, OpenAI's Atlas, and agent-first startups like Browser Use, Strawberry, and Aside. Digital Trends' verdict is the skeptical version of the same point: Polar still has to prove professionals will adopt a second browser dedicated to automation.

The sharper reaction is the privacy critique. Researcher @iAnonymous3000 (post of Aug 27, 2026, covered by PiunikaWeb Aug 31) warned against treating Polar as a primary browser after reading its policies, terms, and roadmap: cookies and passwords stay local, but the agent sends page context, screenshots, recent pages, prompts, and files to Polar and its model providers; retention of conversations, runs, screenshots, memory, and analytics has no stated maximum; there is no self-service account deletion; and the roadmap's 'users-and-data flywheel' sits uneasily next to the absence of a product-wide promise not to train on customer data. TechRepublic's security analysis adds the company's side — provider agreements prohibit training on customer data with zero data retention subject to limited exceptions — plus the gaps: detailed documentation of the agent's guardrails remains limited, SOC 2 compliance is still in progress, and the team had not publicly answered the thread at the time of the PiunikaWeb piece. TechRepublic's pre-deployment checklist names the two risks to test before any team rollout: indirect prompt injection (hostile instructions hidden in pages, emails, or documents — the BioShocking demos) and approval controls for high-risk actions, citing the Claude for Chrome flaw where a rogue extension could trigger Gmail/Docs/Calendar tasks.

What to take away

For the platform work, Polar is interesting for the opposite reason Lightpanda and ARTEMIS were: instead of giving agents a cheap headless browser or hands on a phone, it gives them hands inside already-authenticated SaaS — the integration problem every agent platform hits. No connectors, no OAuth dance per service: the browser session is the integration. That is a genuinely powerful pattern, and it is also exactly the threat model a platform team has to answer before blessing it: a logged-in session plus multi-hour autonomy plus pages that can carry hostile instructions, with retention and training terms that are not yet crisp.

Practical stance: try it for personal, low-stakes workflows (research summaries, outreach drafts, recurring checks) where the blast radius is small; do not connect it to production accounts, CRMs with customer data, or anything financial until SOC 2 lands and the retention/training answers get written down. The positioning lesson is portable too: even Polar's own users keep a daily driver and open Polar only for automation — the winning form factor for agent browsers may be a tool you invoke, not a browser you live in.
